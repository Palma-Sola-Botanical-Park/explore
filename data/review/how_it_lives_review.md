## Antlions and Owlflies (PSBP-90005)  — source 200w → rewrite 169w

**SOURCE**

> behavior: Larvae spend nearly their entire lives underground, moving only backward and never above the surface except when relocating a pit site. Pit-building begins with the larva crawling backward in a spiral, using its abdomen as a plow and its head to flick sand clear; the finished pit sits precisely at the critical angle of repose so that the walls give way at the slightest disturbance. Adult antlions are weak, erratic fliers active mainly after dark and readily drawn to lights; during the day they rest motionless on dry grass stems or bark, relying on camouflage. Owlflies are strong, agile fliers that hunt on the wing at dusk and dawn, snatching prey in flight much as dragonflies do; by day they perch on vertical stems with the abdomen raised, mimicking a broken twig.

> diet: Larvae are sit-and-wait predators that capture ants, small beetles, spiders, and other ground-dwelling invertebrates that fall into their pits. They pierce prey with hollow jaws, inject venom to paralyze and liquefy internal tissues, then drink the contents. Adult antlions feed on nectar, pollen, and in some species small soft-bodied insects such as aphids and caterpillars; adult owlflies are active aerial hunters of moths, gnats, and other flying insects.

**REWRITE**

> The larva builds the pit and lives at the bottom of it, moving only backward and almost never coming above the surface. It digs by crawling in a spiral, plowing with its abdomen and flicking sand clear with its head, finishing with the walls at exactly the angle where they give way under anything that steps on them. Ants slide in, along with small beetles, spiders and whatever else walks the edge. The larva pierces them with hollow jaws, injects venom that liquefies the insides, and drinks it.

> Two different adults — Antlions are weak, erratic fliers that come to lights at night and spend the day motionless on a dry grass stem or a strip of bark, relying on camouflage. They feed on nectar and pollen, and some take small soft-bodied insects like aphids. Owlflies hunt on the wing at dusk and dawn much as dragonflies do, taking moths and gnats out of the air, then perch by day with the abdomen cocked up, doing a convincing impression of a broken twig.

## Evening Bat (PSBP-90007)  — source 195w → rewrite 120w

**SOURCE**

> behavior: Nocturnal and colonial. Evening bats emerge soon after dusk — sometimes while it is still light — and show two foraging peaks: one in early evening and another just before dawn. Flight is slow and steady, well suited to picking insects out of the air in cluttered edge habitats. Nursery colonies are female-dominated groups during the breeding season; adult males roost separately. Females recognize their own pups within the roost by scent and by voice. As weaning nears, some females will nurse pups other than their own — a behavior called allonursing, documented by Wilkinson (1992). Male offspring leave the natal colony at roughly six weeks old; females remain for life, giving nursery colonies a core of related individuals across generations. In fall, individuals from the northern part of the range migrate south; Florida populations shift little if at all.

> diet: Strictly insectivorous. Evening bats forage on a wide variety of small night-flying insects, with beetles as a preferred prey; moths, flies, mosquitoes, flying ants, planthoppers, spittlebugs, and June beetles are also regularly taken. Prey is caught on the wing during slow, maneuverable flight, typically over water or along forest edges where insect concentrations are highest.

**REWRITE**

> It comes out early, sometimes before the light has fully gone, and hunts in two bursts — one soon after dusk and another before dawn. The flight is slow and maneuverable, which suits picking insects out of cluttered air along a wood edge or over water.

> Beetles are the preference, though moths, flies, mosquitoes, flying ants and June beetles all get taken on the wing.

> A colony of relatives — Nursery colonies are female. A mother picks her own pup out of a crowded roost by scent and by voice, and as weaning nears some will nurse pups that are not theirs. Young males leave at about six weeks; the females stay for life, so a colony is built around generations of the same family.

## Common Raccoon (PSBP-90015)  — source 195w → rewrite 121w

**SOURCE**

> behavior: Primarily nocturnal but also active at dawn and dusk; in Florida's mild winters there is no dormancy, and some foraging occurs on warm afternoons year-round. Raccoons are mostly solitary outside of the breeding season, though females with young-of-year form tight family groups through autumn. Home ranges of about one to three square miles are maintained; males tend to range more widely than females. Raccoons are agile climbers and capable swimmers, and regularly travel between wetland habitats. Dens are opportunistic — tree cavities, hollow logs, root tangles, and human structures all serve. Individuals are inquisitive and quick to investigate novel objects with their sensitive forepaws.

> diet: A true omnivore with one of the broadest diets of any Florida mammal. In and near water — including coastal mangroves, tidal marshes, and the intertidal habitats of Palma Sola Bay — raccoons forage for fiddler crabs, blue crabs, oysters, clams, mussels, frogs, and small fish. On land they eat acorns, wild fruits, berries, insects, bird eggs, small rodents, and carrion. Florida-specific plant foods noted in the literature include sea grapes, beautyberry, cabbage palm fruit, saw palmetto berries, and persimmons. Near people, raccoons exploit pet food, unsecured garbage, and compost readily.

**REWRITE**

> Mostly a night animal, though Florida's mild winters mean no dormancy and you may well see one out on a warm afternoon. Adults are solitary apart from females with that year's young, who stay together into autumn.

> The diet is as broad as any mammal's here. Around the water — including the mangroves and tidal flats of Palma Sola Bay — it is fiddler crabs, blue crabs, oysters, clams, frogs and small fish. On land it is acorns, sea grapes, beautyberry, cabbage palm fruit, saw palmetto berries, insects, eggs and carrion. Near houses it is pet food and whatever the garbage gives up.

> A good climber and a strong swimmer, and endlessly curious about anything it can get its forepaws around.

## Bobolink (PSBP-99905)  — source 187w → rewrite 105w

**SOURCE**

> behavior: Bobolinks are sociable outside the breeding season, moving and foraging in flocks that can number in the hundreds during migration. Migrants typically fly at night and refuel in fields, wet meadows, and marsh edges by day. In spring, males perform a conspicuous display flight — hovering low over territory in a slow, stiff-winged, helicopter-like flutter while singing continuously. The species is polygynous: one male may hold a territory encompassing more than one nest. Females build well-hidden ground nests in dense grasses and do all the incubating, though both parents feed nestlings. After fledging, family groups join together into pre-migration flocks.

> diet: Diet shifts with the season. During breeding, the majority of food is insects — beetles, grasshoppers, caterpillars, wasps, ants, and spiders — which are also fed almost exclusively to nestlings. Seeds of grasses, weeds, and wild grains make up the rest of the summer diet. During migration and winter the balance reverses: seeds and grains dominate, with rice, oats, corn, and weed seeds the main items and insects taken only occasionally. Migrants sometimes feed after dark on bright nights to build fat reserves before long over-water flights.

**REWRITE**

> Bobolinks travel in company. Outside the breeding season they move in flocks that can run to hundreds, flying at night and dropping into fields and marsh edges by day to refuel.

> What they eat flips with the season. Through breeding it is mostly insects — beetles, grasshoppers, caterpillars, spiders — and nestlings get almost nothing else. On migration and through winter it reverses to seeds and grain.

> The spring display — Males hover low over the territory in a slow, stiff-winged flutter, singing the whole time. One male may hold ground covering more than one nest; the females build hidden nests in dense grass and do all the incubating.

## Common Crane Fly (PSBP-99889)  — source 182w → rewrite 105w

**SOURCE**

> behavior: Adults are slow, loose-limbed fliers with a characteristic bobbing flight style that makes them easy to track by eye. They are drawn to lights after dark and commonly rest on walls, screens, and vegetation near moist areas. Mating can be preceded by low swarms of males hovering just above grassy or herbaceous vegetation. Females emerge from the pupal case with eggs already mature and mate quickly — often within hours. Eggs are deposited in moist soil, pond or stream margins, or saturated organic debris. Adults seldom move far from where they emerged.

> diet: The larval stage is the only true feeding phase. Larvae consume decaying plant material, leaf litter, fungi, and organic matter in moist soil and stream-margin debris, aided by enzymes capable of breaking down cellulose, lignin, and other tough plant compounds. In more aquatic situations they also take fine detritus and algae. Adults have greatly reduced, non-functional mouthparts and take no solid food; their brief lifespan is fueled entirely by fat reserves banked during the larval months, though they may occasionally sip water or small amounts of liquid to stay hydrated.

**REWRITE**

> The adult you see does not eat. Its mouthparts are reduced to nothing useful, and the whole brief adult life runs on fat banked during the larval months.

> That larval stage is the real animal. It lives in wet soil, stream margins and saturated leaf litter, breaking down decaying plant material with enzymes that handle cellulose and lignin — the tough material most things cannot digest.

> Adults are slow, loose-limbed fliers with a bobbing flight that is easy to follow by eye. They come to lights after dark and rest on walls and screens near damp ground. They rarely travel far from where they emerged.

## Southern Paper Wasp (PSBP-99893)  — source 182w → rewrite 112w

**SOURCE**

> behavior: Colonies are small by social-insect standards — a few dozen to roughly a hundred adults at peak. A single foundress (or a small group of co-foundresses) initiates the nest in spring; once the first workers emerge, they take over foraging, building, and defense while the dominant female focuses on egg-laying. Workers communicate through pheromones, especially alarm signals near the nest. When a nest is destroyed by a predator, the foundress sometimes rebuilds at a new site — satellite nests up to roughly 35 feet from the original have been documented, with such relocations accounting for a substantial share of all nests built in early summer. Wasps are most active and most defensive during the mid-summer peak when the colony is largest.

> diet: Adults fuel their own flight on nectar and other sugary plant secretions. For the larvae, workers hunt soft-bodied insects — caterpillars above all, plus flies and beetle larvae — sting or paralyze them, chew them into a protein paste, and deliver it directly to the cells. UF/IFAS notes that Florida paper wasps specifically target corn earworms, armyworms, and similar garden pests.

**REWRITE**

> Colonies are small — a few dozen to about a hundred adults at their summer peak. One female starts the nest in spring; once her first workers emerge they take over the building, foraging and defense while she concentrates on laying.

> Adults run on nectar. The larvae get meat: workers hunt caterpillars above all, plus flies and beetle grubs, chew them into a paste and deliver it cell by cell. UF/IFAS notes that Florida paper wasps go after corn earworms and armyworms in particular, which makes them worth having near a garden.

> If a predator wrecks a nest, the founder sometimes rebuilds up to about thirty-five feet away rather than giving up.

## Melon Aphid (PSBP-90028)  — source 179w → rewrite 154w

**SOURCE**

> behavior: Colonies feed gregariously on the undersides of leaves and on new shoot growth, typically remaining stationary as long as the host plant is suitable. Reproduction is continuous and parthenogenetic in Florida — live female nymphs are born without mating, and a nymph can itself begin reproducing within about a week. When colonies become overcrowded or the host deteriorates, winged females are produced and disperse to colonize new plants. Aphids release alarm pheromones when disturbed, prompting nearby colony members to drop or scatter. Many colonies are tended by ants, which collect honeydew and drive off natural enemies; the aphids in turn respond to ant-trail pheromones by reducing dispersal behavior.

> diet: Plant sap, specifically phloem fluid drawn through piercing-sucking mouthparts inserted into leaves and stems. The aphid extracts carbohydrates and amino acids from the plant and excretes the excess sugars as honeydew. Host plants span an exceptionally wide range — at least 60 species in Florida — including key garden families: Cucurbitaceae (melons, gourds, cucumbers), Malvaceae (hibiscus, okra), Rutaceae (citrus), and Solanaceae (peppers, eggplant), as well as many ornamental and weedy species.

**REWRITE**

> They barely move. A colony settles on the underside of a leaf or a new shoot and stays as long as the plant holds out, drinking sap through mouthparts fine enough to slip between plant cells. The surplus sugar comes out the other end as honeydew.

> What it feeds on — At least sixty plants in Florida, and the list reads like a garden: melons, cucumbers and gourds; hibiscus and okra; citrus; peppers and eggplant. If you grow it, an aphid will probably try it.

> Females are born already pregnant and can be producing young of their own inside a week, with no males involved. When a leaf gets crowded or the plant starts to fail, winged females appear and leave to start somewhere else.

> Farmed by ants — Ants collect the honeydew and drive off anything that might eat the aphids. Disturb a colony and it scatters — they release an alarm scent that tells the others to drop off the leaf.

## Western Honey Bee (PSBP-90002)  — source 171w → rewrite 117w

**SOURCE**

> behavior: Colonies are eusocial and highly organized, with labor divided among the queen (egg-laying), workers (foraging, nursing, building, and defense), and drones (mating). Workers shift roles as they age: early weeks are spent on in-hive duties including cell cleaning, larval feeding, wax production, and guard duty; foraging begins only in the final weeks of a worker's life. Colonies reproduce by swarming — the old queen leaves with roughly half the workers to found a new colony while daughter queens compete for the original hive. Pheromones govern colony cohesion; the queen's mandibular pheromone alone suppresses worker reproduction and signals her presence throughout the nest.

> diet: Workers collect nectar and pollen from flowering plants, converting nectar into honey for long-term colony energy storage and using pollen as the colony's protein source for raising larvae. Individual foragers may visit hundreds of flowers on a single trip. Colonies also gather propolis — a sticky resin from plant buds — used to seal gaps and varnish the hive interior, and collect water for cooling and diluting stored honey.

**REWRITE**

> A colony runs on division of labor, and the division shifts with age. A worker spends her first weeks inside — cleaning cells, feeding larvae, making wax, standing guard — and only takes up foraging in her last weeks. The queen lays. The drones exist to mate.

> Foragers bring back nectar and pollen: nectar becomes honey, the colony's stored energy, and pollen feeds the larvae. One forager may visit hundreds of flowers in a trip. They also collect resin from plant buds to seal gaps in the hive, and water to cool it.

> How a colony multiplies — By splitting. The old queen leaves with about half the workers to start somewhere new, and the daughters left behind compete for what remains.

## Great Egret (PSBP-99995)  — source 68w → rewrite 94w

**SOURCE**

> behavior: A patient, visual hunter. It wades slowly or stands frozen in the shallows, watching, then strikes in an instant. Active by day, it nests in colonies, often mixed with other wading birds, and flies with slow, deep wingbeats and its neck folded into an S.

**REWRITE**

> It hunts by standing still. Mostly fish, but it takes frogs, crayfish, insects and the occasional small mammal with the same jab of that dagger bill.

> In the air — Slow, deep wingbeats, and the neck folded back into an S rather than held out ahead. It nests in colonies, often mixed in with other wading birds.
