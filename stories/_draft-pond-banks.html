Protecting our pond banks

Three ponds, one bad night of salt water, and twelve hundred plants to put the edges back.
October 2026

Our three ponds are not really ponds. The Front Pond, the Big Pond and Cypress Pond all connect to Palma Sola Bay by pipe, so the water in them is bay water, and whatever runs off our banks ends up in the bay. That is why two estuary programs, Tampa Bay's and then Sarasota Bay's, have helped pay to put plants back on those banks. Protecting our ponds is protecting the bay.

What the storms did

In September 2024 Hurricane Helene passed offshore and pushed the bay over its banks and into the park. The surge came and went in a night. The damage took a season to show.

Salt water does not look like damage until the following spring. The plants that had held the pond edges browned from the roots up, the banks they had held began to slump, and every rain after that carried a little more soil into the water. A bare bank gets worse on its own: the water clouds, the edge creeps back toward the path, and the palms planted along the Big Pond walkway start to lean toward the water.

The water is still brackish. We had the water and the soil at each pond sampled in January 2026, sixteen months after the storm. The salt is still there, and the saltiest soil is not at the water but up the bank, where the surge stood and dried.

What the plants add

Rock stops waves. It does not filter anything. What a healthy pond edge does, and what ours had stopped doing, is slow the runoff down, catch the sediment and the nutrients in it, and send cleaner water on to the bay. The roots hold the bank; the stems take the hit from the rain; the whole tangle is habitat for the dragonflies, wading birds and small fish that the bigger fish and birds depend on.

So the answer is plants. Florida natives that can take salt wind, salty soil, and the occasional flood, chosen with help from the county's extension office and planted in three rows that match the three conditions on a bank. The first slope and top-of-bank rows are in. The rest go in this fall and next spring.

The three rows

At the water's edge: the bulrushes, the plants that can stand a wet foot.

On the slope: sea ox-eye daisy, scorpion tail and the red mallow, where the ground is damp but rarely under water, and Christmas berry on the upper slope, where it never gets its feet wet.

On top of the bank: muhly grass, dune sunflower, sunshine mimosa, beach bean and railroad vine, the dry-ground spreaders whose roots knit the top of the slope together. Scorpion tail goes up here too: it shrugs off salt wind but will not stand in salt water.

Not everything on the list made it. Sea purslane, a beach succulent that should have loved a salty bank, went in on every pond at the edge and on the slope, and died on every pond. It is off the list.

Two grants, one job

The Tampa Bay Estuary Program came first, in November 2025. Its grant paid for the first round of planting, and the students have been putting it in on their Wednesday afternoons since February 2026: the rows of coontie that ring the Big Pond now, and the first slope and top-of-bank rows on the pond banks.

Six months later the Sarasota Bay Estuary Program's Bay Partners grant took the same job and made it much bigger: roughly twelve hundred plants of about a dozen species along about a thousand feet of bank on all three ponds, in two planting seasons. That work starts this October and runs through spring 2027. Every plant gets tracked: species, spot and condition, checked monthly, so that by the end we know which plants survive which row on which pond, and the spring planting changes to match what the winter shows. That record is the part other people can use. There are thousands of private ponds in Manatee and Sarasota counties with the same bare edges.

The people doing the work are the ones who always do it: ten or so adult volunteers, the park's director, who is a licensed landscape architect, and ten to thirty-five high-school students a week. The students are not just labor. They learn to read a bank, pick the plant for the row, and put it in so it stays.

The high school kids and I installed all the rip rap ourselves to keep the storms from knocking the palms into the ponds. It was a threat.

Randy Carter 2026

What comes next

Signs at each pond, by summer 2027, saying what was planted and why. A walking tour of all three ponds when the planting is done, for anyone with a pond edge of their own. And six workdays a year on the banks after the grant ends, because a planting is a beginning, not a finish.

Next time you walk the Big Pond, look at the bank below the walkway: the rock at the waterline, the rows above it, and whatever has seeded itself in between. That is the project, and it is growing.
