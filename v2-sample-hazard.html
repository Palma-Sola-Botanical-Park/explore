Little Blue Heron

Egretta caerulea
On this page

At a glance

  • A small, dark-blue heron stalking the shallows — quiet, solitary, and easy to overlook next to the showier egrets.
  • Young birds are entirely white, and often mistaken for snowy egrets — the blue comes in gradually during their first year, creating a patchy, piebald look.
  • The white-to-blue transition may be a survival strategy: white juveniles are tolerated by snowy egret flocks, gaining safety in numbers until they can fend for themselves.
  • It is a State Threatened species in Florida — its numbers have declined with wetland loss.

How to know it

Adult

Entirely dark slate-blue with a purplish head and neck. Dark-tipped bill.

Juvenile

Entirely white — easily confused with a snowy egret.

Molting immature

Patchy blue-and-white, a unique calico look.

Bill

Gray with a dark tip — not the yellow bill of an egret.

Voice

Generally silent away from the nest.

Size

22 to 29 inches; about 3.3 feet (40 inches)

What to look for

A small, dark-blue heron hunting alone in the shallows. If you see a white heron with a gray-tipped bill instead of yellow, it may be a juvenile little blue.

Where to find it here

Pond edges and shallows, often alone. Look for the dark blue adult or the white juvenile with a gray-tipped bill.

Year-round resident.

How it lives

Patient, slow stalker. Hunts alone more often than in flocks. Quiet and unobtrusive.

Small fish, frogs, crayfish, and aquatic insects — stalks slowly in shallow water.

What it does here

Wading bird predator of small aquatic animals; an indicator of wetland health.

Forages in wetland vegetation; nests in trees near water.

Native to the southeastern United States, Caribbean, and Central and South America. Year-round resident in Florida.

The little blue heron is one of the quieter wading birds — it lacks the dramatic plumes of the great egret and the pink flash of the spoonbill, but it is a skilled, patient hunter of small fish, frogs, and crustaceans.

The juvenile plumage trick is remarkable: young little blue herons are entirely white, looking almost exactly like snowy egrets. Scientists believe this lets them join snowy egret flocks, where more eyes mean more safety from predators. As they mature, blue feathers gradually replace white, creating a mottled, calico look before they settle into full adult blue.

It is listed as State Threatened in Florida, part of a broader decline in wading bird populations linked to wetland loss and degraded water quality.

Take care

Conservation

State Threatened in Florida. Numbers have declined due to wetland loss and water-quality degradation.
