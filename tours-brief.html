Park Tours

A working brief · September 2026

Nine tours. Three exist as beta drafts, three as name-and-blurb stubs, three are new here. Almost nothing here needs research: 232 plant pages and 91 animal pages are finished, each carrying a one-line story written for somebody standing in front of the plant in the sun. What is missing is a voice and a route — somebody to choose the order, write the joins, and give each tour a personality.

⚠ This is a snapshot of a moving train

**Everything counted in this document was true on 1 September 2026 and will be out of date by the time you use it.** The park is still working through its own collection — finding plants, identifying them, photographing them, marking them on the map and writing them up. **This is not a finished inventory. It is a snapshot of an ongoing job, and the collection described here is the smallest it will ever be.**

That backlog is the point. Over five hundred plants are known to be here and simply have not been written up yet. Palms and rare fruit are where the most is still coming in — seventy-seven palms and cycads are waiting to be written up, and the rare fruit grove gains new plants regularly. Both feed tours 4 and 5 directly.

So check with us before you finalise a tour

  • The website is always the current version. If this document and the site disagree, the site is right.
  • Ask before you print. A quick word will surface what has been added, what has moved and what has died since — none of which a document can know.
  • Ask about the map especially. Plants get their locations pinned week by week, and a plant with no location when this was written may have a precise one now, which changes what routes are possible.

None of this is a reason to wait. It is a reason to treat the tables below as a starting shortlist rather than a closed set, and to ask "what else has turned up?" before committing a route to print.

Why the park should have tours at all

A botanical park is a collection that looks like a garden. A visitor walks in, sees that it is pretty, walks a loop and leaves — having missed a palm with fewer than fifty survivors in the wild, a plant Nabisco once ground into animal crackers, and a tree whose timber bounced British cannonballs. The plants do not announce themselves. **A tour is the difference between a nice walk and a collection.**

  • It gives a volunteer something to do that is not weeding. Somebody who can lead a forty-minute walk is worth more than another pair of hands on a Saturday.
  • It gives the board something to point at. "We run four themed tours" is a different organisation from "we have a nice garden."
  • It is the cheapest programming there is. No build, no purchase, no permit — a printed sheet and somebody willing to talk.
  • It creates a reason to come back. Nobody visits a garden twice for the same reason. They will come back for rare fruit in July and butterflies in September.

The best thing about this park is already written down

Two hundred and twenty-nine plants already carry a one-line story, and behind those sit 948 further facts. Somebody has already done the hard part. A tour writer's job here is choosing and sequencing, not researching.

A format has been started — worth building on

The three written tours are beta. They were made quickly, by whoever was willing, to prove the idea was worth pursuing — and they proved it. They are not a finished house style, and they have not been through a branding pass or checked against each other for consistency.

But they arrived at something that works, and the next person should start from it rather than from a blank page. Every stop is number · name · a four-word subtitle · a direction line · two or three sentences.

1 Staghorn Fern — The Plant That Lives in Trees
Paver path through the trellis, elevated on your left
This whole colony clings to a tree trunk with no soil at all…
1 Century Plant — Three Lives
Crushed shell path, Cactus & Succulent Garden
The Century Plant does NOT live 100 years — that's a myth. It grows for 10 to 30 years, building
up energy the whole time. Then one day it shoots a flower stalk into the sky, taller than a
grown-up, blooms once, and dies. One chance. That's it.
YOUR CHALLENGE …

Four things worth keeping from them:

  1. Open with "Can you find them all?" and a Before You Start page listing every stop.
  2. "No phone required — keep it in your pocket and enjoy the park around you." A deliberate stance, and the right one. Do not design a tour that needs an app.
  3. The Kids tour gives each stop a YOUR CHALLENGE. That device is doing a lot of work and should travel to other tours.
  4. The subtitles are the craft. *Three Lives. The Cactus That Climbs. Right Plant, Right Place. The Idea, Grown Up.* Four words that tell you why to stop walking.

Ground rules

Say one thing per stop. The visitor is standing in the sun. One fact well chosen is remembered; four are not.

Lead with the thing, not the taxonomy. *"British cannonballs bounced off the hull of the USS Constitution — that's how it got the name Old Ironsides, and this is the tree" beats "Quercus virginiana, family Fagaceae."*

Never scold the visitor or the sign. Especially on tour 6. Common names are legitimate usage, often centuries old.

Do not promise a plant will be doing anything. Flowering, fruiting and bearing caterpillars are conditional. Write stops that work when nothing is happening; treat a bloom as a bonus.

Safety notes go in the script, not just on the page — particularly with children present.

Credit groups, not children. "The Bright Futures students", "the Manatee County Garden Club" — never a student's full name.

1 — Bite-Sized Tour 🅑 beta

The pitch. *"Only have a few minutes? The greatest-hits loop — the things you shouldn't miss, on the shortest path that touches them all."* Eight stops, about twenty minutes.

Why it works. It is the tour for the visitor who did not come for a tour, which is most of them. It also does the most useful job any of these can do: it proves to somebody that the park is more interesting than it looks, in the time they were going to spend anyway.

3 — Saving Our Ponds 🅑 beta

The pitch. *"How a problem pond became a model for the bay. Walk the restoration from the troubled Front Pond to the native plantings that turned it around."* Four stops, three ponds.

Why it works. It is the only existing tour that tells a story rather than listing plants — the problem, where we began, the work at scale, and the payoff. It also names its people and its funders: a Bay Mini-Grant from the Tampa Bay Estuary Program and a crew of **Bright Futures students**. That is what turns a walk into a case for support, and it is the model every other tour should learn from.

4 — Rare Fruit Trail 📝 draft

The pitch. *"Walk the Manatee Rare Fruit Council's exotic plantings. Best in fruiting season — what's ripe changes month to month."* Already on the site; needs its stops.

Why it works. Food is the easiest way into botany ever invented — nobody needs persuading to care about a fruit. It is also the most naturally clustered walk here: the grove sits between the bathroom, the Big Pond and the bridge, so it routes itself. And the MRFC partnership gives it a story about people as well as plants.

5 — Rare Palm & Plumeria 📝 draft

The pitch. *"Specimen palms you won't find in a typical yard, paired with fragrant plumeria that perfume the path in summer."* Keep this pairing — scent gives the walk a second sense, and it is a better hook than palms alone.

Why it works. Palms are the collection the park is actually known for, and this tour can teach one transferable skill: how to tell palms apart. Crownshaft or no crownshaft, fan or feather, solitary or clumping. Teach that in the first three stops and the visitor spends the rest of the walk identifying things unaided — the most satisfying thing a tour can do.

6 — Everything Here Is Lying to You ✨ new

The pitch. Every stop is a plant the visitor thinks they already know, and every one is mislabelled by its own common name. Not a palm. Not a lily. Not a cherry, an apple, an olive or a bamboo. It is funny, it is true, and by the end the visitor knows what a cycad, a bromeliad and an arum are — without once being taught.

Why it works. It has a spine: common names record what a plant looks like, not what it is. Every stop teaches the same lesson from a different angle. The stops are scattered across the park rather than clustered, which a walking route wants. And it is the only funny one.

**Fifty-three plants in the park carry a common name that names a family they do not belong to. Nineteen already have a finished page.** The full list, including the thirty-four still being written up, can be sent on request.

7 — The Shopfront ✨ new

The pitch. A row of specimen palms grows between the fence and the road — the plants every driver on 17th Ave NW sees, and for most people the only part of the collection they will ever look at. This is the only tour that does not require entering the park: no gate, no opening hours, no admission.

Why it works. It changes who a tour is for. Everything else serves somebody already inside; this reaches somebody who was not going to visit at all. Signs and a QR code out here turn passing traffic into outreach for the price of a stake.

And it is almost purely palms — 16 of the 19 species recorded. That makes it the tightest, most teachable walk of the nine: one family, many forms, side by side at the roadside.

8 — Host and Nectar ✨ new

The pitch. Everybody plants flowers for butterflies and wonders why they do not stay. The answer is that a butterfly needs two entirely different plants — **one to drink from and one to lay eggs on** — and the second is usually something people pull up as scruffy. The park has 43 host plants and 60 nectar plants with finished pages, so the lesson can be walked rather than explained.

Why it works. It sends people home able to do something, which is rare in a garden tour. And it has a perfect closing stop: Coontie and the Atala butterfly — a species thought gone from Florida, which returned when people replanted its one host plant.

9 — The Grand Tour 📝 draft

The pitch. *"The works. Every area, in a sensible order, with the full story along the way — the default 'see it all' route to hand anyone who asks."* 60–90 minutes.

Why it works. It is the only tour that shows the collection whole, and it can be assembled almost entirely from stops written for the other eight — so it costs very little **once they exist.**

Suggested spine, one or two stops from each:

  1. Open at the frontage (tour 7) — the collection starts before the gate
  2. Something that lies (tour 6) — sets a playful tone early
  3. The palm skill (tour 5) — crownshaft versus none, then keep using it
  4. A rarity (tour 5) — now that they can see what makes it different
  5. The butterfly pair (tour 8) — host and nectar, the practical lesson
  6. The pond story (tour 3) — the park at its most purposeful
  7. Rare fruit (tour 4) — the crowd-pleaser, best placed late
  8. Close on something living up close — the Staghorn, or whatever is in bloom

What it needs: to be built last. Cheapest of the nine once the others exist, most expensive if attempted first.

Ideas to flesh out — placeholders, not proposals

Blooming tour. The obvious one, and the hardest to maintain: it would have to be updated constantly, and bloom_months in the database is free-text prose, so nothing can query it by month. Needs a data change or a weekly walk.

Creatures tour. 91 animals already have a page, from an Atlantic blue crab in a pond to a spider that spits venom. Blocked on knowing what gets seen when — the seasonality is not recorded yet. Worth building once it is.

What we lost in storms. Sixteen plants are recorded as lost, four of them stolen. Emotionally strong — and the Pagoda Flower, written off as dead and found alive in 2026, is a real ending. But uncertain whether visitors want it.
