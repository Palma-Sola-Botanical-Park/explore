Wildlife in the Park

Herons on the ponds, owls in the trees, otters, turtles and butterflies, ten acres from the bay.

We share the park with a lot of animals. More than a hundred kinds have pages on this site, from the Roseate Spoonbill to the anole doing push-ups on a fence post, and some only visit for the winter. Here is how to find them, and a few to look for.

Tips for spotting wildlife

Come early. Birds and most animals are busiest in the first hours after sunrise, and the paths are quiet.

Be patient. Animals won’t flock to you the way they do for Cinderella. Find a bench, sit still a while, and let the park forget you’re there.

Look up, then look down. An owl has nested in one of our Royal Palms. Under a nest you may find a pellet: a tangle of fur and tiny bones the owl coughed up, the leftovers of its last meal.

Start at the water. The ponds are where the food is, so birds and land animals stay close to them too. Otters usually turn up early on the Big Pond, then slip out by the dam into a quiet gully. The turtles lined up below were basking in the Front Pond, right by the entrance.

Birds here all year

Most of our wildlife pages are birds, from waders on the pond banks to owls that sit out the day in plain sight. Look too for the Osprey, which once dropped its tilapia bones under the Kapok by the Cypress Pond, the Pileated Woodpecker, whose big rectangular holes are easier to find than the bird, and the White Ibis, probing the lawn for crayfish. Winter visitors and birds passing through

From October to April, birds that nest up north spend the winter here or stop on their way farther south. In most of the country the American Robin means spring; here it means winter. Listen for the Eastern Phoebe, which says its own name, and look down for the Palm Warbler, which spends more time on the ground than in any palm. Plus more.

Butterflies and moths

The Butterfly Garden is planted for them, but they turn up all over the park. Look for the Black Witch Moth, a huge dark moth that some call an omen of death and others a sign of money. Plus more.

Otters, turtles, snakes and more

Some of our wildlife keeps out of sight. Watch for the Red Cornsnake, the Virginia Opossum, the only marsupial native to North America, and the raccoons that sometimes fall into our bins. We lower a plank, and they often stop to look back on the way out. Plus more.

The small stuff

Insects, spiders, worms and the rest do most of the park’s work. They pollinate the flowers, turn fallen leaves into soil, and feed nearly everything bigger than they are. If you love the small stuff, you’re our kind of visitor.

A few we have found: a blue crab in one of our ponds, which are still a little salty from the bay; the Mealybug Destroyer, a beetle whose larva disguises itself as the pest it eats; and the antlion, whose larva digs a pit in loose sand and waits at the bottom. Plus more.
