Great Egret

Ardea alba

At a glance

  • The great egret hunts through sheer stillness, waiting motionless in the shallows until a fish drifts close, then uncoiling its neck in a split-second strike.
  • Standing over three feet tall and entirely snow white, it is hard to miss at the water's edge.
  • A little over a century ago it was nearly wiped out — hunted in breeding season for the lacy plumes on its back, which were prized for women's hats.
  • The outrage over that loss helped spark the early conservation movement, and the great egret became the symbol of the National Audubon Society.

How to know it

Easily confused with

Great Blue Heron — A rare all-white great blue heron is bulkier with pale legs and a heavier bill; the great egret is slim, with black legs and feet and a yellow bill.

Snowy Egret — The snowy egret is much smaller with a black bill and bright yellow feet; the great egret is tall with a yellow bill and all-black legs and feet.

Plumage

Entirely white.

Bill

Long, dagger-shaped, and yellow.

Legs and feet

Black, top to bottom.

Breeding adults

Grow long, wispy plumes on the back and develop a greenish patch of skin on the face.

Voice

Usually quiet away from the colony. When disturbed it gives a low, hoarse croak; nesting birds are noisier.

Size

up to roughly 5.5 feet (52 to 67 inches); about 3.3 feet (39 inches) tall

What to look for

A large, all-white wading bird with a yellow bill and black legs, standing motionless or stalking slowly through shallow water. The yellow bill plus black legs separate it from the smaller snowy egret, which has a black bill and bright yellow feet.

Where to find it here

At the edges of the ponds, standing still in the shallows or stalking slowly along the bank. A tall white bird holding a statue-like pose over the water is almost always this species.

Year-round resident. Often most active hunting in the cooler hours of morning and late afternoon.

How it lives

A patient, visual hunter. It wades slowly or stands frozen in the shallows, watching, then strikes in an instant. Active by day, it nests in colonies, often mixed with other wading birds, and flies with slow, deep wingbeats and its neck folded into an S.

Mostly fish, plus frogs, crayfish, insects, small reptiles, and the occasional small mammal — caught with a fast jab of that dagger bill.

What it does here

A top shallow-water predator that helps regulate fish and amphibian populations. As a colony nester sensitive to wetland health, it is also a living indicator of how well an ecosystem like the Everglades is doing.

Found on every continent except Antarctica. In North America it is widespread, and in Florida it is a common, year-round resident of marshes, ponds, and shorelines wherever there are fish to catch.

In the late 1800s, fashion nearly cost the great egret its existence. During breeding season the bird grows long, lacy plumes called aigrettes, and those feathers became must-have decorations on stylish hats. Plume hunters raided nesting colonies, often killing adults and leaving the chicks to die.

The public backlash was a turning point. Early Audubon societies formed in protest, and landmark laws — the Lacey Act of 1900 and the Migratory Bird Treaty Act of 1918 — eventually shut down the feather trade.

The great egret recovered so fully that the National Audubon Society adopted it as its emblem. Its abundance in the park today is a gentle reminder that landscapes can heal when given a collective chance — and that protections, once won, still have to be maintained.

Take care

What to do

Admire from a distance and never feed it. Crowding a hunting egret ruins its chance at a meal and stresses the bird.
