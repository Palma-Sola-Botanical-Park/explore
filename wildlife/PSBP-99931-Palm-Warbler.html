Palm Warbler

Setophaga palmarum
On this page

At a glance

  • Palm Warblers spend unusual amounts of time on the ground, walking rather than hopping and bobbing the tail almost constantly. That tail movement is often the quickest way to recognize one.
  • The name is misleading. The species was named from early specimens collected on Hispaniola, where palms were part of the landscape; Palm Warblers themselves are not especially tied to palms.
  • Two forms occur in Florida. 'Yellow' Palm Warblers are brighter yellow below and winter mainly along the Gulf Coast; the duller western form has a whitish belly. The two overlap in the Southeast.
  • Unlike most warblers, Palm Warblers are often easiest to find in open grass, weedy edges and low shrubs rather than high in trees.

How to know it

Summary

A small brownish warbler with yellow under the tail, a pale eyebrow and a habit of walking on the ground while repeatedly bobbing its tail.

Breeding plumage

Spring birds develop a rusty cap and stronger breast streaking. The Yellow form is extensively yellow below; the western form has a paler belly.

Winter plumage

Winter birds are duller brown and less strongly streaked, but yellow under the tail and the repeated tail bob remain useful clues.

Voice

The song is a thin, buzzy trill. Calls are short, sharp notes often given in flight.

Size

About five inches long, with a wingspan around eight inches.

What to look for

Look low. A Palm Warbler often walks across grass, bare ground or low vegetation while repeatedly bobbing its tail. Yellow under the tail, a pale eyebrow and brownish streaking help confirm the identification.

Easily confused with

Yellow-rumped Warbler — Yellow-rumped Warblers usually forage more in shrubs and trees and show a bright yellow rump. Palm Warblers spend more time walking on the ground and repeatedly bob the tail.

Where to find it here

Look in open grassy and weedy areas, along woodland edges, in low shrubs and around leaf litter. Palm Warblers spend much more time near the ground than most warblers and may forage with sparrows and Yellow-rumped Warblers.

A winter visitor to Florida, also seen during migration. Palma Sola has only a few records, so local abundance and peak timing are not yet established.

When

A winter visitor and migrant in Florida. Palma Sola records are currently too limited to define a local peak.

How it lives

It spends much of its time walking on the ground and through low vegetation, repeatedly bobbing its tail as it searches for prey.

It picks insects and spiders from the ground and low plants and sometimes flies out after airborne insects. Fruit and seeds supplement the diet in winter.

What it does here

Palm Warblers eat small insects and spiders and are themselves prey for larger animals. Winter fruit feeding may also move some seeds.

The Palm Warbler's name overstates its relationship with palms. At Palma Sola, low vegetation, weedy ground and leaf litter are more important because that is where it searches for insects. Fruiting shrubs such as bayberry and sea grape can also provide winter food.

Native to North America. Palm Warblers breed mainly in boreal bogs and open conifer country across Canada, then winter in the southeastern United States, Caribbean and parts of Central America.
