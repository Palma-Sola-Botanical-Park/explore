Turkey Vulture

Cathartes aura
On this page

At a glance

  • A large dark bird circling overhead with its wings held in a shallow V, rocking side to side and rarely flapping, is almost certainly a Turkey Vulture.
  • It locates carrion largely by smell, an unusual ability among birds, and can find carcasses concealed beneath foliage.
  • The bare red head is not decorative. A featherless head stays cleaner when feeding inside a carcass.
  • It eats only carrion and does not kill live prey.

How to know it

In flight

Large and dark, soaring with the wings held in a shallow V and rocking from side to side. It rarely flaps.

Underwing

Two-toned — dark leading edge, silvery-gray flight feathers behind.

Head

Small and bare, red in adults.

Size

Wingspan around six feet.

What to look for

Judge it by the shallow V and the rocking. A Bald Eagle holds its wings flat and steady; a vulture teeters.

Easily confused with

Bald Eagle — Bald Eagles hold their broad wings flat and fly steadily, with a large head projecting forward. Turkey Vultures have a tiny head, hold their wings in a shallow V and rock from side to side.

Where to find it here

Look up. Almost any large bird circling overhead without flapping is this one.

A year-round Florida resident, visible overhead on any warm day once thermals begin to rise.

How it lives

Soars for hours on rising warm air, covering ground with almost no effort while searching for the smell of carrion below.

It roosts communally, often in dead trees or on structures, and spreads its wings in the morning sun to dry and warm.

It finds carrion by smell, which few birds can do. The sense is sharp enough that gas-pipeline crews have historically watched for circling vultures to locate leaks — the odorant added to natural gas smells like decay.

What it does here

A scavenger that removes carrion which would otherwise decay in the open, reducing what is left to spread disease.

Dead trees as roosts

Vultures gather at communal roosts, and bare dead trees are favored because open branches give easy take-off and room to spread wings in the sun. A standing snag is worth more to them than a dense living canopy.
