Turkey Vulture

Cathartes aura
On this page

At a glance

  • The large, dark bird soaring overhead in lazy circles with its wings held in a slight V — rocking side to side without flapping — is almost certainly a turkey vulture.
  • It finds carrion primarily by smell, one of the very few birds that can — its sense of smell is powerful enough to detect a carcass hidden under a forest canopy.
  • The bare red head is not decorative; it is hygienic — featherless skin is easier to keep clean when you eat by plunging your head into a carcass.
  • Despite their association with death, vultures are essential sanitation workers, cleaning up carrion that would otherwise spread disease.

How to know it

Flight

Large dark bird soaring with wings in a shallow V (dihedral), rocking side to side. Rarely flaps.

Underwing

Two-toned: dark leading edge, silvery-gray flight feathers.

Head

Small, bare, red (adult) — visible at close range.

Size

Wingspan about 6 feet.

Voice

Essentially voiceless — lacks the vocal anatomy of most birds. Hisses and grunts when disturbed.

Size

25 to 32 inches; about 6 feet

What to look for

A large dark bird soaring in circles with wings held in a V and a distinctive side-to-side rocking. Up close, the small bare red head is unmistakable.

Where to find it here

Look up — almost any time you see a large bird circling overhead without flapping, it is a turkey vulture.

Year-round resident, visible overhead on any warm day.

How it lives

It flies without flapping. A turkey vulture finds a thermal and rides it for hours, rocking slightly, wings held in a shallow V. On cold mornings you will see them perched with wings spread open to the sun, drying out.

Carrion only. It does not kill anything. Roosts are communal, usually in a dead tree or on a roof.

What it does here

Nature's cleanup crew — removes carrion that would otherwise breed disease. A keystone scavenger.

Native across the Americas, from southern Canada to South America. A common year-round resident in Florida.

The turkey vulture is one of the most recognizable silhouettes in the Florida sky. It soars for hours on thermals with almost no flapping, tilting and rocking in a distinctive wobbly flight that experienced birders can identify at great distance.

Unlike most birds, it relies heavily on smell — a rare trait. Gas-pipeline crews have historically watched for circling vultures to find leaks, because the odorant added to natural gas mimics the smell of decay.

It is harmless to live animals and pets. Despite its intimidating size and association with death, it feeds exclusively on carrion.
