Ruby-throated Hummingbird

Archilochus colubris
On this page

At a glance

  • Ruby-throated Hummingbirds can hover almost motionless at a flower while beating their wings about 53 times each second.
  • A male's throat can flash brilliant red and then look nearly black when the viewing angle changes. The color comes from microscopic feather structure interacting with light.
  • Some migrants cross the Gulf of Mexico in a single over-water flight, while others follow the Gulf Coast around it.
  • It weighs about as much as a penny but may chase much larger birds away from a productive flower patch.

How to know it

Size

Tiny — only about three inches long, with a wingspan around four inches.

Male

Green above and pale below, with an iridescent red throat that can look black when the light is wrong.

Female

Green above and whitish below, without the male's red throat; the outer tail feathers show white tips.

Flight

Can hover in place, stop abruptly and fly backward.

Voice

Calls are rapid, high chips and chatters. At close range, the wing hum may reveal a bird before you see it.

What to look for

Watch flowering beds for a tiny green bird hovering at tubular flowers, then darting to a nearby perch. Adult males show the red throat only when the light strikes it correctly.

Easily confused with

Rufous Hummingbird — Females and young can resemble a Ruby-throated Hummingbird but usually show rusty orange in the flanks or tail. Adult male Rufous Hummingbirds are extensively orange.

Where to find it here

Watch flower beds and woodland edges, especially where tubular blooms are producing nectar. A feeding bird often returns repeatedly to a productive patch and perches nearby between visits.

Most likely during spring and autumn migration. Ruby-throated Hummingbirds can also occur along the Gulf Coast outside the main migration periods, so an individual outside those windows is possible.

How it lives

Nectar supplies much of the bird's energy, but Ruby-throated Hummingbirds also catch tiny insects and spiders, sometimes taking insects directly from spider webs.

Feeds while hovering and frequently returns to favored flowers. Individuals may defend productive feeding areas by chasing other hummingbirds and larger birds.

A nest built with spider silk

The female builds a tiny nest on a slender tree branch using plant down held together with spider silk. The silk lets the nest stretch as the young grow.

The metabolism sets the whole schedule. It visits hundreds of flowers a day and takes small insects and spiders for protein, its heart runs past 1,200 beats a minute in flight, and it drops into a torpid state overnight to avoid starving before morning.

What it does here

Carries pollen between flowers while feeding on nectar, and also consumes tiny insects and spiders.

Flowers, trees and spider silk

Flowers provide the high-energy nectar that fuels hummingbird flight. Tubular red, orange and pink blooms are frequently used, but hummingbirds visit flowers of other colors as well. Trees provide resting places and, on the breeding grounds, slender branches for the female's spider-silk-bound nest.

This is the only hummingbird that regularly breeds east of the Mississippi, so in this half of the country it is simply the hummingbird.

Take care

What to do

Flowering plants supply everything this bird needs. Watch without crowding one that is feeding.
