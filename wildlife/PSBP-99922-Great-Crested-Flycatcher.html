Great Crested Flycatcher

Myiarchus crinitus
On this page

At a glance

  • A large flycatcher with an olive back, lemon-yellow belly and rusty tail, usually high in the canopy.
  • Listen before you look. A sharp rising WHEEP carrying through the treetops is usually this bird.
  • It nests in tree cavities and often works shed snakeskin into the lining — or plastic and cellophane where snakeskin is not available.
  • It is a summer bird here, present spring through late summer and gone by autumn.

How to know it

Plumage

Olive-brown above with a gray throat and breast and a bright lemon-yellow belly. The tail and wings flash rusty.

Shape

A large, big-headed flycatcher with a slight crest, about seven or eight inches long.

Voice

A loud rising WHEEP that carries through the treetops, plus a rolling prrrreet.

What to look for

Scan the canopy and mid-story of shaded trees and woodland edges. This bird is rarely near the ground, and is usually heard before it is seen.

Easily confused with

Eastern Phoebe — Eastern Phoebes are smaller and plain gray-brown, wag their tails constantly and hunt from low perches. Great Crested Flycatchers are larger, yellow-bellied, rusty-tailed and stay high in the canopy.

Where to find it here

High in the canopy and mid-story of woodland edges, hammocks and shaded park trees — rarely on or near the ground.

A summer breeding resident, present roughly spring through late summer. Do not look for one on a January walk.

How it lives

Hunts from a perch in the canopy, darting out to take insects in flight and gleaning others from foliage. Beetles, crickets, caterpillars, moths and bees are all taken, along with wild fruit.

Snakeskin in the nest

It nests in tree cavities and lines the nest with an odd mix of materials, shed snakeskin among them. Where snakeskin is scarce, birds have been recorded substituting cellophane and plastic — which suggests the appeal may be the crinkled, translucent texture rather than the snake.

It hunts on the wing, watching from a still perch and then launching after a flying insect, taking it out of the air with an audible clack of the bill before looping back to the same branch.

What it does here

It cannot dig its own hole

This flycatcher nests in cavities but cannot excavate one. It depends on holes made by woodpeckers or opened by decay, so mature trees and standing dead wood are what allow it to breed here at all.

It lines the nest with a shed snakeskin. The best guess is deliberate intimidation — a snakeskin at the entrance may put off mice, squirrels and other small raiders after the eggs. When no snakeskin is available the birds improvise with whatever crinkles the same way, and plastic wrappers, onion skins and cellophane have all turned up in nests.

Take care

What to do

Leave dead limbs and cavity trees standing wherever it is safe to keep them. This bird nests in tree holes it cannot excavate itself, so standing dead wood is its housing supply.
