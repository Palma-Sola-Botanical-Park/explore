Great Crested Flycatcher

Myiarchus crinitus
On this page

At a glance

  • A large, colorful flycatcher with an olive back, a lemon-yellow belly, and a rusty tail - and a famous habit of weaving shed snakeskins into its tree-cavity nest.
  • Listen before you look. This bird doesn't sing so much as shout: a loud, rising "WHEEP!" that rings through the canopy and usually gives it away long before you spot it.
  • A genuine aerial acrobat - it perches dead still, then bursts out to snatch a flying insect mid-air with an audible snap of the bill before swinging back to its branch.
  • Keep your eyes up. This is a canopy bird that rarely drops to the ground, so scan the treetops for that flash of yellow against the green.

How to know it

Plumage

Olive-brown above with a gray throat and breast and a bright lemon-yellow belly. The tail and wings flash rufous (rusty) - a good field mark.

Shape

A large, big-headed flycatcher with a slight bushy crest and an upright posture when perched.

Voice

Loud and vocal. The signature call is a sharp, rising "WHEEP!" that carries through the treetops, along with a rolling "prrrreet." If you hear a bossy, far-carrying shout from high in the canopy, this is often the bird making it.

Size

about 7 to 8 inches (a large flycatcher)

What to look for

Look up. This is a canopy and mid-story bird that rarely comes to the ground - scan the treetops for a flash of yellow belly and a rusty tail, and let the loud rising "WHEEP!" lead your eyes to it.

Where to find it here

High in the canopy and mid-story of woodland edges, hammocks, and shaded park trees - rarely on or near the ground.

Spring through late summer, when breeding birds are most vocal and active (roughly March to September). They leave by early fall and are absent in winter, so don't look for them on a January walk. Listen for the loud "WHEEP!" to locate one.

When

A summer resident in our area - arrives around March or April to breed and departs by early fall for the Caribbean and South America. Absent in winter.

How it lives

Hawking

It perches still, spots a flying insect, launches out and takes it from the air with an audible clack of the bill, then loops back to the same perch. That clack is often the first sign one is there.

Beetles, crickets, caterpillars, moths and bees, gleaned from foliage as well as caught in flight, plus wild fruit — elderberries and blackberries among them.

A cavity nester, and it defends its hole vigorously. Conspicuous by sound while staying hidden in the leaves.

What it does here

A canopy insect predator that helps keep beetle, moth, and other insect populations in check, and a seed disperser for the fruiting plants it feeds on. As a cavity nester it depends on older trees and dead snags with suitable holes.

Breeds across the eastern United States and southern Canada and winters in the Caribbean, Mexico, and South America. In our area it is a summer breeding resident - present spring through late summer and gone by fall.

The Great Crested Flycatcher is a cavity nester, raising its young inside holes in trees (and readily using nest boxes). It is famous for lining the nest with a shed snakeskin - and scientists think this is deliberate intimidation: the snakeskin may help scare off mice, squirrels, and other small raiders eyeing the eggs. When no snakeskin is available, these birds improvise with crinkly substitutes that mimic it: plastic wrappers, onion skins, and cellophane have all turned up in their nests.

It is a true flycatcher, hunting on the wing. From a still perch it watches for flying insects, then launches into a quick aerial chase, snapping the prey out of the air with an audible clack of the bill before looping back - pure canopy acrobatics.
