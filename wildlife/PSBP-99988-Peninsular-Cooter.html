Peninsular Cooter

Pseudemys peninsularis

At a glance

  • The peninsular cooter is a large, dark freshwater turtle you will most often spot basking on a log or bank, soaking up the sun — often several piled together.
  • Look for the bright yellow pinstripes running along its dark neck and shell; they are the easiest way to recognize it.
  • Adults are almost entirely vegetarian, grazing on underwater plants, which is unusual among Florida's pond turtles.
  • All that basking has a purpose: warming in the sun helps the cold-blooded turtle digest its fibrous, plant-based meals. Approach too quickly and you will hear a series of quiet plops as the whole row slips back into the water.

How to know it

Shell

Domed and dark olive to brown, often with faint yellowish markings, growing dull and worn with age.

Skin

Dark with thin yellow stripes on the head, neck, and legs.

Size

Large — clearly bigger than a slider when full grown; females larger than males.

What to look for

A big, dark turtle basking on a log or bank, or just its head poking above the water as it swims. The combination of large size, domed dark shell, and yellow head stripes points to a cooter rather than the smaller, more boldly marked pond slider.

Easily confused with

Florida Red-bellied Cooter — The red-bellied cooter shows a reddish wash below and a red arrow-shaped mark on the head; the peninsular cooter has yellow hairpin-loop stripes and no red.

Pond Slider — Sliders are smaller and wear a colored patch just behind the eye; the peninsular cooter is larger with no ear patch.

Where to find it here

Basking on logs, banks, and debris at the ponds on sunny days, or as a dark head moving across the water's surface.

Year-round resident. Most visible while basking on warm, sunny days.

When

Most visible basking on warm, sunny days.

How it lives

Much of the day is spent basking — a cooter uses the sun to run its body temperature — alternating with dives to feed. Wary of people, and quick to drop off a log at any movement.

Adults graze on submerged aquatic plants and algae. Young turtles also take insects, snails and other small animals.

What it does here

As a plant-grazing turtle, the cooter helps shape underwater vegetation, and its eggs and young feed a range of predators. A healthy basking population is a good sign of a healthy pond.

Adults graze on submerged native vegetation such as eelgrass (tapegrass) and pondweeds, along with algae — making the cooter one of the pond's natural plant-grazers.

Native to peninsular Florida and found almost exclusively here. A close relative of other cooters and sliders found across the southeastern United States.

The peninsular cooter is one of the big, dark turtles you will see hauled out and basking on logs, banks, and floating debris on sunny days. Basking is not just for show — it helps the turtle warm up, dry off, and fight skin parasites and algae.

While young cooters eat some insects and other small animals, adults shift to a largely plant-based diet, grazing on submerged aquatic vegetation. That makes them important grazers in the pond ecosystem.

Cooters are long-lived and slow to mature, so the adults basking at the park's ponds may well be older than many of the visitors watching them.

Take care

What to do

Watch from a distance and do not handle or feed it. Basking turtles need their sun time; approaching just sends them diving off their logs.
