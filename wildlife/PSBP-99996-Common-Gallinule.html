Common Gallinule

Gallinula galeata
On this page

At a glance

  • Floating along the marshy edges of the ponds is a slate-gray bird with a red shield on its forehead — a bit like a swimming chicken.
  • It is often taken for a duck, but a look at its feet gives it away: it belongs to the rail family and has no webbing at all.
  • It has long, widely splayed toes that work like snowshoes, spreading its weight so it can walk across lily pads and floating plants without sinking.
  • It is also one of the more talkative residents of the wetland, filling the quiet with an unexpected mix of clucks, squawks, and odd, laughing chatter.

How to know it

Adults

Slate-gray body, brownish back, a bold white stripe along the flanks, and a red frontal shield with a yellow-tipped red bill.

Legs and feet

Yellow-green with very long, unwebbed toes.

Young

Duller and browner, lacking the bright red bill.

Voice

Loud and varied for its size: sharp clucks, squawks, and a whinnying, laughing series of notes.

Size

about 13 inches; 21 to 24 inches

What to look for

A dark, hen-sized water bird swimming with a head-bobbing motion, showing a vivid red-and-yellow bill and a white side stripe. If it climbs onto floating plants on long, skinny toes rather than webbed feet, you have confirmed a gallinule, not a duck.

Easily confused with

American Coot — The American coot has a white bill and forehead shield and lobed toes; the gallinule has a red shield, a yellow-tipped red bill, and a white stripe along the flank.

Where to find it here

Along the vegetated margins of the ponds — watch where plants meet open water, and listen for its loud calls from the reeds.

Year-round resident. Most easily found near pond edges with thick plant cover.

How it lives

It stays near cover at the water's edge, swimming, walking across floating plants, and ducking into the reeds when something alarms it. Usually in ones and twos, and often loud about it.

An opportunist: seeds, leaves and stems of water plants, plus insects, snails and other small aquatic creatures, taken while swimming, dabbling or walking on vegetation.

What it does here

Eats and spreads wetland plants and helps keep aquatic insect and snail numbers in check. A dependable presence in healthy freshwater marshes.

Feeds on and moves through marsh plants — cattails, reeds, and floating vegetation such as lily pads.

Native across the Americas, from the United States south through South America. In Florida it is a common, year-round resident of freshwater marshes, ponds, and any quiet water with thick edge vegetation.

The common gallinule is often mistaken for a duck because of how it swims, pumping its head as it goes, but it belongs to the rail family — and a look at its feet gives it away.

Where a duck has webbing, the gallinule has long, splayed toes. They spread its weight out so it can clamber over lily pads and matted vegetation, picking at food along the way without breaking through.

Until 2011 this bird was lumped with a close Old World relative under the name Common Moorhen. Scientists then split them into separate species, and the New World bird became the Common Gallinule.

Take care

What to do

Watch from a distance and do not feed it. Gallinules are shy by nature and slip into cover when crowded.
