Eastern Giant Swallowtail

Heraclides cresphontes
On this page

At a glance

  • One of Florida's largest butterflies: dark wings crossed by yellow bands in a rough X, gliding long distances between wingbeats.
  • Citrus growers call the caterpillar an orangedog, because it eats citrus leaves.
  • Wild Lime, a native plant that grows in the park, is one of the plants its caterpillars eat.
  • When it lands, the underside of the wings is mostly yellow, nearly the reverse of the dark top.

How to know it

Size and pattern

A very large swallowtail, about five to seven inches across. The wings are dark brown to black with two bold diagonal bands of yellow spots that meet near the body in a rough X, and the tails have yellow centers.

Underneath

The underside is mostly yellow with black borders, nearly the reverse of the dark upper side.

Where to find it here

Flowers and woodland edges

Watch flowering beds and sunny woodland edges for adults. It flies through most of the year in Florida, especially on warm, sunny days.

How it lives

Gliding

The flight is strong and leisurely, with long glides between wingbeats. Males patrol routes along woodland edges and through citrus groves looking for females. Adults visit a wide range of flowers for nectar.

A caterpillar dressed as a dropping

Females lay their eggs singly on the upper side of a leaf. The caterpillar is olive-brown with creamy patches, and resting out in the open it passes for a bird dropping. Feeding usually happens at night.

What it does

Citrus family

The caterpillars eat plants in the citrus family. In Florida that means cultivated citrus and native plants such as Wild Lime, which grows in the park. A disturbed caterpillar rears up an orange, forked organ behind its head and gives off a smell that repels small predators such as ants.

Why orangedog

Citrus growers know the caterpillar as the orangedog, a minor pest of sweet orange. Florida has two or three generations a year, so adults and caterpillars overlap through the warm months.
