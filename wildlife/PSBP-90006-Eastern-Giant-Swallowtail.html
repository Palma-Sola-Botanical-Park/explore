Eastern Giant Swallowtail

Heraclides cresphontes

At a glance

  • The Giant Swallowtail is among Florida's largest butterflies, with broad dark wings crossed by yellow bands. Its long glides make it conspicuous over gardens and woodland edges.
  • The caterpillar is called an orangedog because it feeds on citrus and other plants in the rue family. Its mottled brown, white and gray pattern resembles a bird dropping.
  • If disturbed, the caterpillar can extend a forked orange organ called an osmeterium from behind its head. It releases a strong odor that helps deter predators.
  • In south Florida, adults can be seen throughout the year. Farther north, the flight season is more restricted.

How to know it

Size

A very large swallowtail, usually around four to six inches across. Females often run slightly larger than males.

Upperside

Wings are dark brown to black with two bold diagonal bands of yellow spots — one crossing the forewings, one sweeping across the hindwings — that meet near the body to form a rough X-shape. Hindwings have yellow-centered tails and a small blue-and-red spot near the inner margin.

Underside

Largely yellow with black borders and blue scaling near the body — a striking contrast to the dark upperside. A small red patch on the ventral hindwing helps distinguish this species from the similar but much rarer Schaus' Swallowtail.

In flight

Broad wings held flat, with long slow beats interspersed with extended glides.

Caterpillar

Brown, gray, and white with a creamy saddle mark — a convincing bird-dropping mimic at every stage. Mature larvae may also show faint eyespot-like markings. When disturbed, the orange-red forked osmeterium everts from just behind the head.

Size

Wingspan usually about four to six inches.

What to look for

Look for a very large dark swallowtail crossed by broad yellow bands. It often alternates slow wingbeats with long glides. The caterpillar rests in the open and looks like a bird dropping.

Where to find it here

Watch flowering beds and sunny woodland edges for adults. For caterpillars, check plants in the citrus family, especially citrus, wild lime and Hercules' club. The mottled larvae often rest openly on leaves or stems.

Adults can be seen through much of the year in southwest Florida, especially on warm sunny days.

When

Multiple broods overlap, so adults and caterpillars may both be present. Cooler days reduce activity.

How it lives

A strong flier that spends most of its time gliding — with that wingspan it can cover ground without much flapping. Males run patrol routes through wooded edges and citrus groves looking for females, mostly in the afternoon.

A caterpillar dressed as bird droppings

Females lay eggs singly on the upper surface of a leaf. The caterpillar often rests exposed on a leaf or stem during the day, where its mottled pattern resembles a bird dropping, and feeds at night.

Multiple broods follow one another through the year in Florida, so adults and caterpillars can be present at the same time.

What it does here

Caterpillars consume leaves of citrus-family plants and are prey for birds, spiders and predatory insects, as well as parasitoid wasps and flies. Adults visit many kinds of flowers and can carry pollen between them.

The caterpillar can eat only a limited group of host plants, chiefly members of the citrus family. In Florida these include native wild lime and Hercules' club as well as cultivated citrus. Adults use a much wider range of flowering plants for nectar.

The Eastern Giant Swallowtail is native to eastern North America, ranging from southern New England and the Great Lakes south through Florida, the Caribbean, and into Mexico. It is common throughout the entire state of Florida and is one of the most frequently seen large butterflies in the region. Florida and the deep South are the only parts of the eastern United States where the species can overwinter and remain active year-round. Populations once thought to extend west to South America are now recognized as a separate species, the Western Giant Swallowtail (Papilio rumiko), based on DNA evidence; the butterfly at Palma Sola is the eastern form.

The round eggs are orange-colored and laid singly on host leaves.
