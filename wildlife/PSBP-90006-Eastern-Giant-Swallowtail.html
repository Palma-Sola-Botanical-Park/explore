Eastern Giant Swallowtail

Heraclides cresphontes

At a glance

  • Florida's largest butterfly — and the largest in North America — the Eastern Giant Swallowtail is hard to miss when it glides through the park. Its wingspan regularly exceeds five inches, and its bold black-and-yellow pattern is unmistakable on the wing.
  • Its caterpillars are called 'orangedogs' because they feed on citrus leaves and can defoliate a young tree in short order. Look closely at the larva, though, and you'll see one of nature's better disguises: it mimics a bird dropping so convincingly that most predators ignore it entirely.
  • When a caterpillar feels threatened, it everts a bright red-orange, Y-shaped organ called the osmeterium from just behind its head. The organ mimics a snake's tongue visually, and it releases a chemical cocktail — including isobutyric acid and 2-methylbutyric acid — that smells like rancid, sour citrus and drives off ants, wasps, and other invertebrate predators.
  • Florida is one of the few places in the eastern United States where this butterfly can overwinter and fly year-round. Most of the continent sees only one or two brief flight seasons; here you can find adults in any month.

How to know it

Size

The largest butterfly in North America. Wingspan typically 4 to 6.5 inches; occasional individuals reach nearly 7 inches. Both sexes are large, with females often running slightly larger than males.

Upperside

Wings are dark brown to black with two bold diagonal bands of yellow spots — one crossing the forewings, one sweeping across the hindwings — that meet near the body to form a rough X-shape. Hindwings have yellow-centered tails and a small blue-and-red spot near the inner margin.

Underside

Largely yellow with black borders and blue scaling near the body — a striking contrast to the dark upperside. When perched with wings open, the yellow underside is the most visible surface. A small red patch on the ventral hindwing helps distinguish this species from the similar but much rarer Schaus' Swallowtail.

In flight

The combination of large size, dark wings, and yellow banding is diagnostic. The leisurely, gliding flight is distinctive — broad wings held flat, long slow beats interspersed with extended glides.

Caterpillar

Brown, gray, and white with a creamy saddle mark — a convincing bird-dropping mimic at every stage. Mature larvae may also show faint eyespot-like markings. When disturbed, the orange-red forked osmeterium everts from just behind the head.

Voice

Essentially silent, as with all butterflies. No calls, songs, or vocalizations at any life stage; the soft rush of large wings in flight is the only sound associated with this species.

Size

Wingspan 4 to 6.5 inches, occasionally to nearly 7 inches; body length roughly 1 to 1.5 inches

What to look for

Look for a very large, dark butterfly with a prominent yellow-banded X across the wings. The gliding, unhurried flight style is as much a field mark as the pattern — no other common Florida butterfly of this size moves quite like it. At rest with wings spread, the yellow underside is prominent; the small red spot near the hindwing tail is a reliable clincher. On host plants, search for the bird-dropping caterpillar on upper leaf surfaces or along stems.

Where to find it here

Check any flowering garden bed for nectaring adults, especially plants with larger open blooms — lantana, pentas, and azalea are reliable draws. Scan the canopy edges and open sky above the treeline for the characteristic gliding silhouette. On native Zanthoxylum shrubs or any citrus trees in the park, examine upper leaf surfaces carefully for the cryptic caterpillar. Adults often come to eye level when nectaring, making them easy to observe at close range.

Year-round in Manatee County, with adults present in every month. Multiple broods mean there is rarely a gap in the adult flight. Activity peaks during warm, sunny mornings and afternoons; on cooler winter days adults are less visible but not absent. Spring and summer tend to produce the most conspicuous numbers.

When

Year-round resident and flier in Florida, including Manatee County. Multiple broods mean adults are present in virtually every month; cooler winter days may reduce visibility but adults can appear on warm days even in January and February.

How it lives

A strong, swift flier that spends most of its time gliding — with that wingspan it can cover ground without much flapping. Males run patrol routes through wooded edges and citrus groves looking for females, mostly in the afternoon.

A caterpillar dressed as bird droppings

Females lay eggs singly on the upper surface of a leaf. The caterpillar feeds at night and spends the day sitting motionless and exposed on a stem, relying entirely on looking like something no bird would eat.

Caterpillars eat the citrus and rue family — wild lime and Hercules' club among Florida natives, plus common rue, hop tree and ornamental citrus. Adults nectar at lantana, azalea, goldenrod, bougainvillea, swamp milkweed and Japanese honeysuckle. Multiple broods through the year.

What it does here

As caterpillars, Eastern Giant Swallowtails are consumers of Rutaceae foliage and in turn serve as prey for birds, parasitoid wasps and flies, spiders, and predatory insects. The elaborate bird-dropping disguise and osmeterium reflect a long evolutionary arms race with those predators. As adults, they visit a wide variety of flowers and function as pollinators. They sit squarely in the middle of Florida's garden and woodland food web, connecting native host plants both upward to insectivorous birds and downward to leaf tissue.

The Eastern Giant Swallowtail is native to eastern North America, ranging from southern New England and the Great Lakes south through Florida, the Caribbean, and into Mexico. It is common throughout the entire state of Florida and is one of the most frequently seen large butterflies in the region. Florida and the deep South are the only parts of the eastern United States where the species can overwinter and remain active year-round. Populations once thought to extend west to South America are now recognized as a separate species, the Western Giant Swallowtail (Papilio rumiko), based on DNA evidence; the butterfly at Palma Sola is the eastern form.

There is something almost casual about the way this butterfly moves. Because of its large wingspan, it can glide surprisingly long distances on still wings between slow, powerful beats — a style of flight that sets it apart from most butterflies, which flicker and dart. When it drifts through a garden, it tends to hold your eye in a way that smaller species simply don't.

Host plant fidelity is the key to understanding where you'll find caterpillars. Females lay eggs on plants in the citrus and rue family (Rutaceae) — in Florida that means both ornamental citrus trees and native species like wild lime (Zanthoxylum fagara) and Hercules' club (Zanthoxylum clava-herculis).

The round eggs have a rough, orange-tinged coating that makes them look, fittingly, like tiny oranges.

The caterpillar's bird-dropping disguise is one of the animal kingdom's more convincing tricks. Young and mature larvae alike are patterned in glossy gray, brown, and white with a creamy saddle mark that completes the illusion at every stage. A caterpillar resting motionless on a citrus stem in full daylight is easy to walk right past — which is, of course, the point.

If the disguise fails, the caterpillar deploys a backup that is equally impressive. It everts the osmeterium — a Y-shaped, red-orange glandular organ — from a slit just behind the head. The organ floods the air with volatile acids, including isobutyric acid and 2-methylbutyric acid, producing a pungent, rancid-citrus odor that deters ants, wasps, and other invertebrate attackers.
