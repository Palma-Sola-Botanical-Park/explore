Eastern Giant Swallowtail

Heraclides cresphontes
On this page

At a glance

  • One of Florida's largest butterflies — broad dark wings crossed by bold yellow bands, and a habit of long gliding flight.
  • The caterpillar is a convincing bird-dropping mimic, mottled in brown, white and gray.
  • Citrus growers call the caterpillar an orangedog, because it feeds on citrus and its wild relatives.
  • Disturb one and it everts a forked orange organ from behind its head that gives off a strong smell.

How to know it

Size

A very large swallowtail, usually four to six inches across. Females often run slightly larger than males.

Upperside

Dark brown to black with two bold diagonal bands of yellow spots — one crossing the forewings, one sweeping across the hindwings — meeting near the body in a rough X. The tails are yellow-centred, with a small blue-and-red spot near the inner margin.

Underside

Largely yellow with black borders and blue scaling near the body, a complete reversal of the dark upperside.

In flight

Broad wings held flat, with long slow beats broken by extended glides.

Caterpillar

Brown, gray and white with a creamy saddle mark — a bird-dropping mimic at every stage.

Where to find it here

Watch flowering beds and sunny woodland edges for the adults. For caterpillars, check plants in the citrus family — citrus itself, wild lime and Hercules' club — where the larvae often rest right out in the open on a leaf or stem.

When

Adults fly through much of the year here, especially on warm sunny days. Broods overlap, so adults and caterpillars can both be around at once.

How it lives

A strong flier that spends most of its time gliding — with that wingspan it can cover ground without much flapping. Males run patrol routes through wooded edges and citrus groves looking for females, mostly in the afternoon.

A caterpillar dressed as bird droppings

Females lay eggs singly on the upper surface of a leaf. The caterpillar often rests exposed on a leaf or stem during the day, where its mottled pattern resembles a bird dropping, and feeds at night.

Multiple broods follow one another through the year in Florida, so adults and caterpillars can be present at the same time.

What it does here

The caterpillar can eat only a narrow group of host plants, nearly all in the citrus family. In Florida that means native wild lime and Hercules' club as well as cultivated citrus, and it is why the butterfly turns up wherever those plants grow. The adults are far less particular and will nectar at a wide range of flowers.

Both of the caterpillar's defenses are worth watching for. The bird-dropping disguise does the work while it sits still in daylight; if that fails it rears up and everts a forked orange osmeterium from behind the head, releasing a smell aimed at small predators. Neither is any threat to a person.

Females lay single orange eggs on the upper surface of a host leaf. The caterpillars feed at night and rest out in the open by day, trusting the disguise.
