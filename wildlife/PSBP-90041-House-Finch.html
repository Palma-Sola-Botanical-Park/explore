House Finch

Haemorhous mexicanus
On this page

At a glance

  • His color is a diary. A male's red, orange or yellow comes from pigments in the food he ate while his new feathers were growing, so it records his last molt, not today's lunch.
  • The eastern birds started as pets. Western House Finches were caught and sold as cage birds, and a release in New York in the early 1940s founded the population that spread across the East and on into Florida.
  • A seed cracker. It sits still and crushes seeds with quick bites of its thick bill, shelling them where it perches.
  • He sings on the wing. A courting male flutters straight up, singing, then glides back down.

How to know it

The male

Color on the face and upper breast, red in the classic bird, and blurry brown streaks down the flanks and belly. The bill is stout, with a curved upper outline, and the tail has only a shallow notch.

Orange and yellow count too

A yellow or orange male is still a House Finch. In an experiment with captive males, different diets gave pale yellow, orange or bright red replacement feathers. The color is laid into each feather as it grows, which is why a male can't change it until his next molt.

The female

She is brown and heavily streaked all over, with a fairly plain face and no color at all.

Not a cardinal

A male Northern Cardinal is red all over, with a tall crest and a heavy red-orange bill. A male House Finch has no crest, and his red covers the head, breast and rump, with brown streaks on the belly and sides.

How it lives

Courting with food

Pairs can start forming while the birds are still in winter flocks. The female pecks at the male's bill and flutters her wings, and he feeds her.

A nest almost anywhere

The female builds a cup of fine stems, leaves, rootlets and fibers with a softer lining. She puts it in a tree or shrub, on a ledge, in a hanging planter or a recess in a building. It is at home around people's buildings.

Seeds for the chicks

Even the young are raised on seeds: the parents swallow the seeds first and bring them back up to feed the nestlings.

Some go, some stay

As they spread east, these finches took up partial migration. The same bird may move south one winter and stay put the next.

Their own disease

In 1994, House Finches with swollen eyes turned up near Washington, D.C. It was the start of an eye disease outbreak that spread through the eastern birds. It can blur a finch's vision and make feeding hard.

What it does

Mostly a seed eater

Seeds, buds and fruit make up most of what it eats. Flocks often feed together on the ground, on seed-bearing stems and in fruiting trees.

Watch one at work and you can see the bill do it: a few fast bites, the shell falls away, and it reaches for the next seed.
