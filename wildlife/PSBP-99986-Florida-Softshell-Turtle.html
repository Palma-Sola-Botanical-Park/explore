Florida Softshell Turtle

Apalone ferox

At a glance

  • The Florida softshell turtle looks like no other turtle in the state — a flat, leathery, pancake-shaped reptile with a long, snorkel-like snout.
  • Its shell is not the hard dome you expect; it is soft and rubbery, covered in skin rather than hard plates.
  • The long snorkel nose lets it breathe at the surface while staying almost completely hidden underwater, just the nostrils poking up.
  • It is the largest softshell turtle in North America, and a powerful one: handled or cornered, it can deliver a serious bite, so it is strictly a look-but-don't-touch animal.

How to know it

Shell

Flat, round, and leathery rather than hard; olive to dark brown, often with darker blotches in young turtles.

Snout

Long and tube-like, with the nostrils at the tip.

Feet

Broadly webbed for swimming.

Size

Females grow much larger than males.

Voice

Essentially silent.

What to look for

A flat, pancake-shaped turtle with a pointed, snorkel-like nose, either basking on a bank or showing just its nostrils at the water's surface. The soft, leathery shell tells it apart instantly from the park's hard-shelled cooters and sliders.

Where to find it here

At the ponds — often only the pointed snout breaking the surface, or the whole turtle basking on a muddy bank. Give it room; it will slip away fast if disturbed.

Year-round resident. Most often seen surfacing to breathe or basking on warm days.

How it lives

It spends most of the day buried. A softshell settles into sand or mud in shallow water with only its eyes and that long snorkel of a snout showing, waits for something to come close, and takes it. Fish, snails, frogs, crayfish, aquatic insects, occasionally a small waterbird.

Females come ashore to dig a nest and lay, which is the one time you are likely to see the whole animal out of the water.

What it does here

A top turtle predator of Florida's ponds, helping control fish and invertebrate numbers, while its eggs are an important food for raccoons, birds, and other animals.

Native to Florida and the surrounding southeastern coastal plain. It is one of the most common large turtles in Florida's fresh waters.

Almost everything about the Florida softshell is built for an ambush life underwater. The flat, flexible shell and webbed feet make it a fast swimmer, and the long snout works like a snorkel: the turtle can rest on the bottom of a shallow pond and simply extend its nose to the surface to breathe, staying out of sight.

It is a hunter, not a grazer. The softshell eats fish, snails, frogs, crayfish, and other animals, and its long neck and strong jaws can strike with surprising speed and force.

That bite is the reason for caution. Florida softshells are not aggressive toward people in the water and pose no threat to someone simply watching, but a cornered or handled one can bite hard — a good reason to admire them and leave them be.

Take care

The risk

Harmless to people who keep their distance, but a cornered or handled softshell can bite hard with strong jaws and a fast, long-reaching neck. Never try to pick one up or block its path.

What to do

Look but do not touch. Do not handle, feed, or corner it. Observe from a comfortable distance and let it move freely.
