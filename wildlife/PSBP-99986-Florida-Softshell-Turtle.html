Florida Softshell Turtle

Apalone ferox
On this page

At a glance

  • A broad, flattened turtle with a leathery shell and a long tubular snout.
  • The flat shell and heavily webbed feet make it a fast swimmer — nothing like the domed pond turtles.
  • The long nose lets it breathe at the surface with the rest of the body still underwater.
  • Females grow much larger than males, and a big one has a long neck and powerful jaws.

How to know it

Shell

Broad, flat and leathery, without the hard plates that form most turtles' shells.

Snout

Long and narrow, ending in a small pair of nostrils.

Feet

Large and strongly webbed.

Size

Large females can have shells more than a foot and a half long. Males stay much smaller.

In the water you may see nothing but the nose. On land the flat leathery shell separates it instantly from the park's cooters and sliders.

Easily confused with

Peninsular Cooter — Has a hard, domed shell with yellow markings and a conventionally shaped turtle head. The Florida Softshell is much flatter, with a leathery shell and long tubular nose.

Florida Red-bellied Cooter — Also a large pond turtle, but has a hard shell and a striped head. The softshell's flat leathery shell and long nose separate it at close range.

Pond Slider — Smaller and hard-shelled, usually with a colored patch behind the eye. Softshells lack the hard shell plates and have a much longer snout.

Where to find it here

Freshwater ponds and muddy or sandy shorelines. Softshells spend most of their time in the water and often bury themselves in soft bottom sediment with only the head showing, so the usual sighting is a nose at the surface rather than a turtle up on a log.

Present year-round. Most often noticed when one surfaces to breathe or comes out onto land.

How it lives

Most of its life is spent in water. The flattened body and webbed feet make it a strong swimmer, and it can bury itself in soft bottom sediment while waiting for prey or hiding.

Coming ashore

Females leave the water to lay eggs on land, so a softshell seen crossing open ground may be moving between water and a nesting site.

What it does here

Unlike the cooters it shares the pond with, this one is a hunter — snails, crayfish, aquatic insects, fish, and whatever carrion turns up. The flat body and webbed feet make it fast enough to run prey down, and it can also lie buried in the bottom and wait.

Plants matter to it at one remove. Beds of aquatic vegetation and shoreline cover hold the fish and crayfish it hunts, while bare muddy or sandy openings along the bank give females somewhere to leave the water and nest.

Take care

The risk

Do not handle a Florida Softshell. It has powerful jaws and a long, flexible neck that can reach surprisingly far around the sides of the shell, and a defensive bite can cause a serious wound.

What to do

Watch from a distance and never pick one up. If one is crossing a path, give it room to keep going the way it was already heading, and keep dogs from cornering it.
