Scarlet Skimmer

Crocothemis servilia
On this page

At a glance

  • On hot days, a perched Scarlet Skimmer may point its abdomen almost straight at the sun. Aiming the tip skyward reduces the amount of its body exposed to direct sunlight.
  • Mature males become vivid red, while females and young males are yellowish to olive-brown. A young male can therefore look more like a female than like the red adult he will become.
  • Scarlet Skimmers are native to Asia, not Florida. They were first recorded in the Miami area in the 1970s and are now established across much of the Florida peninsula.
  • Both sexes have amber-orange coloring at the bases of the wings. It is especially useful when identifying the yellow-brown females and immature males.
  • Before becoming an aerial hunter, a dragonfly lives underwater as a nymph. Scarlet Skimmer nymphs prey on small aquatic animals before climbing out of the water for their final molt.

How to know it

Easily confused with

Roseate Skimmer — Roseate Skimmer males are pink-purple rather than scarlet red. Needham's Skimmers are more orange. Females and immature males can be harder to separate from other brownish skimmers than the bright red adult male.

Size and shape

A medium-sized dragonfly about an inch and a half long, with a sturdy body and broad, clear wings.

Male

Mature males are bright red across most of the body, with reddish eyes and a thin dark line along the top of the abdomen. The wing bases are washed with amber-orange.

Female and immature male

Females and immature males are yellowish to olive-brown rather than red. Look for the dark line along the abdomen and amber-orange coloring near the wing bases.

Similar species

Roseate Skimmer males are pink-purple rather than scarlet red. Needham's Skimmers are generally more orange. Females and immature Scarlet Skimmers are harder to identify than mature males, so the wing-base color and abdominal markings are useful.

Size

About an inch and a half long.

What to look for

Look around sunny pond edges for a medium-sized dragonfly repeatedly returning to a low perch. Mature males are bright red; females and young males are yellow-brown. Amber-orange coloring at the wing bases is useful in both sexes.

Where to find it here

Look around sunny ponds, drainage areas and other still or slow-moving freshwater. Males often choose a low stem near the shoreline, make short flights over the water and return to the same perch.

Most active on warm, sunny days. Florida's long warm season allows adults to be seen through much of the year, but local seasonal timing at Palma Sola is not yet well established.

When

Most likely on warm, sunny days. There are not yet enough local observations to define a reliable seasonal peak at Palma Sola.

How it lives

Males defend stretches of shoreline from low perches, making short patrol flights and often returning to the same stem.

Adults catch small flying insects in the air, using their spiny legs to help trap prey. The aquatic nymphs hunt small animals among submerged vegetation.

Females lay eggs at the water, touching the tip of the abdomen to the surface as they fly.

What it does here

Native to Asia and introduced to Florida, where it was first recorded in the Miami area in the 1970s. It is now established through much of the Florida peninsula and readily uses ponds, canals and other human-made waters.

Scarlet Skimmers are predators at both stages of life. Adults catch flying insects; aquatic nymphs eat small animals underwater. In turn, dragonflies and their nymphs are eaten by fish, birds and other predators.

Waterside plants provide perches for adults and cover for aquatic nymphs. Males often choose exposed stems for territorial perches, while submerged and emergent vegetation creates hunting habitat for the young underwater.

Introduced and established in Florida. It is not currently a major management target or listed among Florida's principal invasive species.
