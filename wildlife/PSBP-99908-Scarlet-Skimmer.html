Scarlet Skimmer

Crocothemis servilia

At a glance

  • Only mature males earn the name. Young males and females are a muted yellow-brown — the vivid blood-red develops gradually as a male matures, signaling to females and rival males alike that he is ready to compete.
  • Florida got this species by accident. The most likely pathway was the aquatic plant or aquarium trade; the first confirmed U.S. sighting was near Miami in 1975, and the species has spread steadily across the southern half of the peninsula since then.
  • On a scorching summer day, watch for a perched scarlet skimmer to tilt its abdomen straight up toward the sun — a move called the obelisk posture. By pointing the tip of the abdomen at the sun, the dragonfly minimizes the body surface exposed to direct solar radiation and avoids overheating during midday territory patrols.
  • The orange patches at the base of all four wings act like tiny built-in stained-glass windows. They appear in every individual regardless of age or sex, making them the single most reliable field mark for separating this species from similar-looking native skimmers.

How to know it

Size and shape

A medium-sized skimmer dragonfly; males run about 1.6 inches in body length. The build is fairly robust for a skimmer, with a broad thorax and a moderately tapered abdomen. Wings are held flat and outstretched at rest, as is typical of the family.

Male

Unmistakable when mature: the entire body — thorax, abdomen, and tail appendages — is blood-red. The eyes are red above and purplish on the sides. A thin dark line runs along the top of the abdomen. Orange-amber veining is visible near the base of all four wings.

Female and immature male

Olive-brown to yellowish overall, with a pale stripe running along the top of the thorax. The same orange wing-base veining seen in males is present and is one of the most reliable field marks for the species regardless of sex or age. The abdomen has a dark dorsal line.

Similar species

The roseate skimmer male is pink rather than red, and lacks the dark dorsal stripe. Needham's skimmer males are more orange-yellow than red. The orange wing-base veining combined with the pale thorax stripe is the best combination mark for females and immatures, separating them from similar-looking species.

Voice

Essentially silent as far as human observers are concerned. Dragonflies produce no true vocalizations; the faint dry rustle of wings in flight is the only sound this species makes.

Size

Males approximately 1.6 inches; females similar in length

What to look for

On a mature male, the all-red body — including the eyes — is conclusive. For females and young males, look first at the wing bases: the orange veining near the base of all four wings, paired with a pale stripe down the thorax, distinguishes this species from most lookalikes. Both sexes tend to perch low on emergent vegetation at the water's edge and return repeatedly to the same stem.

Where to find it here

Check any sunny open shoreline, especially where low emergent vegetation — grasses, sedges, or low shrubs — grows right at the water's edge. Males will be perched conspicuously at eye level or lower, making short territorial sallies and returning to the same stem. Working slowly along a pond margin on a warm sunny morning is the most reliable approach.

Active during daylight hours on warm days; like most dragonflies, they are most visible during the warmer months and become less active during the coolest parts of winter. In Manatee County, adults are present for a long season — roughly spring through fall — with peak abundance in summer. Cool or overcast days significantly reduce activity.

When

Adults reliably establish territorial waters from April through October in Manatee County, with peak abundance and activity in the summer months. Activity drops sharply on cool or overcast days and slows considerably in winter, though Florida's mild climate may allow some individuals to persist into the cooler months in the southernmost parts of the state. Peak visibility is on warm, sunny mornings in summer.

How it lives

Males pick a low perch on waterside vegetation and patrol a stretch of shoreline against rivals, returning to the same perch after every sortie — which makes them unusually easy to watch.

Adults hunt in the air, catching mosquitoes, midges, gnats and small flies with their legs held out as a basket. The aquatic young ambush small invertebrates among submerged plants.

Females come to the water only to mate and lay, dipping the tip of the abdomen to the surface. Both sexes bask in open sun to reach the temperature they need to fly and hunt.

What it does here

The scarlet skimmer is native to East and Southeast Asia, ranging from Japan and China through Southeast Asia to northern Australia — one of the most widespread odonate species on that side of the globe. It arrived in Florida accidentally, most likely via the aquatic plant or aquarium trade, and was first recorded near Miami in 1975. Since then it has become common across the southern half of the Florida peninsula, with central Florida representing roughly its northern limit; no self-sustaining population has established farther north than the Florida-Georgia line. It has also spread from Florida to Jamaica, Cuba, and other Caribbean islands. In the continental United States it is found only in Florida and Hawaii.

Adults are efficient predators of small flying insects, including pest species such as mosquitoes and biting midges — a real benefit in Florida's wetland-rich landscape. Nymphs fill an equivalent role in the water column, thinning populations of aquatic invertebrates. At the same time, both life stages are prey: nymphs are taken by fish and wading birds, and adults are caught by flycatchers, kestrels, and other aerial predators. Because their nymph stage is aquatic and sensitive to water quality, dragonflies like this one also serve as informal indicators of wetland health.

The scarlet skimmer is an introduced species in Florida, not native to the Americas. It is not listed on Florida's primary invasive species lists and is not an active management target. Current evidence suggests it occupies a role similar to native skimmers without obvious displacement effects, but it is a non-native and is treated as such here.

The color difference between the sexes is striking enough that you might not guess they belong to the same species. Mature males are an intense blood-red from the eyes to the tip of the abdomen — even the eyes are red above and purplish on the sides.

Females and young males wear a quieter olive-brown and yellow, and share only the orange wing-base veining and the pale thorax stripe with their red counterparts.

That drab coloring in young males is not a disadvantage — it is a strategy. Territorial adult males tend to ignore dull-colored individuals that don't look like competitors, giving young males time to mature and gain strength before they have to defend a perch of their own. When the deep red finally develops, there is no mistaking the message.

Females lay eggs directly in or at the surface of the water, and the aquatic nymphs that hatch are every bit as predatory as the adults. They stalk small aquatic invertebrates through pond vegetation, molting repeatedly over months to years before crawling out of the water for the last time to emerge as adults.
