Great Egret

Ardea alba
On this page

At a glance

  • A Great Egret is a tall, all-white heron with a long yellow bill and black legs and feet.
  • It often hunts by doing almost nothing: standing motionless in shallow water until a fish comes within striking distance.
  • During breeding season, adults grow long ornamental plumes on the back. Demand for those feathers in the hat trade devastated egret populations in the late 1800s and helped spur some of America's earliest bird-protection campaigns.
  • Great Egrets nest in colonies, building stick nests in trees and shrubs, often over water and alongside other herons and egrets.

How to know it

Body

Entirely white, with a very long neck and long black legs.

Bill

Long, straight and usually yellow.

Feet

Black. This immediately separates an adult Great Egret from a Snowy Egret, which has yellow feet.

Breeding season

Long white plumes grow from the back, and the bare skin between the bill and eye becomes brighter green.

Size

About three feet tall, with a wingspan around four and a half to five and a half feet.

What to look for

Look for a tall, slender white heron with a yellow bill and black legs. It often stands motionless in shallow water with its neck partly drawn in, then strikes suddenly at prey.

Easily confused with

Snowy Egret — Much smaller, with a black bill, black legs and bright yellow feet.

Little Blue Heron — Young Little Blue Herons are also white, but smaller, with dull greenish legs and a pale bill tipped with black.

Great Blue Heron — The rare white form is larger and heavier-bodied with paler legs. An ordinary Great Egret has black legs.

Wood Stork — Wood Storks are white with broad black flight feathers, a bare dark head and a heavy downcurved bill. A Great Egret is all white with a straight yellow bill and a feathered head.

Where to find it here

Look along pond edges and shallow water. Great Egrets often hunt alone, either standing motionless or walking slowly along the shoreline.

Present in Florida year-round and an occasional visitor to Palma Sola's ponds — recorded by several observers, though not on every visit.

How it lives

It hunts in shallow water by standing still or walking slowly, then drives the long bill forward when fish or other prey come within reach.

Fish make up much of the diet, along with frogs, crustaceans, insects, reptiles and other small animals.

Nesting in company

Great Egrets nest colonially in trees and shrubs, often over water and often alongside other wading birds.

What it does here

A predator of fish and other small wetland animals. Great Egrets also concentrate in mixed nesting colonies that become important feeding sites for predators and scavengers.

Trees make the colony

Trees and shrubs provide the structure for nesting colonies. Great Egrets build stick nests in woody vegetation, often over water; documented nest plants include live oak, wax myrtle, mangroves and buttonwood. Along the shoreline, aquatic and emergent plants also create cover for the fish and other prey the birds hunt.

Take care

No meaningful danger when watched at a normal distance. Do not handle an injured or trapped egret; its pointed bill can cause serious injury.

Watch from a distance and do not feed it. If the bird stops feeding, retreats or flies away as you approach, give it more space.

Conservation

Least Concern today. Great Egrets were nearly eliminated from parts of North America by hunting for the plume trade in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Legal protection allowed populations to recover.
