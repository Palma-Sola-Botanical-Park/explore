Great Egret

Ardea alba
On this page

At a glance

  • A tall, all-white heron with a long yellow bill and black legs and feet.
  • In breeding season it grows long, lacy plumes from its back.
  • It hunts by doing almost nothing: standing still or wading slowly until a fish is within reach.
  • The National Audubon Society, a bird-protection group, chose a flying Great Egret as its symbol in 1953.

How to know it

Tall and white

A tall, slender white heron with a long yellow bill and black legs. It stands about three feet tall, with a wingspan of four and a half to five and a half feet. A Snowy Egret is smaller, with a black bill and yellow feet.

Where to find it here

Pond edges

Look along pond edges and in shallow water. Great Egrets often hunt alone, standing motionless or walking slowly along the shoreline. They stay in Florida all year.

How it lives

Doing almost nothing

It stands still or walks slowly through shallow water, then drives the long bill forward when a fish comes within reach. Frogs, crustaceans, insects and small reptiles go the same way.

What it does

The plumes

In breeding season long white plumes grow from the back, and the bare skin between the bill and the eye turns brighter green.

What people did for them

In the late 1800s those plumes were sold for women's hats. Plume hunters killed so many egrets that the species was nearly eliminated in the United States, and the outcry helped start some of the country's first bird-protection campaigns. The National Audubon Society later chose a flying Great Egret as its symbol, in 1953. The bird wading at the pond edge is that recovery.
