Gulf Fritillary

Dione vanillae

At a glance

  • The gulf fritillary is a bright orange butterfly with black markings above and an underside scattered with brilliant, mirror-like silver spots.
  • Like the zebra longwing, it depends entirely on passionflower vines — the two butterflies share the same host plant.
  • Its caterpillars are showy too: bright orange with rows of soft-looking black spines that look fierce but do not sting.
  • In late summer and fall, large numbers move south into the Florida peninsula, a seasonal drift that brings them into gardens in good numbers.

How to know it

Upper side

Bright orange with black spots and markings.

Underside

Brown with elongated, iridescent silver-white spots.

Shape

Somewhat long-winged, like its longwing relatives.

Size

2.5 to 3.7 inches

What to look for

A bright orange butterfly nectaring in the open; when it closes its wings, look for the unmistakable rows of shiny silver spots underneath. The caterpillar is orange with branching black spines, found on passionflower vines.

Easily confused with

Monarch — The monarch is broader-winged with heavy black veins and no silver; the gulf fritillary is narrower with bright silver spots on the underside.

Where to find it here

In sunny, flowery spots, nectaring with wings closed to show the silver underside. Check passionflower vines for the spiny orange caterpillars. Most numerous in late summer and fall.

Year-round resident, most numerous during the fall southward movement into Florida.

How it lives

Active in sunny open ground, through several generations a year, and in fall it joins a general southward movement down the Florida peninsula.

Caterpillars eat passionflower vines and nothing else. Adults nectar widely — lantana, Spanish needles and other open blooms — usually with the wings held closed, flashing that silver underside.

What it does here

A common garden pollinator and, with the zebra longwing, a passionflower specialist — together they make native passionvine a high-value plant for supporting butterflies.

Caterpillars feed only on passionflower vines (Passiflora spp.) — the same host as the zebra longwing — so one native passionvine planting supports both butterflies.

Native to the southern United States and the Americas. In Florida it is a common, year-round resident, with numbers rising in fall as butterflies move south down the peninsula.

The gulf fritillary is one of the most familiar orange butterflies in Florida gardens, and despite the name it belongs to the longwing group rather than the true fritillaries.

Its caterpillars feed only on passionflower vines — the very same Passiflora the zebra longwing uses — so a single planting of native passionvine can support both of Florida's beloved longwings at once. The caterpillars take in the vine's compounds, which makes them and the adults unpalatable to many predators.

The undersides of its wings are the real surprise: long, gleaming silver-white spots that flash like little mirrors when the butterfly basks or nectars with its wings closed.
