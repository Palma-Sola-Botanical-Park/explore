Blue Jay

Cyanocitta cristata
On this page

At a glance

  • A crested blue, white, and black bird with a loud, ringing call is a blue jay — one of the boldest and brainiest birds in the park.
  • The blue is a trick of the light. Its feathers contain no blue pigment; tiny structures scatter light to make the color, so a crushed feather looks plain brown.
  • It is a champion oak-planter. A single jay can cache thousands of acorns each fall, and the ones it never retrieves grow into new oak trees.
  • It is also a skilled mimic, copying the scream of a red-shouldered hawk — handy for scaring other birds off a feeder.

How to know it

Color

Blue above, white to pale gray below, with a black necklace across the chest.

Wings and tail

Barred with black and white and tipped with white.

Crest

A pointed blue crest that rises and lowers with the bird's mood.

Behavior cue

Loud, bold, and often in small noisy groups.

Voice

Loud and varied: a ringing "jay-jay" scream, a musical "queedle" like a squeaky gate, and dead-on imitations of hawk calls.

Size

9 to 12 inches; 13 to 17 inches

What to look for

A crested blue-and-white bird, larger than a cardinal, calling loudly from the trees or visiting feeders. The black necklace and white-tipped wings and tail confirm it.

Where to find it here

Around the park's oaks and larger trees, often announcing itself with loud calls before you spot the flash of blue.

Year-round resident, conspicuous in every season and especially busy caching acorns in fall.

How it lives

Active by day, social, and noisy, often moving in family groups. Bold at feeders and quick to mob hawks, owls, and cats as a group. Raises and lowers its crest to signal alarm or excitement.

Mainly acorns, nuts, and seeds, plus insects, fruits, and the occasional egg or nestling. Caches large numbers of acorns for winter.

What it does here

A key seed disperser, especially of oaks, and a sharp-eyed alarm system whose calls alert other animals to predators.

Closely tied to oaks and other nut trees — it eats their acorns and, by burying and forgetting them, plants the next generation of trees.

Native to eastern and central North America. In Florida it is a common year-round resident of woodlands, parks, and leafy neighborhoods, especially where oaks grow.

Blue jays are members of the crow family, and it shows in their intelligence. They are curious, social, and quick to learn, with a wide vocabulary of calls and a knack for problem-solving.

They are also unsung foresters. In autumn a jay gathers acorns and other nuts and buries them across its territory for winter — far more than it can ever eat. The forgotten caches sprout, and over centuries jays have helped oaks spread across the continent, a quiet partnership written into whole forests.

That famous blue is structural, not pigmented. Microscopic structures in the feathers scatter light so we see blue, which is why a backlit or crushed feather loses the color entirely. Jays round out their reputation with mimicry, especially convincing imitations of hawk calls that can clear rivals away from food.

Take care

What to do

Watch and enjoy, and please do not feed park wildlife. Jays are confident enough to approach on their own — no encouragement needed.
