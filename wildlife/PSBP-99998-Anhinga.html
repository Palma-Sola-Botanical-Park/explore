Anhinga

Anhinga anhinga
On this page

At a glance

  • A long-necked, long-tailed waterbird with a straight, pointed bill. Males are black with silver streaks on the wings; females and young have a pale tan neck.
  • Its feathers soak through and its bones are dense, so it rides low, with only the neck and head above water. That is where the name snakebird comes from.
  • Underwater it stalks fish slowly, then thrusts its bill through the side of one.
  • Once its feathers dry, it can soar high on rising warm air.

How to know it

Neck, bill and tail

A very long, slender neck, a long, straight bill with a sharp point, and a long, fan-shaped tail. The neck is often the only part of a swimming bird you see. The wingspan is nearly four feet.

Male and female

Adult males are black with silvery-white streaks on the wings and back. Females and young birds have a pale tan head, neck and breast.

Where to find it here

Calm water, then branches

Scan calm pond water for a bird swimming with its body mostly submerged. Then check the branches, snags and banks around the water for birds drying their wings. The sunny side of a pond is where we see them most often, wings spread wide.

All year

It stays in Florida all year.

How it lives

Low in the water

Most waterbirds float high on waterproofed feathers. An Anhinga's body plumage soaks through and its bones are dense, so it rides low and can sink at will. That lets it hunt underwater. The price is that it has to dry out afterward.

Stalking

Underwater it swims slowly and stalks fish rather than chasing them. It strikes by thrusting its partly open bill through the side of a fish.

Drying

Afterward it climbs onto a branch or a bank and holds its wings open. A bird perched with its wings spread is the posture to look for. Once dry, it can leave the perch and soar high on rising warm air, so check the sky as well as the water.

What it does here

The sequence to watch

At a pond the order goes like this: a neck moves along the surface with no body behind it, then a dark bird climbs out onto a snag, then the wings open. If you see only the second or third act, look at the water nearby for the first.
