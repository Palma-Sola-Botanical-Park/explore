Anhinga

Anhinga anhinga
On this page

At a glance

  • An Anhinga often swims with its body submerged and only the long snakelike neck above water, which is why it is called the snakebird.
  • Its feathers become waterlogged more easily than those of most waterbirds. Combined with dense bones, that reduces buoyancy and helps the bird slip below the surface to hunt.
  • Underwater, an Anhinga stalks fish and drives its sharp bill through the side of the prey with a rapid thrust.
  • After fishing, Anhingas often perch with their wings spread, drying waterlogged feathers before flying again.

How to know it

Bill

Long, straight and sharply pointed.

Neck

Very long and slender, often the most visible part of a swimming bird.

Plumage

Adult males are black with silvery-white streaks on the wings and back. Females and young have a pale tan head, neck and breast.

Tail

Long and fan-shaped, extending conspicuously behind the body.

Size

Wingspan about three and a half feet.

What to look for

Look for a long-necked waterbird swimming extremely low or perched with its wings spread. The straight daggerlike bill and long fan-shaped tail separate it from the Double-crested Cormorant.

Easily confused with

Double-crested Cormorant — Cormorants are heavier-bodied, with shorter tails and bills that hook at the tip. Anhingas are slimmer, with long fan-shaped tails and straight daggerlike bills.

Where to find it here

Scan calm pond water for a swimming bird with its body mostly submerged, then check branches, snags and banks around the water for birds drying their wings.

A year-round Florida resident and regularly recorded at Palma Sola. Look whenever the ponds are active; perched birds with spread wings are especially conspicuous.

When

Recorded repeatedly at Palma Sola over multiple years.

How it lives

Underwater, the Anhinga swims slowly around submerged vegetation and stalks fish rather than chasing them at high speed. It usually strikes by thrusting its partly opened bill through the side of a fish.

After fishing, it often climbs onto a branch or bank and spreads its wings while the waterlogged feathers dry. Once airborne, it can soar high on rising warm air.

What it does here

A specialized predator of small and medium-sized fish in shallow wetlands.

Anhingas depend on waterside vegetation in two ways. Submerged plants provide structure around which fish can hide and be stalked, while trees and shrubs over or beside the water provide drying perches and nest sites. Nests are bulky stick platforms that may be lined with fresh leaves and green twigs.

Native to the warmer Americas. In Florida it is a year-round resident of shallow ponds, lakes, marshes and slow waterways, with occasional use of brackish coastal water.
