At a glance

  • Watch the water for a slim black bird swimming with only its snakelike neck above the surface — that is the anhinga, and the pose earns it the nickname snakebird.
  • Unlike ducks, the anhinga's feathers are not waterproof, and that turns out to be the key to how it hunts.
  • Waterlogged feathers make the bird less buoyant, so it can sink low and stalk fish beneath the surface instead of bobbing on top.
  • Because it gets soaked to the skin, you will often see one afterward perched with its wings spread wide like a cape, drying out in the sun.

How to know it

Easily confused with

Double-crested Cormorant — The cormorant has a hook-tipped bill and a shorter tail; the anhinga's bill is straight and dagger-like, its tail long and fan-shaped, and it shows silvery patches on the wings.

Bill

Long, straight, and sharply pointed — built for spearing, not grabbing.

Neck

Very long and thin, often the only part visible while swimming.

Plumage

Males are glossy black with silver-white streaks on the wings; females and young have a pale tan head and neck.

Tail

Long and fan-shaped, longer than a cormorant's.

Voice

Usually silent. Around nesting colonies it makes clicking, rattling, and croaking sounds.

Size

30 to 37 inches; about 3.7 feet (45 inches)

What to look for

A long-necked black bird either swimming low with just its head and neck showing, or perched with wings fully spread to dry. Pale-necked birds are females and juveniles. Compared with the similar cormorant, the anhinga has a straight, pointed bill (the cormorant's is hooked) and a longer tail.

Where to find it here

On and around the ponds. Scan the open water for a swimming snakebird, and check waterside branches, posts, and snags for one drying its wings in the sun.

Year-round resident. Often easiest to spot in the morning when birds are perched and drying their wings.

How it lives

Active by day. Hunts by swimming submerged and ambushing fish, then perches in the open to dry its wings and warm up. A strong soaring flier, it often rides thermals high overhead. Nests in colonies, frequently alongside herons and egrets.

Mainly fish — larger ones speared on its sharp bill, smaller ones gripped in the serrated edges — plus the occasional aquatic invertebrate or small amphibian.

What it does here

A specialized fish predator that helps keep fish populations in balance. By nesting in mixed colonies with herons, egrets, and ibises, it is part of the larger wading-bird community that depends on healthy wetlands.

Native to the warm wetlands of the Americas, from the southeastern United States through Central and South America. In the U.S. it is a year-round resident across Florida and the Gulf Coast, favoring fresh and brackish water with plenty of fish and perches.

The anhinga earns its snakebird nickname honestly. As it swims, its body rides below the surface and only the long, thin neck shows, giving it a serpentine look that has fooled many a passerby.

Most water birds keep their feathers waterproof with oils from a special gland. The anhinga does not — its feathers wet through, and combined with unusually dense bones, that lets it settle to near-neutral buoyancy and slip quietly underwater to stalk fish, rather than floating high like a duck.

When it strikes, the anhinga's neck does something remarkable. A hinge-like joint between the eighth and ninth neck bones acts like a coiled spring, snapping the head forward to spear a fish on its sharp bill. Larger fish are speared; smaller ones are gripped in the finely serrated bill. The bird then surfaces, tosses the catch into the air, and swallows it head-first.

Because its feathers soak through, the anhinga loses body heat in the water and cannot fly well while wet — which is why it spends so much time perched with wings spread, drying and warming in the sun.

Take care

What to do

Enjoy from a distance and please do not feed it or any park wildlife. Give perched, wing-drying birds space — pushing one off its sunning spot makes it harder for the bird to warm up.
