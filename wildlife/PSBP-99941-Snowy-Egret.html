Snowy Egret

Egretta thula
On this page
At a glanceHow to know itWhere to find it hereHow it livesWhat it does here

At a glance

  • A medium white egret with a black bill, black legs and bright yellow feet — as if it were wearing golden slippers.
  • The yellow feet are a hunting tool: it shuffles them through the shallows to stir prey into moving.
  • Smaller and far more active than a Great Egret, dashing through the shallows rather than standing still.

How to know it

Feet

Bright yellow — the golden slippers, and the single best field mark.

Bill and legs

Both black, the bill slender and sharp.

Size

Medium, around two feet tall — noticeably smaller than a Great Egret.

The other giveaway is temperament. This is the white egret that will not stand still.

Easily confused with

Great Egret — Great Egrets are considerably larger, with a yellow bill and black feet, and they hunt by standing still. The Snowy Egret has a black bill, yellow feet and rarely stops moving.

Little Blue Heron — Young Little Blue Herons are also white but have dull greenish legs and a pale bill with a dark tip, and they feed slowly rather than dashing about.

Where to find it here

Work the shallow edges — pond margins and wet flats where the water is only a few inches deep.

Present year-round, turning up occasionally rather than on every visit.

How it lives

The busiest of the egrets. Where a Great Egret stands still and waits, this one runs, stops, stirs the bottom with a bright yellow foot to flush something out, and stabs at whatever moves.

Small fish, shrimp, crabs and insects. The foot-shuffling is the giveaway — no other white heron here feeds like that.

What it does here

The yellow feet are a tool, not decoration. The egret shuffles one foot through the silt to panic small fish and shrimp out of cover, then strikes at whatever breaks. It works the vegetated shallows for the same reason — stems and roots are where the prey is hiding.

It nests in colonies in trees and shrubs standing over or beside water, out of reach of anything hunting from the ground. A colony packs many birds into a few trees, which draws its own crowd of predators and scavengers.

A century ago the species was nearly finished. Breeding birds grow fine recurved plumes that were worth more than their weight in gold to the hat trade of the late 1800s, and the campaign to stop the killing became one of the founding causes of the Audubon movement.
