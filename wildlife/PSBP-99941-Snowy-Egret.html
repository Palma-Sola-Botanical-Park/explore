Snowy Egret

Egretta thula
On this page
At a glanceHow to know itWhere to find it hereHow it livesWhat it does here

At a glance

  • A medium white egret with a black bill, black legs, and bright yellow feet — as if it is wearing golden slippers.
  • The bright yellow feet are a feeding tool: it shuffles them through the water to stir up small fish and shrimp, then strikes.
  • A century ago it was nearly wiped out for its lacy breeding plumes, just like the great egret — and its recovery helped fuel the early Audubon movement.
  • Smaller and more active than the great egret, it dashes and darts through the shallows rather than standing still.

How to know it

Feet

Bright yellow — the 'golden slippers.' The single best field mark.

Bill

Black and slender.

Legs

Black.

Size

Medium — about 24 inches, smaller than the great egret.

Voice

Generally quiet; some harsh squawks at the colony.

Size

22 to 26 inches; about 3.3 feet (39 inches)

What to look for

A medium white egret actively running through shallows. Check the feet: if they are yellow, it is a snowy egret.

Easily confused with

Great Egret — Great Egrets are considerably larger, with a yellow bill and black feet, and they hunt by standing still. The Snowy Egret has a black bill, yellow feet and rarely stops moving.

Little Blue Heron — Young Little Blue Herons are also white but have dull greenish legs and a pale bill with a dark tip, and they feed slowly rather than dashing about.

Where to find it here

Work the shallow edges — pond margins, wet flats, anywhere the water is only a few inches deep. Look for the egret that will not stand still. If the feet are yellow, it is this one.

Year-round resident.

How it lives

The busiest of the egrets. Where a Great Egret stands still and waits, this one runs, stops, stirs the bottom with a bright yellow foot to flush something out, and stabs at whatever moves.

Small fish, shrimp, crabs and insects. The foot-shuffling is the giveaway — no other white heron here feeds like that.

Nesting together

Snowy Egrets nest in colonies, often mixed with other herons and egrets, building in trees and shrubs standing over water.

What it does here

A predator of small fish, shrimp and aquatic insects in shallow water. Its nesting colonies concentrate many birds in a few trees, which in turn attracts predators and scavengers.

Wetland plants matter at both ends of the day. The egret hunts along the vegetated shallows where small fish and shrimp shelter among stems and roots, and it nests colonially in trees and shrubs standing over or beside water, where the nests are hard for ground predators to reach.

Native to the Americas. Year-round resident in Florida.

The plume trade nearly ended this species. Breeding birds grow fine recurved feathers that were worth more than their weight in gold to the hat trade in the late 1800s, and the campaign to stop the killing became one of the founding causes of the Audubon movement.
