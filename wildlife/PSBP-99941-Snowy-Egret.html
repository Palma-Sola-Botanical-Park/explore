Snowy Egret

Egretta thula
On this page
At a glanceHow to know itWhere to find it hereHow it livesWhat it does here

At a glance

  • A medium white egret with a black bill, black legs, and bright yellow feet — as if it is wearing golden slippers.
  • The bright yellow feet are a feeding tool: it shuffles them through the water to stir up small fish and shrimp, then strikes.
  • A century ago it was nearly wiped out for its lacy breeding plumes, just like the great egret — and its recovery helped fuel the early Audubon movement.
  • Smaller and more active than the great egret, it dashes and darts through the shallows rather than standing still.

How to know it

Feet

Bright yellow — the 'golden slippers.' The single best field mark.

Bill

Black and slender.

Legs

Black.

Size

Medium — about 24 inches, smaller than the great egret.

Voice

Generally quiet; some harsh squawks at the colony.

Size

22 to 26 inches; about 3.3 feet (39 inches)

What to look for

A medium white egret actively running through shallows. Check the feet: if they are yellow, it is a snowy egret.

Where to find it here

Pond edges and shallows, often actively running and shuffling. Look for the yellow feet.

Year-round resident.

How it lives

Active, energetic hunter — runs, shuffles feet, and strikes rapidly. More animated than other egrets.

Small fish, shrimp, crabs, insects, and aquatic invertebrates — often flushed with foot-shuffling.

What it does here

Predator of small aquatic animals; historically a flagship for the conservation movement.

Forages in wetland vegetation; nests colonially in trees near water.

Native to the Americas. Year-round resident in Florida.

The snowy egret is the energetic counterpart to the patient great egret. Where the great egret stands motionless and waits, the snowy runs, hops, and shuffles its feet to flush prey — a distinctly different hunting style.

The golden-yellow feet are the single best field mark. In breeding plumage, it also grows delicate, recurved plumes on its back, head, and chest — the same plumes that made it a target for the millinery trade in the late 1800s.

Its recovery is one of the early triumphs of the conservation movement, though more recently its numbers in some areas have declined again with wetland loss.
