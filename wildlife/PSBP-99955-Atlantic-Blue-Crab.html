At a glance

  • A swimming crab with sapphire-blue claws turning up in a park pond is the Atlantic blue crab — and its presence is a clue that the water is brackish, not pure fresh.
  • Its scientific name, Callinectes sapidus, means "savory beautiful swimmer," and it lives up to all three words.
  • The back pair of legs are flattened into paddles, letting it swim sideways through the water rather than only crawl.
  • Tip for telling males from females: females have red-tipped claws, often described as wearing "painted fingernails."

How to know it

Color

Olive to grayish body with bright blue legs and claws; mature females have red-tipped claws.

Claws

Strong pincers, blue in males, red-tipped in females.

Back legs

Flattened into swimming paddles.

Shell

Broad, with a sharp spine on each side.

Voice

Silent.

What to look for

A broad crab with blue-tinged claws and paddle-shaped back legs in or near the water. Red-tipped "painted" claws mark a female; all-blue claws mark a male.

Where to find it here

In and around the ponds if the water carries any brackish influence. Scan the shallows and edges for a crab with blue claws.

Possible whenever conditions suit; most active in warm water.

How it lives

Active in and around the water, swimming with its paddle legs and foraging on the bottom. Defends itself readily with strong pincers when cornered. Burrows into mud to overwinter and to molt.

An omnivore — clams, snails, small fish, plants, detritus, and dead animals — scavenged and hunted along the bottom.

What it does here

A wide-ranging omnivore and scavenger that helps clean and recycle material in estuarine and brackish waters, and an important link in coastal food webs.

Forages among submerged and shoreline vegetation, eating plant material and detritus along with animal prey.

Native to the western Atlantic, from the U.S. East Coast through the Gulf of Mexico. It lives in estuaries and coastal waters and ranges into brackish and even fresh water, which is how it reaches inland ponds with a tidal or saltwater connection.

The blue crab is an estuary animal, built for the in-between waters where rivers meet the sea. Its Latin name, Callinectes sapidus, translates neatly as "savory beautiful swimmer" — a nod to its blue-tinged beauty, its swimming ability, and its long history as prized seafood along the Atlantic and Gulf coasts.

What sets it apart from a typical crab is the back pair of legs, flattened into broad paddles. With them the blue crab can truly swim, sculling sideways through open water rather than only scuttling along the bottom.

That tolerance for a wide range of saltiness is why it shows up where you might not expect a crab. Finding one in a park pond usually means the water is brackish or connected to tidal flow — the crab itself reading as a living water-quality clue. Sex is easy once you know the trick: mature females have red-tipped claws, like painted nails, while males' claws stay all blue.

Take care

The risk

Harmless at a distance, but the pincers are strong and a cornered or handled crab can deliver a hard pinch. Do not try to pick one up.

What to do

Look, do not touch. Give it room at the water's edge and do not handle it; those claws mean business.
