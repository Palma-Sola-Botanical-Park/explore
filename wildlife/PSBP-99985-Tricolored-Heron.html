Tricolored Heron

Egretta tricolor
On this page

At a glance

  • Blue-gray and purple above but bright white below — the only dark Florida heron with a clean white belly.
  • It hunts on the move, running, turning and lunging through shallow water after small fish.
  • Listed as Threatened in Florida, though the species is not considered at risk globally.

How to know it

Color

Blue-gray upperparts with purple tones, against a bright white belly.

Neck

Long and slender, with a white stripe down the front in adults. Young birds are much rustier on the neck and upperparts.

Bill

Long, narrow and pointed.

The white belly is the whole identification. No other dark heron here is clean white underneath.

Easily confused with

Little Blue Heron — Adult Little Blue Herons are dark underneath as well as above and move more deliberately. Tricolored Herons have a bright white belly and often feed more actively.

Great Blue Heron — Much larger and heavier, with a pale head and thick yellowish bill. Tricolored Herons are slimmer and less than half the bulk.

Where to find it here

Shallow pond edges and open wetland. This is the dark heron that keeps moving — quick runs, sharp turns and lunges, where a Little Blue Heron would be picking its way slowly.

A year-round Florida resident and an occasional visitor here.

How it lives

Tricolored Herons hunt small fish in shallow water by stalking, running and making quick turns and lunges. Their feeding style is generally more active than that of Little Blue Herons.

What it does here

Small fish are the target, and the hunting is built around chasing rather than waiting. A bird will sometimes crouch so low before striking that its belly nearly touches the water.

They nest in dense shrubs and small trees, usually on islands or standing over water. The male gathers sticks and the female builds, and some nests are lined with finer twigs or cordgrass. That same wetland vegetation is what gives the small fish their cover.

Take care

Conservation

Tricolored Herons are listed as Threatened in Florida, though the species is not considered at risk globally. The state points to wetland development, altered water flow, reduced prey and disturbance at nesting colonies as the pressures behind the listing.
