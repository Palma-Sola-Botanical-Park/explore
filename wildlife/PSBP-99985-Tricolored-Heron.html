Tricolored Heron

Egretta tricolor

At a glance

  • The tricolored heron is a slender, elegant wader in blue-gray and purple with a clean white belly — the white stripe down its front is the quickest field mark.
  • It is an active, almost theatrical hunter, dashing, lunging, and twisting through the shallows after small fish rather than standing still.
  • It tends to hunt alone, a bit more solitary than the other wading birds it shares the ponds with.
  • Once known as the Louisiana heron, it is now a Threatened species in Florida, with declining numbers in the Tampa Bay area.

How to know it

Color

Blue-gray head, back, and wings washed with purple, and a distinctive white belly and underparts.

Neck

Long and slender, often with a rusty-and-white stripe down the front.

Bill

Long, thin, and pointed.

Behavior cue

Active, darting feeding style.

Voice

Generally quiet away from the colony, where it gives groans and squawks.

Size

22 to 30 inches; about 38 inches

What to look for

A medium, slim heron that is dark above and clean white below, working the shallows in quick, restless movements. That white belly on an otherwise dark heron is the giveaway, and the active hunting style separates it from the patient great blue and the all-white egrets.

Where to find it here

Along the pond shallows and edges, usually hunting alone with quick, darting movements. Look for the dark heron with the bright white belly.

Year-round resident. Most often seen actively feeding in the shallows during the day.

How it lives

Active by day and an energetic forager, often hunting alone. Wades, runs, and lunges through shallow water. Nests in colonies with other wading birds between roughly February and August.

Mainly small fish, plus aquatic insects, crustaceans, and amphibians, caught with quick stabs during active pursuit.

What it does here

A small-fish specialist that helps balance shallow-water fish populations, and a sensitive member of Florida's wading-bird colonies whose decline signals wetland stress.

Native to coastal regions of the Americas, from the U.S. Atlantic and Gulf coasts through the Caribbean to northern South America. In Florida it is a widespread, year-round resident, though less common in parts of the Panhandle.

Where a great blue heron hunts by patience, the tricolored heron hunts by motion. It runs through the shallows with wings half-raised, stabs, pivots, and chases — an energetic style that is fun to watch and well suited to catching small, fast fish.

It is also more of a loner than most herons, often feeding by itself even where other waders gather, though it still nests in mixed colonies.

Its status is a concern: the tricolored heron is listed as Threatened in Florida, and biologists have documented declines around Tampa Bay tied to lost nesting habitat, predation, and disturbance — including the danger of discarded fishing line.

Take care

What to do

Keep your distance and do not feed it. As a Threatened species, it is especially worth giving room — and please pack out any fishing line, which entangles wading birds.

Conservation

A State-designated Threatened species in Florida, and globally Least Concern. Florida populations, including around Tampa Bay, have been declining due to habitat loss, predation, and disturbance.
