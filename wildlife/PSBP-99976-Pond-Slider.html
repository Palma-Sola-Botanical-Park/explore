At a glance

  • The pond slider is the classic basking turtle you see lined up on logs, sliding into the water at the first hint of trouble — which is how it got its name.
  • Two kinds turn up in Florida, and telling them apart matters: the yellow-bellied slider, with a yellow patch behind the eye, is native here; the red-eared slider, with a red stripe behind the eye, is not.
  • The red-eared slider is one of the most widely released pet turtles in the world, and where people dump unwanted pets, it competes with and even interbreeds with native turtles.
  • Young sliders eat mostly small animals; as they grow, they shift toward a diet of aquatic plants.

How to know it

Easily confused with

Peninsular Cooter — Sliders are smaller and have a distinct colored patch (red or yellow) just behind the eye; cooters are larger and lack the ear patch.

Florida Red-bellied Cooter — The slider's colored ear patch behind the eye is the giveaway; the larger red-bellied cooter has none.

Shell

Smooth, oval, olive-green to brown with yellow markings, darkening with age.

Head stripe

A yellow patch behind the eye (yellow-bellied) or a red stripe behind the eye (red-eared) — the key to telling the two apart.

Skin

Dark with yellow stripes.

Size

Females larger than males.

Voice

Essentially silent.

What to look for

A medium turtle basking on a log, with yellow stripes on the head and neck. Check just behind the eye: a yellow blotch points to the native yellow-bellied slider; a red stripe points to the introduced red-eared slider.

Where to find it here

Basking on logs, banks, and debris at the ponds on sunny days, often several together. Look behind the eye to tell native from introduced.

Year-round resident. Most visible basking on warm, sunny days.

When

Most visible basking on warm, sunny days.

How it lives

It basks. A slider spends long stretches on a log or a bank warming up, and slides into the water fast at the first sign of anything approaching — which is where the name comes from.

Omnivorous, and the balance shifts with age. Young sliders take insects, tadpoles, snails and small fish; adults move toward aquatic plants and algae.

What it does here

The native yellow-bellied slider (Trachemys scripta scripta) belongs to the southeastern U.S., including north Florida. The red-eared slider (Trachemys scripta elegans) is native to the Mississippi Valley, not Florida — but released pets have spread it across much of the peninsula and beyond.

A basking pond turtle that grazes vegetation and scavenges. The native yellow-bellied slider is part of Florida's natural pond life, while released red-eared sliders can crowd out native turtles.

Adults graze on submerged aquatic plants and algae (younger turtles eat more animal prey) and bask among pond-edge vegetation.

Mixed. The native yellow-bellied slider belongs in Florida. The red-eared slider is an introduced subspecies, spread by released pets, that competes and interbreeds with native turtles and ranks among the world's worst invasive species.

'Pond slider' covers two subspecies that look similar but tell very different stories in Florida. The yellow-bellied slider is a true native. The red-eared slider — the little green turtle once sold by the millions in pet stores — comes from farther west and does not naturally belong here.

The trouble starts when people release pet turtles they can no longer keep. Red-eared sliders are hardy and adaptable, and in the wild they compete with native turtles for basking sites and food, and can interbreed with the native yellow-bellied slider, blurring its genetic line. Globally, the red-eared slider is listed among the world's worst invasive species, largely because of the pet trade.

The takeaway for visitors is simple: enjoy the turtles basking on the park's logs, but never release a pet turtle into a pond. What looks like a kindness can quietly harm the native wildlife already living there.

Take care

What to do

Watch from a distance, and never release a pet turtle into the park or any wild pond — released red-eared sliders harm native turtles. Do not handle or feed them.
