Pond Slider

Trachemys scripta
On this page

At a glance

  • Pond Sliders are medium-sized freshwater turtles that often bask on logs and banks, dropping quickly into the water when disturbed.
  • Two forms occur in Florida. Yellow-bellied Sliders, with a large yellow cheek patch, are native only to northern Florida. Red-eared Sliders, with a red stripe behind the eye, are introduced in Florida and widespread through the peninsula.
  • Red-eared Sliders spread largely through released pets. In Florida they compete with native turtles for food and basking sites and can hybridize with native Yellow-bellied Sliders.
  • Young sliders eat more animal prey; vegetation becomes increasingly important as they mature.

How to know it

Shape

A medium-sized, fairly rounded pond turtle with yellow stripes on the head, neck and legs. The shell reaches about a foot long, and females grow larger than males.

Red-eared form

A broad red stripe behind the eye. The shell is usually olive to dark brown with yellow patterning.

Yellow-bellied form

A large yellow patch behind the eye rather than a red stripe.

What to look for

Look behind the eye. A red stripe identifies a Red-eared Slider, an introduced form in Florida. A large yellow cheek patch indicates a Yellow-bellied Slider, whose natural Florida range is farther north.

Easily confused with

Peninsular Cooter — Larger and lacks the prominent red or yellow patch behind the eye. Peninsular Cooters instead show fine yellow striping on the head.

Florida Red-bellied Cooter — Usually larger, without an ear patch, and often shows reddish color on the underside of the shell.

Where to find it here

Look for sliders basking on logs, banks or floating debris around the ponds on warm sunny days. Check the colored patch behind the eye before deciding which form you are seeing.

Present year-round in Florida and easiest to notice while basking in warm, sunny weather. Palma Sola currently has too few records to estimate local frequency.

How it lives

Basking lets a slider raise its body temperature before returning to the water to feed. An approached turtle usually drops quickly from its perch into the pond.

Young sliders eat insects and other small aquatic animals more heavily. As they mature, aquatic vegetation makes up a larger part of the diet.

What it does here

Two forms, two geographies

The Yellow-bellied Slider is native to the southeastern United States and reaches only northern Florida naturally. The Red-eared Slider is native farther west in the Mississippi drainage but has been introduced widely through the pet trade and now occurs throughout much of Florida. A slider basking here is unlikely to be a naturally occurring animal either way.

Plants in the pond

Aquatic plants become increasingly important food as sliders mature. Dense submerged and shoreline vegetation also provides cover for young turtles and the small aquatic animals they eat.

Take care

Do not handle. A frightened turtle may bite in self-defense. Keep dogs from approaching or chasing turtles on land.

Watch without handling or feeding. Never release a pet turtle into a park pond or other natural waterway. Florida's Exotic Pet Amnesty Program provides an alternative for owners who can no longer keep nonnative pets.

Conservation

The species as a whole is widespread. In Florida, the conservation issue is not scarcity of sliders but the effect of introduced Red-eared Sliders on native freshwater turtles and on the genetic integrity of native Yellow-bellied Slider populations.

Also known as

Yellow-bellied Slider (native to northern Florida) · Red-eared Slider (introduced in Florida)
