Marsh Rabbit

Sylvilagus palustris
On this page

At a glance

  • A small, dark rabbit with short ears and a tiny brownish tail — not the long-eared cottontail most people picture when they hear 'rabbit.'
  • It is one of the very few rabbits that swims, and it does so willingly — wading into water to escape predators or to reach better feeding areas.
  • Unlike cottontails, the marsh rabbit's tail is not white and fluffy — it is small, brown, and inconspicuous.
  • It is a true Florida native, tied to the state's wetlands, marshes, and dense, damp vegetation.

How to know it

Size

Small — smaller than a cottontail, about 14 to 16 inches.

Ears

Short for a rabbit.

Tail

Small, brown — not the white 'cotton ball' of a cottontail.

Color

Dark brown to blackish-brown, darker than a cottontail.

Size

14 to 16 inches

What to look for

A small, dark rabbit near water with short ears and no white tail. If it runs toward water instead of away, it is a marsh rabbit.

Where to find it here

Along pond edges and in dense low vegetation, especially at dawn or dusk.

Year-round resident, most visible at dawn and dusk.

When

Crepuscular; rarely seen in midday.

How it lives

Most active at dawn and dusk, feeding on grasses, sedges and whatever low green growth is at hand. Strictly a plant eater.

Two things separate it from a cottontail. It swims — readily, and well. And when something alarms it, it freezes before it runs, which is often the only reason you get a look at one.

What it does here

Herbivore and prey animal — a food source for hawks, owls, bobcats, and snakes.

Feeds on grasses, sedges, and wetland plants.

Native to the southeastern United States from Virginia to Florida. Year-round resident in Florida's wetlands.

The marsh rabbit is the rabbit of Florida's damp places — swamps, pond edges, marshes, and dense wet thickets. It is smaller and darker than the eastern cottontail, with proportionally shorter ears and a dingy brown tail instead of the cottontail's white powder puff.

Its willingness to swim sets it apart from most rabbits. When threatened, it may plunge into water and swim with only its nose above the surface, or sit motionless in shallow water among emergent vegetation.

It is most active at dawn and dusk, feeding on grasses, sedges, and aquatic plants.
