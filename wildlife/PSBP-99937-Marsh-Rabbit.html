Marsh Rabbit

Sylvilagus palustris
On this page

At a glance

  • A Marsh Rabbit is smaller and darker than an Eastern Cottontail, with shorter ears and a small, inconspicuous brownish tail.
  • Marsh Rabbits are strong swimmers and usually stay close to water, using wetlands as both feeding habitat and escape cover.
  • They feed on grasses, sedges and other vegetation growing in and around wetlands.
  • Most activity occurs around dusk, dawn and at night, so a rabbit seen crossing a path may disappear into dense vegetation quickly.

How to know it

Size and shape

Small and compact, about fourteen to sixteen inches long, with noticeably shorter ears than an Eastern Cottontail.

Color

Dark brown to blackish-brown, generally darker than a cottontail.

Tail

Small and inconspicuous, gray-brown rather than the cottontail's obvious white puff.

What to look for

Look near dense vegetation around wet areas for a small, dark rabbit with short ears and no conspicuous white cottontail. Marsh Rabbits may also walk rather than making the long bounding hops associated with many rabbits.

Easily confused with

Eastern Cottontail — Larger and generally paler, with longer ears and a conspicuous white 'cotton' tail. Marsh Rabbits are darker, shorter-eared and closely associated with wetlands.

Where to find it here

Look along dense, damp vegetation near pond margins, wet swales and other low areas. Marsh Rabbits usually remain close to thick plant cover and water rather than feeding far out on an exposed lawn.

Present year-round in Florida but most active around dusk, dawn and at night. Daytime sightings are possible, especially where dense vegetation provides nearby cover.

How it lives

Unlike the familiar cottontail of open fields, the Marsh Rabbit is closely tied to wet ground and dense vegetation. It moves through grasses and thickets and can swim strongly when crossing water or escaping danger.

Its food is vegetation growing in and around wetlands, including grasses, sedges and other green plant material.

Nesting on the ground

Females place shallow nests on the ground in dense cover, lining them with grass and fur.

It swims, which almost no other rabbit does. A Marsh Rabbit under threat may go straight into the water and swim with only its nose above the surface, or sit motionless in the shallows among the stems until whatever worried it has gone.

What it does here

A native wetland herbivore and an important prey animal for owls, foxes, bobcats, alligators and other predators.

Cover and food are the same plants

Wetland plants provide both food and shelter. Marsh Rabbits eat grasses, sedges and other green vegetation and stay close to dense plant growth where they can feed while remaining near cover. Maintaining thick, low vegetation around wet areas preserves the structure this rabbit depends on.
