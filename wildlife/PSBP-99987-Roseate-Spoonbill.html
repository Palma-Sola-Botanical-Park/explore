Roseate Spoonbill

Platalea ajaja

At a glance

  • There are six spoonbill species in the world. The roseate spoonbill is the only one native to the Americas — and despite that strange bill, it is actually a relative of ibises.
  • Spoonbills do not manufacture their pink color. Carotenoid pigments from shrimp, crayfish, and other small aquatic prey end up deposited in their feathers. Young birds start out much duller and become pinker as they mature.
  • The bill works by touch. A spoonbill sweeps it through shallow water and snaps it shut the instant prey contacts the sensitive inside — letting it feed even when the water is too muddy to see through.
  • Florida nearly lost them. Plume hunters killed spoonbills by the thousands for the feather trade in the late 1800s and early 1900s, and by the 1930s only around 30 to 40 breeding pairs remained in Florida Bay. Legal protection allowed the population to recover, but they remain Threatened in Florida.

How to know it

Overall

A large pink wading bird with a white neck, bare pale greenish head, and an enormous bill that widens dramatically at the tip into two flat, paddle-like lobes. That combination solves the identification immediately.

Color

Pink body with deeper red on the wings and tail coverts; white neck and back; legs pinkish-red. Young birds are much paler and lack the vivid adult pink.

Bill

Long and gray, flattening into a broad, spatulate tip — straight, not down-curved. The pink color can suggest a flamingo, but this bill is unmistakable: straight and flat, not down-bent.

Head

Bare and pale greenish-gray in adults — a surprisingly distinctive feature. A pink bird with a bald, pale head has essentially already been identified.

In flight

Flies with neck outstretched, like an ibis, showing the rosy wings against the sky.

Voice

Generally quiet, giving low grunts and clucking sounds, mostly around the colony.

Size

28 to 34 inches; about 4 to 4.5 feet (50 to 54 inches)

What to look for

A large pink wading bird: white neck, bare pale head, long legs, and a bill that widens into flat paddle-like lobes at the tip. The long legs let it work shallow water while keeping most of its body above the surface. No other Florida bird combines all of these features.

Where to find it here

In the shallows and along pond edges. The bright pink plumage stands out against the water, but spoonbills move around in response to water conditions and food availability, so sightings at the park are occasional rather than predictable. Not a bird you're likely to see every visit — but when one turns up, it is hard to miss.

Present in Florida year-round, but it wanders widely in search of feeding areas, so park sightings are more occasional than for the resident herons.

When

Wanders widely in search of feeding areas, so sightings are sporadic.

How it lives

It feeds by feel, wading slowly and sweeping that spoon of a bill side to side through the shallows, snapping shut the moment something touches it. Small fish, shrimp, crayfish, crabs, aquatic insects — sifted rather than seen.

Highly social: it feeds and roosts in groups and nests in colonies with herons, egrets and ibises, in trees and shrubs over or near water.

Give a colony room

Disturbance at a nesting colony can cause the adults to abandon their nests outright.

What it does here

Spoonbills depend on healthy shallow wetlands with abundant small aquatic prey. Because their feeding and breeding success track water conditions so closely, they are useful indicators of wetland ecosystem health — including the health of the Everglades.

Nests in waterside trees and shrubs, including mangroves.

Roseate spoonbills are the only spoonbill species native to the Americas — one of six spoonbill species found worldwide. They range from the southern United States through Central and South America. In Florida the species is found mainly along the coasts and in the southern peninsula, and the Tampa Bay region holds one of the state's important breeding populations.

Despite the unusual bill, the roseate spoonbill is a member of the ibis and spoonbill family, Threskiornithidae. Look past the pink and the body plan — long legs, long neck, flight with neck outstretched — reveals the family connection clearly.

The bill is less like a spoon and more like two flat paddles meeting at the end, covered in touch sensors. A spoonbill sweeps it open through shallow water, and when prey contacts the sensitive inside, the bill snaps shut. That lets the bird feed in water too murky to see through.

Spoonbills do not produce their pink color. They absorb carotenoid pigments — mainly from crustaceans and other small aquatic prey — and deposit those pigments into their feathers during molting. Young birds are considerably duller than adults, and their coloration strengthens as they mature and consume more carotenoid-rich food.

Florida nearly lost the species entirely. Between the 1850s and early 1900s, spoonbill feathers were fashionable in ladies' hats and fans, and hunters killed the birds across Florida Bay and the Gulf Coast. By the 1930s only around 30 to 40 breeding pairs remained.

Legal protection brought a slow recovery, but the species is still State-designated Threatened in Florida — and globally listed as Least Concern, reflecting how different the pressures on Florida's population are from those facing the species across the Americas as a whole.

Because their feeding and breeding depend so closely on shallow-water conditions and prey availability, spoonbills are useful indicators of how wetland ecosystems are functioning. Biologists watch Florida Bay colonies carefully as a reflection of Everglades health.

Take care

What to do

Admire from a distance and never feed it. Disturbance at nesting colonies can cause adults to leave nests, exposing eggs and chicks. As a State-designated Threatened species, spoonbills especially deserve their space.

Conservation

A State-designated Threatened species in Florida, and globally Least Concern. It recovered from near-extinction during the plume-hunting era, but habitat loss and changing water conditions keep it on Florida's watch list.
