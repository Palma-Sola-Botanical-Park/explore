Roseate Spoonbill

Platalea ajaja
On this page

At a glance

  • Roseate Spoonbills occur around Tampa Bay as well as farther south in Florida. The region supports important breeding colonies of these large pink wading birds.
  • The pink comes from carotenoid pigments in the spoonbill's diet. Young birds are much paler and develop stronger pink coloration as they mature.
  • The flattened bill works partly by touch. The bird sweeps it through shallow water and snaps it shut when sensitive tissue inside contacts prey.
  • By the 1930s, plume hunting had reduced Florida's population to roughly 30 to 40 breeding pairs in Florida Bay. The species recovered after legal protection, but it remains State-designated Threatened in Florida.

How to know it

Overall

A large pink wading bird with a white neck, bare pale head and a long bill that widens into a flat spoon shape at the tip.

Color

Adults are pink with deeper rose-red on parts of the wings. Young birds are much paler.

In flight

Flies with the neck stretched straight out, unlike herons that pull the neck back.

Size

Roughly two and a half feet tall, with a wingspan a little over four feet.

What to look for

Look for a large pink wading bird sweeping a flattened bill from side to side through shallow water. Adults have a bare pale head and white neck. In flight, the neck remains extended.

Where to find it here

Look in shallow water along pond margins and other open wetland edges. Spoonbills move frequently as water depth and prey availability change, so a park sighting is an occasional event rather than something to expect every visit.

Present in Florida year-round, but it wanders widely in search of feeding areas, so park sightings are more occasional than for the resident herons.

When

Wanders widely in search of feeding areas, so sightings are sporadic.

How it lives

A spoonbill wades through shallow water with its bill partly open, sweeping its head from side to side. Contact with a fish, shrimp or other small animal triggers the bill to close.

Spoonbills feed and roost socially and nest in colonies with other wading birds, often in trees or shrubs over or near water.

What it does here

Spoonbill feeding and nesting success depend strongly on water depth and the concentration of small aquatic prey. For that reason, biologists use breeding performance in places such as Florida Bay to track how wetland hydrology is functioning.

Mangroves and other waterside trees and shrubs provide nesting structure, often on islands or over water where colonies gain some protection from land predators. The plants around a wetland therefore matter even though the spoonbill's food comes from the water.

Native to the Americas, from the southern United States through the Caribbean and Central and South America. In Florida, important populations occur in Florida Bay and around Tampa Bay.

The spoonbill belongs to the ibis family, and the resemblance shows once you look past the pink and the bill: long legs, a long neck carried straight out in flight, and the same steady flap-and-glide as a White Ibis.

Nesting is a communal business. Spoonbills build bulky stick nests in trees and shrubs standing over or beside water, usually alongside herons, egrets and ibises rather than in colonies of their own. Sites on islands or above open water are harder for raccoons and other climbing predators to reach, which is part of why the surrounding trees matter as much as the shallow water does.

Take care

What to do

Watch from a distance and do not feed or approach a spoonbill. Never approach a nesting colony; repeated disturbance can cause adults to leave eggs or young exposed. Roseate Spoonbills are State-designated Threatened in Florida.

Conservation

State-designated Threatened in Florida and globally classified as Least Concern. Florida spoonbills recovered dramatically after plume hunting ended, but wetland alteration, nesting disturbance and changing water conditions remain concerns.
