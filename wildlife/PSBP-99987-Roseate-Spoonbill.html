Wading Birds
Roseate Spoonbill
Platalea ajaja Threskiornithidae
📷 Photo by Rob Carr · CC-BY-NC · via iNaturalist · 📅 Mar 22, 2025
⚡Quick Hits
  • There are six spoonbill species in the world. The roseate spoonbill is the only one native to the Americas — and despite that strange bill, it is actually a relative of ibises.
  • Spoonbills do not manufacture their pink color. Carotenoid pigments from shrimp, crayfish, and other small aquatic prey end up deposited in their feathers. Young birds start out much duller and become pinker as they mature.
  • The bill works by touch. A spoonbill sweeps it through shallow water and snaps it shut the instant prey contacts the sensitive inside — letting it feed even when the water is too muddy to see through.
  • Florida nearly lost them. Plume hunters killed spoonbills by the thousands for the feather trade in the late 1800s and early 1900s, and by the 1930s only around 30 to 40 breeding pairs remained in Florida Bay. Legal protection allowed the population to recover, but they remain Threatened in Florida.
🔎How to Spot It
Overall

A large pink wading bird with a white neck, bare pale greenish head, and an enormous bill that widens dramatically at the tip into two flat, paddle-like lobes. That combination solves the identification immediately.

Color

Pink body with deeper red on the wings and tail coverts; white neck and back; legs pinkish-red. Young birds are much paler and lack the vivid adult pink.

Bill

Long and gray, flattening into a broad, spatulate tip — straight, not down-curved. The pink color can suggest a flamingo, but this bill is unmistakable: straight and flat, not down-bent.

Head

Bare and pale greenish-gray in adults — a surprisingly distinctive feature. A pink bird with a bald, pale head has essentially already been identified.

In flight

Flies with neck outstretched, like an ibis, showing the rosy wings against the sky.

Voice

Generally quiet, giving low grunts and clucking sounds, mostly around the colony.

What to Look For

A large pink wading bird: white neck, bare pale head, long legs, and a bill that widens into flat paddle-like lobes at the tip. The long legs let it work shallow water while keeping most of its body above the surface. No other Florida bird combines all of these features.

🍽️What It Eats

Small fish, shrimp, crayfish, crabs, aquatic insects, and other small creatures, sifted from shallow water by feel with that spoon-shaped bill.

📍Where & When
Where in the park

In the shallows and along pond edges. The bright pink plumage stands out against the water, but spoonbills move around in response to water conditions and food availability, so sightings at the park are occasional rather than predictable. Not a bird you're likely to see every visit — but when one turns up, it is hard to miss.

When to see it

Present in Florida year-round, but it wanders widely in search of feeding areas, so park sightings are more occasional than for the resident herons.

🤝Watching It Respectfully

Admire from a distance and never feed it. Disturbance at nesting colonies can cause adults to leave nests, exposing eggs and chicks. As a State-designated Threatened species, spoonbills especially deserve their space.
