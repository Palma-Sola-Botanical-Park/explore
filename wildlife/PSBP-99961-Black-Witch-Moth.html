Black Witch Moth

Ascalapha odorata
On this page

At a glance

  • A huge, dark, bat-like moth — wings spanning up to seven inches — resting in a shady spot is the black witch, one of the largest moths in North America.
  • It is wrapped in folklore. In parts of Mexico it is the "mariposa de la muerte," an omen of death, while in the Caribbean and Texas it can be a sign of coming good fortune or money.
  • For all its menacing looks, it is utterly harmless. The adult drinks fruit juices and cannot bite or sting.
  • It is a long-distance wanderer, drifting north from the tropics through the warm months and turning up far from where it was born.

How to know it

Size

Very large, wingspan to about 5 to 7 inches.

Color

Mottled dark brown and gray, with faint iridescent purple and a pale band across the wingtips.

Marking

A small comma- or number-9-shaped spot near the front edge of each forewing; females show a paler band.

Posture

Rests flat and wings-spread in shaded, sheltered spots by day.

Voice

Silent.

Size

5 to 7 inches — among the largest of any moth in the region.

What to look for

A bat-sized dark moth tucked into shade — under eaves, bridges, or dense foliage — by day, or flapping heavily at dusk. The sheer size and the little hook-shaped wing marks set it apart.

Where to find it here

Resting in shady, sheltered spots by day — under eaves, in dense foliage, or beneath structures near the ponds — and around lights at night in the warm season.

A warm-season visitor, most likely from late spring through fall as individuals wander north from the tropics.

When

Wanders north from the tropics.

How it lives

Mainly active at dusk and night, hiding by day in shaded, sheltered places. A strong, wandering flier that ranges far from where it developed. Drawn to lights and to fermenting fruit.

Adults sip juices from overripe and rotting fruit and tree sap. Caterpillars feed on the leaves of legumes such as acacia and other woody plants.

What it does here

Part of the nighttime insect world as both pollinator-adjacent fruit feeder and prey for bats and birds. As a tropical wanderer, its appearances mark the seasonal movement of insects northward.

Caterpillars feed on legume trees and shrubs (such as acacia and cassia); adults are drawn to fermenting and fallen fruit.

Native to the American tropics, from South America up through Mexico, and a regular warm-season wanderer into Florida and across the southern United States.

The black witch is a moth built on a grand scale, with a wingspan that can top seven inches — broad, dark, and scalloped, with a small comma-and-number-eight marking on each forewing. Seen flapping at dusk, it is easy to mistake for a bat.

Few insects carry as much folklore. Across its range it has gathered names and beliefs that pull in opposite directions: in much of Mexico and Central America it is the butterfly of death, an ill omen if it enters a home where someone is sick; in parts of the Caribbean and South Texas, by contrast, one landing over your doorway is said to bring money or luck. The same big dark moth, read as a curse in one place and a blessing in another.

Behind the stories is a gentle, mobile insect. Adults feed on rotting fruit and sap, not blood or nectar exclusively, and they have no interest in people. Each year individuals drift far north of the tropics, which is how a tropical giant ends up resting under a Florida eave or bridge.
