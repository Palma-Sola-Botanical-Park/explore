Brown Thrasher

Toxostoma rufum
On this page

At a glance

  • A rake and a hammer in one bill. Quick sideways swipes toss dead leaves aside to uncover insects, and the same long bill can pound an acorn until the shell breaks.
  • Say it twice. The song runs through phrase after phrase, and the bird usually repeats each one before switching to the next. Listen for the pairs.
  • A borrower. Its songs can include imitations of other birds, the Northern Cardinal and the Tufted Titmouse among them. It belongs to the mimic family, along with the mockingbird and the catbird.
  • A ground bird that sings from the top. It does most of its feeding on the ground, close to thick cover, but a singing male may climb to a high, open perch.

How to know it

Eyes, bars and tail

Rusty brown above, pale below with heavy dark streaks and a long tail. Look for the bright yellow eye, two narrow pale bars across each wing, and a long bill with a slight downward curve. That combination settles it.

Its cousins

The Northern Mockingbird is gray, with big white patches that flash in the wings. The Gray Catbird is plain slate gray with a black cap. A Wood Thrush is spotted too, but it has dark eyes, rounder spots and a much shorter tail.

Listen for it

Besides the long, doubled song, it has a sharp, smacking call that sounds nothing like the song.

How it lives

A singer with a huge songbook

One count puts its repertoire at more than 1,100 song types. The male strings them together, usually twice each, and works in borrowed phrases from other birds. When he sings he may leave the undergrowth for a high, open branch, which is your best chance at a clear look.

Two builders

Both parents build the nest, a bulky cup of twigs, leaves, bark and grass, low in a dense shrub or tangle. Both sit on the eggs and both feed the chicks, which leave the nest after about two weeks. A bird disturbed at its nest often drops to the ground and runs off into thick cover instead of flying.

Around all year

In this part of Florida it has been recorded in every season, nesting included. Our photographs of it here are from June and July.

What it does

Working the leaf litter

Watch the ground under shrubs. A thrasher sweeps its bill from side to side through the dead leaves and soil, then grabs what it turns up: beetles, caterpillars and other small animals. It also pokes into clumps of dead leaves caught in branches, and now and then snaps an insect out of the air.

It is not only an insect eater. Berries, seeds and nuts fill out the menu, and that is where the acorn hammering comes in.
