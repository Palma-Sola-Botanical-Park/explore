Western Cattle-Egret

Ardea ibis
On this page
At a glanceHow to know itWhere to find it hereHow it livesWhat it does here

At a glance

  • Cattle Egrets reached the Americas without human help. The species spread from Africa to South America in the late 1800s and had reached Florida by the middle of the twentieth century.
  • A compact white egret standing on dry grass rather than at the water's edge is usually a Cattle Egret.
  • It feeds by following something larger. Cattle, a mower or a tractor pushes insects out of the grass ahead of it, and the egret walks behind picking them off.
  • Breeding adults develop orange-buff patches on the crown, back and breast, and the bill and legs brighten.

How to know it

Where it stands

Dry fields and lawns rather than shallow water. That alone separates it from the park's other white waders most of the time.

Build

Compact and stocky, with a shorter, thicker neck than a Snowy or Great Egret.

Bill and legs

Short, stout and yellow to orange. Not the long black bill of a Snowy Egret.

Breeding adults

Orange-buff patches appear on the crown, back and breast.

Size

About twenty inches tall, with a wingspan around three feet.

What to look for

Look for a small, thick-necked white bird walking on open grass with a short yellow bill. If it is following a mower or a large animal, that is the bird.

Easily confused with

Snowy Egret — Snowy Egrets are slimmer with a long black bill, black legs and yellow feet, and they feed in shallow water. Cattle Egrets are stocky, short-billed and usually on dry grass.

Great Egret — Great Egrets are much larger and taller with a long yellow bill and black legs. A Cattle Egret is roughly half the height and thick-necked.

Where to find it here

Check open grassy areas rather than the pond edges — especially anywhere the grass is being cut or disturbed. Cattle Egrets often feed in loose groups.

A year-round Florida resident. Palma Sola has only a single record, so treat it as an occasional visitor rather than a bird to expect.

When

Occurs year-round in Florida. Palma Sola has too few records to establish a local pattern.

How it lives

It hunts by following something bigger. Cattle, a tractor, a mower — anything that walks through grass and pushes insects out ahead of it. The egret is not feeding on the cattle; it is taking the grasshoppers and crickets their feet flush from the vegetation, and a mower does exactly the same job.

Grasshoppers, crickets and flies make up most of the diet. Usually in loose groups, and colonial when nesting.

What it does here

This egret separates its two lives between very different plants. It feeds on open mown or grazed grass, where short turf lets it walk and see what is flushed out, but it nests colonially in trees and shrubs near water, often alongside other wading birds. Neither habitat works without the other.

This egret arrived under its own power. An Old World species, it crossed the Atlantic to South America in the late 1800s, spread north, and reached Florida by the middle of the twentieth century. That makes it new to the Americas but not introduced — nobody carried it here, and a natural range expansion is a different thing from an introduced species.
