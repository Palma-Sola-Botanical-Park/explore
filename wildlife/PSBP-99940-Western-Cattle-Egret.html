Western Cattle-Egret

Ardea ibis
On this page
At a glanceHow to know itWhere to find it hereHow it livesWhat it does here

At a glance

  • A compact white egret standing in a field next to cattle or mowing equipment — not at the water's edge — is almost certainly a cattle egret.
  • It is the only egret in Florida that forages primarily on dry land, following large animals and machinery to catch the insects they stir up.
  • It arrived in the Americas on its own, crossing the Atlantic from Africa in the 1940s and spreading rapidly across the continent — a natural colonizer, not an introduction.
  • In breeding plumage it flushes with patches of orange-buff on the head, chest, and back.

How to know it

Habitat

Dry fields and lawns, not water — the key distinction from other egrets.

Size

Compact and stocky — smaller and thicker-necked than a snowy egret.

Bill

Short, stout, yellow to orange.

Breeding

Patches of orange-buff on head, chest, and back.

Voice

Generally quiet; croaks at the colony.

Size

18 to 22 inches; about 3 feet

What to look for

A compact white egret in a field or lawn, often near livestock or mowing equipment. Stockier and shorter-necked than other egrets, with a yellow bill.

Where to find it here

Open grassy areas, especially if mowing is happening nearby. Not at the water's edge.

Year-round resident.

How it lives

It hunts by following something bigger. Cattle, a tractor, a mower — anything that walks through grass and flushes insects out ahead of it. The egret walks behind and picks off what jumps.

Grasshoppers, crickets, flies. Usually in loose groups, and it nests in colonies.

What it does here

Insect predator in agricultural and open-ground habitats. A natural colonizer that filled an unoccupied niche.

Nests colonially in trees near water, though it forages far from it.

Originally from Africa. Naturally colonized South America in the 1870s and North America by the 1940s–50s. Now a common year-round resident in Florida.

The cattle egret is a remarkable natural success story. Without any human assistance, it crossed the Atlantic from Africa and colonized the New World, reaching Florida by the 1950s and spreading across the continent within decades.

Its trick is its feeding strategy: instead of hunting in water like other egrets, it follows large animals — cattle, horses, tractors — and snaps up the grasshoppers, crickets, and other insects they disturb. This terrestrial niche was largely unoccupied, which is part of why it spread so fast.

Despite being a recent arrival, it is not considered invasive — it filled an open niche without displacing native species.
