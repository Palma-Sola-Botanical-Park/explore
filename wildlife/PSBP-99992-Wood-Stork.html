Wood Stork

Mycteria americana
On this page

At a glance

  • The Wood Stork is the only stork that breeds in the United States. Adults are large white birds with black flight feathers, a bare dark head and a heavy downcurved bill.
  • It feeds mainly by touch, sweeping an open bill through shallow water and snapping it shut when prey makes contact.
  • Wood Storks feed most efficiently where falling water levels concentrate fish in shallow pools. Their breeding success therefore depends heavily on the timing of wet and dry conditions.
  • The U.S. breeding population was listed as endangered in 1984, improved enough to be downlisted to threatened in 2014, and was removed from the federal endangered-species list in March 2026.

How to know it

Body

Large and white, with black flight feathers that become especially obvious when the wings are spread.

Head

Adults have a bare, dark gray, scaly-looking head and neck.

Bill

Long, thick and curved downward.

In flight

Black trailing edges contrast strongly with the white wings, and the long legs extend behind the tail.

Size

About three feet tall, with a wingspan around five feet.

What to look for

Look for a large white wading bird with a bare dark head and heavy downcurved bill. In flight, broad black areas show along the wings and the long legs trail behind.

Easily confused with

American White Pelican — Also white with black in the wings, but has an enormous orange bill and short legs that do not trail far behind the tail.

Great Egret — Entirely white in flight, with a yellow bill, feathered head and black legs. The Wood Stork has black flight feathers and a bare dark head.

Where to find it here

Look in shallow pond margins, marshy edges and other places where fish become concentrated in receding water. Wood Storks often feed with other wading birds.

Wood Storks occur in Florida year-round. Their movements follow changing water conditions, so they may appear where shallow water has concentrated fish. At Palma Sola they are an occasional visitor rather than a bird to expect on any given day.

When

Present in Florida year-round, but local numbers shift with water levels. Recorded at Palma Sola only a handful of times.

How it lives

The bird walks slowly through shallow water with its bill partly open. Instead of aiming visually at individual fish, it waits for prey to touch the bill and then closes it.

Fish dominate the diet, with other aquatic animals taken when available.

Nesting over water

Wood Storks nest colonially in trees, often over wetlands or on islands where water helps reduce access by land predators.

What it does here

A shallow-water predator whose feeding success depends on fish being concentrated by changing water levels. Because nesting and feeding respond strongly to wetland hydrology, Wood Storks have also been used to evaluate wetland restoration.

Trees and wetlands have to work together for a Wood Stork colony. The birds build large stick nests in trees or shrubs over water or on wetland islands, while nearby shallow wetlands provide the concentrated fish needed to feed adults and young. Protecting a colony therefore means protecting both the nesting trees and the surrounding feeding wetlands.

Native to the Americas. In the United States it breeds mainly in the Southeast, especially Florida, Georgia and South Carolina, with the breeding range expanding northward during its recovery.

Wood Storks often travel between wetlands by soaring on rising warm air, sometimes circling high overhead with other large birds.

Take care

What to do

Watch from a distance and do not feed or approach the bird. Never enter or closely approach a nesting colony; repeated disturbance can cause adults to leave nests.

Conservation

Removed from the federal endangered-species list in March 2026 after decades of recovery. The U.S. breeding population had been listed as endangered in 1984 and threatened since 2014. Federal biologists will continue monitoring the population for ten years after delisting.
