Wood Stork

Mycteria americana

At a glance

  • The wood stork is the only stork that breeds in the United States — a large, quiet wading bird with a bare, scaly head and a heavy, down-curved bill.
  • It feeds by touch rather than sight: it sweeps its open bill through the water and snaps it shut the instant a fish brushes against it, in a matter of milliseconds.
  • Feeding by touch only works where fish are concentrated, so wood storks depend on the shrinking pools of the dry season — which ties them closely to the health of wetlands like the Everglades.
  • After decades of recovery, the U.S. wood stork was removed from the endangered species list in 2026, though some scientists feel the comeback is still fragile and worth watching.

How to know it

Easily confused with

American White Pelican — The white pelican has an enormous orange bill and pouch; the wood stork is smaller-billed with a bare, scaly dark head and long legs that trail in flight.

White Ibis — The white ibis is much smaller with a curved red bill; the wood stork is far larger with black flight feathers and a bare dark head.

Body

Large and white with black flight feathers, obvious in flight.

Head and neck

Bare, dark gray, and scaly-looking — unfeathered in adults.

Bill

Thick, long, and curved downward.

In flight

Neck and legs outstretched (unlike herons, which fold the neck).

Voice

Adults are nearly silent — they lack the voice-box muscles for true calls — and communicate mostly by clattering their bills and hissing. Nestlings, by contrast, are noisy.

Size

5 to 5.8 feet (60 to 69 inches); about 3.5 feet (33 to 45 inches)

What to look for

A big white wading bird with black wing edges and a dark, bald, almost reptilian head and heavy curved bill. If it is flying with its neck stretched straight out, that rules out the similar-sized herons and egrets.

Where to find it here

Around the ponds and any shallow, fish-holding water, sometimes feeding alongside other wading birds. Also worth scanning the sky — wood storks often soar high overhead.

Present in Florida year-round, though numbers and movements shift with water levels and the breeding season. A less frequent park visitor than the herons and egrets.

When

Numbers shift with water levels; a less frequent visitor than the herons and egrets.

How it lives

Active by day, often feeding in groups where fish are concentrated. Soars high on thermals between feeding and nesting sites. Nests in colonies in trees standing over or near water.

Mainly fish, caught by touch in shallow water, along with crayfish, amphibians, and other small aquatic animals concentrated in drying pools.

What it does here

As a touch-feeder that needs concentrated prey, the wood stork is a key indicator of wetland health; thriving storks signal a functioning system. It also helps regulate fish populations in seasonal pools.

Nests in waterside trees such as cypress and mangroves.

Native to the Americas, from the southeastern U.S. through Central and South America. In the U.S., breeding is centered in Florida and has expanded north into Georgia and the Carolinas. The Everglades was historically its stronghold.

The wood stork's feeding method is genuinely unusual. It wades with its bill held open underwater, and the moment a fish touches the inside, the bill snaps shut — a reflex measured in milliseconds. Because it hunts by feel rather than sight, it needs water where fish are packed together, like the pools that shrink as wetlands dry down each season.

That dependence ties the wood stork closely to the health of its wetlands. When water management or drought disrupts the natural dry-down, fish don't gather, and storks may abandon their nests or fail to raise chicks — so how well storks nest tells biologists a great deal about how well the wetlands are working.

The bird's legal status has shifted over the years: listed as endangered in 1984 after steep declines, downlisted to threatened in 2014 as numbers improved, and removed from the U.S. endangered species list in early 2026. It is still protected under other laws, and groups such as Audubon have cautioned that south Florida's population remains vulnerable.

Take care

What to do

Observe from a distance and do not feed it. Wood storks are sensitive to disturbance, especially when feeding or nesting.

Conservation

Recently recovered: the U.S. wood stork was removed from the federal endangered species list in 2026 after rebounding from its 1984 endangered listing. It stays protected under other laws, and some conservationists consider south Florida's population still fragile and closely tied to Everglades restoration.
