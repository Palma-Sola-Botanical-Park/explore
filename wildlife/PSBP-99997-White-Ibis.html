White Ibis

Eudocimus albus
On this page

At a glance

  • A flock of white birds walking across wet grass with long downcurved reddish bills is usually a flock of White Ibises.
  • Young White Ibises are brown and white rather than all white. Their adult plumage and bright red-pink bare parts develop gradually.
  • The curved bill is a sensitive probe. An ibis pushes it into mud or soft ground and closes it when it feels a crayfish, crab, insect or other prey.
  • White Ibises nest in colonies in trees, shrubs and sometimes dense marsh vegetation, often alongside other wading birds.

How to know it

Adults

White overall with a long downcurved reddish-pink bill, red facial skin and reddish legs. Black wingtips show mainly in flight.

Juveniles

Brown above and white below, with the same long curved bill. They become progressively whiter as they mature.

Size

About two feet tall, with a wingspan a little over three feet.

What to look for

Look for medium-sized white birds walking through wet lawns or shallow water with their heads down and curved bills probing the ground. Black wingtips show in flight. Brown-and-white birds with the same bill shape are juveniles.

Easily confused with

Wood Stork — Much larger, with a bare dark head and heavy downcurved bill. White Ibises are smaller, with reddish facial skin and a much narrower curved bill.

Where to find it here

Check damp lawns, grassy pond edges and shallow water, often for several birds feeding together. Soft wet ground lets the curved bill probe easily for buried prey.

A year-round Florida resident and one of the more regularly recorded wading birds at Palma Sola.

How it lives

White Ibises feed mainly by touch, probing soft mud, shallow water and wet soil with the curved bill for crayfish, crabs, insects and other small animals.

They are highly social, feeding and roosting in groups and often traveling between sites in lines or loose formations.

What it does here

A predator of crayfish, crabs, insects and other small animals in wet soils and shallow water.

White Ibises nest colonially in trees and shrubs near water. Documented nesting plants include black mangrove, red bay, willow, cypress and elderberry; where woody sites are limited, they may nest in dense grasses or sedges. Wet lawns and low marsh vegetation also create the soft feeding ground where they probe for prey.

Native to the southeastern United States, Caribbean, Central America and northern South America. In Florida it is a familiar year-round resident of wetlands, shorelines, parks and wet lawns.

Florida folklore says the ibis is the last wildlife to seek shelter before a hurricane and the first to return afterward. The University of Miami cites that tradition as part of the reason the ibis became the Hurricanes' mascot.
