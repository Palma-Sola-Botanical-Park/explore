Downy Woodpecker

Dryobates pubescens
On this page

At a glance

  • The smallest woodpecker in North America — barely bigger than a sparrow.
  • Males wear a small red patch on the back of the head. Females have none.
  • Its size lets it work thin branches and weed stems that heavier woodpeckers cannot balance on.
  • In cooler months it travels with mixed flocks of chickadees, titmice and nuthatches.

How to know it

Size

About six inches — the smallest woodpecker in North America.

Bill

Short and stubby, clearly shorter than the head is wide.

Pattern

Black and white with a white stripe down the back. The male adds a small red patch on the nape.

Voice

A high, sharp pik, and a whinnying rattle that falls away at the end.

It taps lightly rather than hammering, and works thin branches and dead stems as often as trunks.

Easily confused with

Hairy Woodpecker — Hairy Woodpeckers look almost identical but are noticeably larger, with a bill about as long as the head is wide. The Downy's bill is short and stubby by comparison.

Where to find it here

Scan thin branches, dead limbs and tall weed stems, not just trunks. Listen for the sharp pik. In cooler months it usually turns up inside a moving flock of small birds rather than on its own.

A year-round resident, active through the day.

How it lives

A quiet, methodical worker. It moves along thin branches and weed stems, tapping lightly rather than hammering, probing for insects and larvae. It will take seeds and suet too.

In winter it joins mixed flocks — chickadees, titmice, warblers — moving through the trees together. Several pairs of eyes find more food and spot more danger than one.

Dead wood is the nest

It chisels a cavity into a standing dead limb, and when the pair has finished with it, smaller birds that cannot dig their own take it over.

What it does here

Being small is the whole strategy. A Downy is light enough to cling to twigs, dead flower stalks and goldenrod stems, which puts the insects wintering inside plant stems within reach — food that heavier woodpeckers cannot get at.

It also needs dead wood. Nest cavities are chiselled into standing dead limbs and snags, and those holes keep working long after the woodpecker has moved on: chickadees, wrens and other small birds cannot dig their own and take over old ones. A dead limb left standing is still doing a job.
