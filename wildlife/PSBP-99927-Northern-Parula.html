Northern Parula

Setophaga americana
On this page

At a glance

  • Northern Parulas are tiny blue-gray and yellow warblers, often easier to hear in the canopy than to follow with your eyes.
  • In the southern United States, females often build inside hanging Spanish moss, hollowing a small chamber within the strands and lining it with finer material.
  • They search the outer foliage for insects, sometimes hanging beneath leaves or twig tips to reach prey on the underside.
  • The song rises as a buzzy trill and ends abruptly with a sharp final note, a pattern that can reveal the bird high overhead before you see it.

How to know it

Summary

A very small warbler with blue-gray upperparts, a greenish patch on the back, yellow throat and breast, two white wing bars and broken white crescents around the eyes. Adult males also show a dark and chestnut band across the breast.

Voice

The song is a rising buzzy trill that accelerates and ends abruptly with a sharper final note. Calls are short chips.

Size

About four and a half inches long, with a wingspan around seven inches.

What to look for

Look high in mature trees for a tiny blue-gray and yellow bird working the outer leaves and branch tips. Broken white eye crescents and two white wing bars are useful clues. Listen for the rising buzzy song when birds are vocal.

Easily confused with

Yellow-throated Warbler — Yellow-throated Warblers are larger, with a longer bill, black facial markings and no broken white eye crescents. Northern Parulas are much smaller and more compact.

Where to find it here

Look in mature trees and wooded edges, scanning the middle and upper canopy. During migration and winter, Northern Parulas use a wide variety of wooded habitats, including parks and gardens.

Northern Parulas occur in Florida during migration and winter, and they breed in parts of the state. Palma Sola currently has only one observation event, in March, so local seasonal timing is not yet established.

When

Recorded at Palma Sola during spring migration. Local observations are too limited to define a reliable seasonal pattern.

How it lives

It moves through outer foliage, picking insects from leaves and sometimes hovering or hanging underneath branch tips to reach prey.

Its diet is mostly insects and spiders, especially caterpillars and other small arthropods taken from foliage.

What it does here

A small canopy predator of insects and spiders. Parulas in turn become prey for larger birds and other predators.

Spanish moss is nesting habitat, not decoration, for Northern Parulas in the southern part of their breeding range. The female hollows a chamber inside a hanging clump and lines it with finer material. Spanish moss is an epiphyte rather than a true moss and takes support — not food — from the tree, so a mature tree draped with healthy Spanish moss can provide nesting structure without being harmed by it.

Native to the Americas. Northern Parulas breed across much of eastern North America, including parts of Florida, and winter mainly in southern Florida, the Caribbean and Central America.
