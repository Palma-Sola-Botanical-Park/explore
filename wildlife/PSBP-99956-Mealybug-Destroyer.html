Mealybug Destroyer

Cryptolaemus montrouzieri
On this page

At a glance

  • A small dark ladybird beetle with a rusty-orange head and tail, working over a plant looking for mealybugs.
  • The larva is covered in white waxy filaments and closely resembles the mealybugs it hunts, letting it feed among them.
  • It was brought from Australia in 1891 to control mealybugs and is now one of the oldest successes in biological pest control.
  • Both the larva and the adult eat the same prey — mealybugs, soft scales and their eggs.

How to know it

Adult

Small and oval, dark brown to black with a rusty-orange head and rear end.

Larva

Covered in white, waxy filaments — easily mistaken for a large, fuzzy mealybug.

Size

Tiny, only a fraction of an inch.

What to look for

Check plants that already have mealybugs. The white fuzzy thing that is moving purposefully rather than sitting still is the beetle larva, not the pest.

Where to find it here

On plantings affected by mealybugs or scale, especially in warmer, sheltered parts of the garden.

Active in the warm season and year-round in mild, sheltered spots.

How it lives

Active by day, searching foliage for colonies of mealybugs and soft scale. Larva and adult both feed steadily on the pests and their eggs.

Hiding in plain sight

The larva's waxy coat makes it look like its prey, so it can feed inside a mealybug colony without being conspicuous — to the mealybugs' predators or to a gardener glancing at the leaf.

What it does here

A predator of mealybugs and soft scales, and one of the reasons an infested plant may recover without treatment.

Introduced on purpose

Brought from Australia in 1891 to fight citrus mealybugs in California, it has been a standard ally in groves, greenhouses and gardens ever since. It specialises narrowly on soft scale pests rather than spreading into the wider insect community.

Take care

Harmless to people.

Leave it alone and let it work. Before treating a mealybug problem, check whether these beetles are already on the plant.

Conservation

Not a conservation concern. Common, and deliberately maintained in agriculture and horticulture.
