Mealybug Destroyer

Cryptolaemus montrouzieri

At a glance

  • A small dark ladybird beetle with a rusty-orange head and tail, working over a plant, is a mealybug destroyer — a beetle introduced on purpose to fight a pest.
  • It was brought from Australia over a century ago to control mealybugs and scale insects, and it has been doing the job ever since.
  • Its larva is a clever disguise artist: covered in white waxy fluff, it looks almost exactly like the mealybugs it eats, hiding in plain sight among them.
  • Despite being introduced, it is a welcome helper, not an invader — it sticks to soft scale pests and does not run wild.

How to know it

Adult

Small, oval, dark brown to black with a rusty-orange head and rear end.

Larva

Covered in white, waxy filaments — looks like a large, fuzzy mealybug.

Size

Tiny, only a few millimeters long.

Size

about 0.15 inches

What to look for

A small dark beetle with orange ends moving over plants where mealybugs or scale insects gather — or its fuzzy white larva creeping among the pests it mimics. The larva's mealybug disguise is the memorable clue.

Where to find it here

On plantings affected by mealybugs or scale, especially in the warmer, sheltered parts of the garden. Look for the fuzzy white larva among the pests.

Active in the warm season and year-round in mild, sheltered spots.

When

Year-round only in mild, sheltered spots.

How it lives

A beetle that eats mealybugs, and it works at it all day — searching foliage for colonies of mealybugs and soft scale and eating them, eggs included. The larva does the same.

Slow-moving and completely harmless to handle. If you find one on a plant with an infestation, it is doing you a favor.

What it does here

Native to Australia, introduced into the United States in 1891 as a biological control agent and now established in warm regions including Florida, where it lives wherever mealybugs and soft scales occur.

A beneficial predator that controls mealybugs and soft scales, widely used to protect plants without chemicals.

Found on plants infested with mealybugs or soft scale — ornamentals, citrus, and many others — where its prey lives.

The mealybug destroyer is one of the early success stories of biological control. A ladybird beetle from Australia, it was imported in 1891 to fight citrus mealybugs in California and has since become a standard ally in greenhouses, groves, and gardens across the warm parts of the country.

Both the adult and the larva are predators of mealybugs, soft scales, and their eggs. The larva is the strange one: it grows a coat of white waxy filaments that makes it look uncannily like an oversized mealybug, a disguise that lets it feed right inside a colony of its prey.

Unlike many introduced insects, this one is considered beneficial rather than invasive. It specializes on soft-bodied scale pests, does not damage plants, and does not displace native wildlife in any notable way, which is why growers still release it deliberately.
