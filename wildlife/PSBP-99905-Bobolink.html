Bobolink

Dolichonyx oryzivorus
On this page

At a glance

  • A Bobolink may fly about 12,500 miles in a year, between grasslands in northern North America and southern South America.
  • Spring males are black below with a pale back and a buff nape. By fall they have molted into streaky brown and look much like the females.
  • Florida sits on a major Bobolink route — birds cross here rather than following the mainland down through Mexico.
  • They replace every feather twice a year: once after breeding, and again in South America before heading north.

How to know it

Size and shape

Bigger than a sparrow, smaller than a Red-winged Blackbird. Compact, fairly short-tailed, with a stubby pointed bill.

Spring male

Black below with a pale back, white shoulders and a buff patch on the back of the head. Dark underneath and pale on top is an unusual combination, and it makes spring males unmistakable.

Female and fall male

Buffy brown with dark streaking, a dark line through the eye and a pale stripe along the crown. The bill is pale pink.

Voice

A short metallic pink, often from birds passing overhead.

In fall the streaky brown birds are easy to walk straight past. Listening does more than looking.

Where to find it here

Open grassy areas, wet meadows and weedy edges — not the wooded parts of the park. Feeding flocks drop low among grasses and seed heads and can disappear into the vegetation until something puts them up.

When

A passage migrant only. Look from late April into May, and again from August into October. Many travel at night and come down to feed during the day.

How it lives

Outside the breeding season, Bobolinks often travel in flocks. They migrate largely at night and stop in fields and marshy grasslands during the day to feed and rest.

Their diet changes with the season. During breeding they eat mostly insects and feed insects to their young. During migration and winter they rely much more heavily on seeds and grain.

What it does here

Bobolinks are not here to stay. Florida is a crossing point on a route between northern grasslands and southern South America, and a bird in the park is refuelling between legs of that trip.

Grass carries them at every stage — insects while they are breeding, then the seeds of grasses and sedges through migration and winter. On the northern breeding grounds the female hides her nest on the ground beneath dense cover.

The scientific name oryzivorus means roughly rice eater. Migrating flocks once came down on southern rice fields in such numbers that the birds were known as Ricebirds and treated as pests.

Take care

Conservation

Bobolinks are still widespread, but the population has fallen substantially and the 2025 State of the Birds report places them in its Orange-Alert Tipping Point category. Loss of grassland is the main concern. Stopover habitat matters for a bird making a trip this long, so keeping to the paths and out of grass where migrants may be feeding is worth doing.
