Bobolink

Dolichonyx oryzivorus

At a glance

  • The breeding male is the only North American bird with black underparts and a white back — an upside-down tuxedo that makes him unmistakable in spring. By fall he molts into the same streaky buff-and-brown plumage as the female, and migrating flocks passing through Florida are easily mistaken for sparrows.
  • The Bobolink makes one of the longest round-trip migrations of any songbird in the Western Hemisphere — roughly 12,500 miles each year between northern grasslands and the pampas of southern South America. A bird that lives nine or ten years may travel the equivalent of traveling around the earth four or five times.
  • Florida is not a destination for Bobolinks — it is a crossroads. The vast majority of the eastern population funnels through the state twice a year, southbound in late summer and northbound in spring, making it one of the best places in North America to see this species without ever leaving the peninsula.
  • To navigate a journey that spans two continents, Bobolinks carry internal compasses: iron oxide deposits in nasal bristles let them sense the earth's magnetic field. They also steer by the night sky, using a learned star compass calibrated against the magnetic compass — a system that must adapt as the entire sky changes after the bird crosses the equator into the southern hemisphere.

How to know it

Size and shape

A compact, flat-headed songbird, larger than a sparrow but smaller than a Red-winged Blackbird. Length 6.3 to 7.1 inches; wingspan 10.6 to 11.8 inches. The bill is short and conical, the tail short with distinctly pointed feathers — a useful detail at close range.

Breeding male

Unmistakable: entirely black below, with a bold white lower back and rump, white shoulder patches, and a rich cream or buff nape. No other North American bird is dark below and pale above. The black bill is also distinctive in this plumage. This striking alternate plumage is grown through a complete molt on the South American wintering grounds — one of two full molts the species completes every year.

Female and nonbreeding male

Warm buffy-brown overall, with dark brown streaks on the back and flanks. The head shows a dark crown with a pale central stripe, a dark eye-line, and a clean, unstreaked nape — that clear nape is the best mark separating this plumage from streaky sparrows. The bill is pinkish. This basic plumage is the result of the post-breeding complete molt, after which the bird's feathers are fully renewed for the long migration south.

Juvenile

Similar to female but slightly paler and with less defined streaking. The unstreaked nape is still present and useful for identification.

In flight

Breeding males flash the white back and rump prominently. In nonbreeding plumage, flocks look sparrow-like in flight but tend to move in loose, rolling groups with a buoyant, slightly undulating style. Listen for the soft pink call note, which flocks give almost continuously.

Voice

The male's song is one of the most distinctive in North American ornithology — a long, cascading, electronic-sounding series of 25 to 50 notes that tumble across a wide pitch range, mixing buzzy low tones with bright, sharp highs. It is frequently delivered in flight and carries well across open ground. Both sexes give a soft, metallic pink or bink call year-round; this note — often the first sound you notice from a passing flock — is especially useful for picking out migrants in mixed flocks or overhead. Only males sing the full display song.

Size

6.3 to 7.1 inches; wingspan 10.6 to 11.8 inches; weight 1.0 to 2.1 oz, varying by sex and season

What to look for

Spring (northbound, late April to mid-May): the breeding male's reversed tuxedo is unlike anything else in Florida — jet black below, brilliant white above, with a creamy-yellow patch glowing on the back of the neck. Fall (southbound, August through October): look for compact, flat-headed birds in buffy streaked brown moving in loose flocks; the key mark is a clean, unstreaked nape and a pinkish bill that separates them from the many sparrows they superficially resemble. By ear, year-round: a soft, metallic pink or bink call note, given almost continuously by passing flocks, is usually the first clue a group is overhead — hear it, then look up.

Where to find it here

Scan the open grassy areas and the margins of the park's lakes, particularly in the early morning after a night of migration. Flocks often perch low in tall grasses or on weed stalks while feeding, and can be surprisingly inconspicuous until flushed. Listen first: the soft pink call note often betrays a flock before you can see the birds. In spring, the possibility of a male in breeding plumage — jet black below, white above — makes the search especially worthwhile.

A migrant visitor to Manatee County, not a resident. Spring passage typically runs from late April through mid-May; fall passage runs from mid-August through October, with peak numbers in September. Florida is one of the primary corridors for the eastern population on both legs of the journey. Bobolinks migrate nocturnally, so the birds that appear in fields and wetlands by day are resting and refueling after a night's flight. Morning is by far the best time to look.

When

Strictly a passage migrant in Manatee County — not a resident at any season. Spring birds move through late April to mid-May, typically in smaller numbers and often in breeding plumage. Fall passage is larger and more protracted, running mid-August through October, with September the peak month. Birds are most detectable in the morning after overnight flights.

How it lives

Bobolinks travel in company. Outside the breeding season they move in flocks that can run to hundreds, flying at night and dropping into fields and marsh edges by day to refuel.

What they eat flips with the season. Through breeding it is mostly insects — beetles, grasshoppers, caterpillars, spiders — and nestlings get almost nothing else. On migration and through winter it reverses to seeds and grain.

The spring display

Males hover low over the territory in a slow, stiff-winged flutter, singing the whole time. One male may hold ground covering more than one nest; the females build hidden nests in dense grass and do all the incubating.

What it does here

As migrants, Bobolinks connect grassland and wetland ecosystems across two continents, consuming and dispersing the seeds of grasses and weeds, and controlling invertebrate populations during their breeding season in northern meadows. During Florida passage, flocks move through fields and wet edges where they take insects and seeds, briefly occupying a consumer role in the local food web. Raptors — including merlins, which are themselves migrants — take Bobolinks during passage. The species is also a host for cowbird parasitism on its breeding grounds, connecting it to broader nest-parasite dynamics in North American grasslands.

The Bobolink breeds across a broad swath of the northern United States and southern Canada, roughly between 40 and 50 degrees north latitude, in grasslands and hayfields from the Great Plains east to the Atlantic coast. It winters in the grasslands, rice fields, and marshes of Bolivia, Paraguay, and Argentina, well south of the equator. Florida is neither breeding nor wintering ground for this species — it is the primary passage corridor. The eastern population migrates almost entirely through Florida and the West Indies rather than overland through Mexico or Central America, making Florida disproportionately important to the species. Manatee County lies directly in this flyway, and Bobolinks pass through twice annually.

Few birds demonstrate how thoroughly a single species can transform its appearance between seasons. The spring male is a showstopper — jet black below, cream-and-white above, with a rich buff nape that gleams in sunlight.

By late summer he has molted into the same streaky, sparrow-like plumage as the female, and the migrating flocks that drift through Florida in August and September look nothing like the birds that passed through in May. The call — a soft, metallic pink — is often the first and only clue that a flock overhead is Bobolinks at all.

What drives this dramatic transformation is one of the most demanding molt schedules of any songbird. Bobolinks undergo two complete molts every year — one on the breeding grounds after nesting, and a second full molt on the South American wintering grounds — a trait shared with almost no other bird of comparable size.

The abrasive grasslands and open pampas they inhabit on both continents degrade feathers rapidly, and the exposure to intense ultraviolet radiation in open habitats compounds the wear.

The species is polygynous, and males may hold territories with more than one nesting female. Bobolinks were also among the first birds in which multiple paternity within a single clutch was documented: a female may mate with more than one male, so a single nest can hold nestlings sired by different fathers. Studies found that roughly 28% of nests contained at least one extra-pair offspring.

Females do all incubating, but both parents feed nestlings, which are given insects almost exclusively.

The family groups that form after fledging quickly merge with others, and by the time fall migration begins the birds are moving in loose, sociable flocks.

The Bobolink's scientific name, oryzivorus, translates roughly as 'rice eater,' a name earned during its passage through the rice-growing regions of the South, where large fall flocks once descended on fields in great numbers. That history made the species deeply unpopular with farmers for centuries. Today the rice-field stops are mostly behind it, but the name — and the memory — persist.

Take care

Conservation

Least Concern (IUCN), but listed as an Orange Alert Tipping Point species by the 2025 State of the Birds report, indicating a population loss of more than 50% over the past 50 years. Also listed on the U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service Birds of Conservation Concern 2021. The primary driver of decline is habitat loss: the disappearance of grasslands, damp meadows, and traditional hayfields across the breeding range. Not federally listed as threatened or endangered, and not specially listed in Florida, but a species of genuine and growing conservation concern.
