Pileated Woodpecker

Dryocopus pileatus
On this page

At a glance

  • A Pileated Woodpecker is nearly crow-sized, mostly black, with a tall red crest and bold white stripes on the face and neck.
  • Its loud laughing calls, heavy chopping and deep drumming often carry through the trees before the bird comes into view.
  • Pileated Woodpeckers make distinctive rectangular excavations in dead and decaying wood while following carpenter-ant tunnels.
  • Large cavities excavated by Pileated Woodpeckers can later provide roosting or nesting space for animals that cannot carve such holes themselves.

How to know it

Size and shape

Nearly crow-sized, with a long neck and tall red crest.

Color

Mostly black with bold white stripes on the face and neck; large white patches show in the wings during flight.

Male and female

Males have a red cheek or 'mustache' stripe; females have black there.

Sign left behind

Large rectangular or oblong excavations in dead or decaying wood.

Voice

A loud, laughing series of calls plus deep, rolling drumming. Even the chopping sounds of feeding can carry through the woods.

What to look for

Look for a crow-sized black woodpecker with a red crest on large trunks, snags or fallen logs. Rectangular excavations are strong evidence that a Pileated Woodpecker has been feeding there.

Easily confused with

Red-bellied Woodpecker — Much smaller and lacks the Pileated's long pointed crest and large white wing patches. Pileated Woodpeckers are nearly crow-sized and leave much larger rectangular excavations.

Downy Woodpecker — Tiny by comparison — sparrow-sized rather than crow-sized.

Where to find it here

Check the park's largest trees, standing dead trunks, stumps and fallen logs. Look for rectangular feeding holes and listen for the loud call or deep drumming.

A year-round Florida resident. Palma Sola currently has only one documented observation, so sightings here should be considered occasional.

How it lives

Forages on standing and fallen wood, chiseling deeply into decaying areas for carpenter ants and wood-boring beetle larvae. Other insects, fruit and nuts are also eaten.

Usually encountered alone or in pairs.

The excavations are rectangular, and nothing else here makes them. Working with a chisel-tipped bill and a long barbed tongue, it digs deep squared-off holes into rotting wood after carpenter ants and beetle larvae. A tree it has worked over can look as though someone took a hand axe to it.

What it does here

Its excavations change the physical structure of dead wood. Feeding holes expose insect-rich wood, while larger abandoned cavities can later be used by other birds and mammals.

Dead wood is habitat

Large trees and dead wood are central to the Pileated Woodpecker's life. It digs for ants and beetle larvae in decaying trunks, snags, stumps and fallen logs, and excavates nesting cavities in large trees — often dead ones. Retaining safe standing snags and fallen wood therefore preserves habitat that living ornamental trees alone do not provide.

The holes outlast the bird. Wood Ducks, screech-owls, flying squirrels and a long list of others nest in abandoned pileated cavities they could never cut for themselves, which makes this one woodpecker a housing supply for much of the forest around it.

Take care

Watch from a distance and leave feeding trees, cavities and dead wood undisturbed.

Conservation

Widespread and currently of low conservation concern. Large trees, snags and downed wood remain important habitat.
