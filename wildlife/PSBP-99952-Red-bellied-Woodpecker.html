Red-bellied Woodpecker

Melanerpes carolinus
On this page

At a glance

  • Despite the name, the red belly is just a faint blush you rarely see — what catches your eye is the vivid red cap running from bill to nape on the male.
  • It is one of Florida's most common woodpeckers, turning up in parks, yards, and woodlands alike.
  • It wedges nuts and seeds into bark crevices to hold them steady while hammering them open — a built-in vise.
  • Its long, barbed tongue can extend nearly two inches past the tip of its bill to extract insects from deep inside wood.

How to know it

Head

Bold red stripe from bill to nape (male) or just on the nape (female). Black-and-white barred back.

Belly

Faint pinkish wash — rarely visible in the field despite the name.

Size

About 9 inches, roughly robin-sized.

Sound

A rolling 'churr' call and steady drumming on wood.

Voice

A rolling, somewhat nasal 'churr-churr-churr' and steady drumming. One of the more vocal woodpeckers.

Size

9 to 10.5 inches; about 16 inches

What to look for

A medium woodpecker with a barred black-and-white back and a red cap, hitching up a trunk or clinging to a branch. Listen for the distinctive rolling call.

Where to find it here

Listen for drumming and the rolling call; scan tree trunks and larger branches in the canopy.

Year-round resident, active by day in every season.

How it lives

It hitches up and around trunks and branches, probing bark for insects and spiders, and flies between trees in a deep undulating line.

Insects, seeds, nuts, fruit and the occasional small vertebrate. It wedges food into a bark crevice to hold it still while it works, and stores food the same way.

What it does here

Controls bark-dwelling insects; its nest cavities are later used by other species including screech-owls, squirrels, and small birds.

Feeds on native fruits and berries; nests in dead wood of oaks, palms, and other trees.

Native throughout the eastern United States. A year-round resident in Florida, common in woodlands, suburbs, and parks.

The red-bellied woodpecker is one of those birds whose name sends people looking for the wrong field mark. The belly has a faint pinkish wash, but it is the bold red stripe on the head that identifies this bird — running from the bill to the nape on males, limited to the nape on females.

It is an adaptable, opportunistic feeder: insects, spiders, nuts, seeds, fruit, and occasionally tree frogs or small lizards. That flexibility is part of why it thrives in suburban Florida.

Its undulating flight — a few quick wingbeats, then a fold and a dip — is typical of woodpeckers and easy to spot crossing an open space.
