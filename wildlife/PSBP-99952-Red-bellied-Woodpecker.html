Red-bellied Woodpecker

Melanerpes carolinus
On this page

At a glance

  • The name misleads. The red belly is a faint wash that is rarely visible; the obvious red runs over the crown and nape.
  • Males carry red from the bill right back over the crown; females show red only on the nape.
  • It wedges food into bark crevices to hold it steady while feeding, and stores seeds and nuts the same way.
  • The cavities it excavates are later used by screech-owls, squirrels and small birds that cannot dig their own.

How to know it

Head

A bold red stripe from bill to nape in males, or red on the nape alone in females.

Back

Finely barred black and white, a pattern often called ladder-backed.

Belly

A faint pinkish wash that is rarely visible in the field, despite the name.

Voice

A rolling, nasal churr-churr-churr, plus steady drumming. One of the more vocal woodpeckers here.

Size

About ten inches long, with a wingspan around sixteen inches.

What to look for

Scan trunks and larger branches rather than twigs. Listen for the rolling call or drumming, which usually arrives before the bird does.

Easily confused with

Pileated Woodpecker — Pileated Woodpeckers are nearly crow-sized with a tall red crest and large white wing patches. Red-bellied Woodpeckers are much smaller, with a barred back and red confined to the head.

Where to find it here

Listen for drumming and the rolling call, then scan tree trunks and the larger branches in the canopy.

A year-round resident, active by day in every season.

How it lives

Hitches up and around trunks and branches, probing bark for insects and spiders. Its flight between trees is deeply undulating.

A vice made of bark

It jams food into a crevice to hold it steady while working on it, and stores seeds and nuts the same way for later.

What it does here

A predator of bark-dwelling insects that also takes seeds, nuts and fruit.

The holes outlast the bird

Nest cavities are excavated in dead or dying wood, including oaks and palms. Once abandoned they are taken over by screech-owls, squirrels and small birds that cannot excavate their own — so a dead limb left standing keeps working for years after the woodpecker has gone.

Take care

No meaningful danger to people.

Watch from a distance and leave dead wood and cavity trees undisturbed.

Conservation

Widespread and not currently considered threatened.
