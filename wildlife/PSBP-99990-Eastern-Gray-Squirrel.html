Eastern Gray Squirrel

Sciurus carolinensis
On this page

At a glance

  • The eastern gray squirrel is the bushy-tailed acrobat of Florida's oaks — equally at home leaping through the canopy and raiding a bird feeder.
  • It is a dedicated scatter-hoarder, burying thousands of nuts one at a time and relying on memory and smell to find them again later.
  • It never finds them all — and the nuts it forgets sprout into trees, making the squirrel an accidental but genuine forest planter.
  • It can head down a tree trunk face-first, thanks to ankles that rotate almost backward to grip the bark.

How to know it

Color

Usually gray above with a paler, whitish belly; some individuals are unusually dark or reddish-brown.

Tail

Long, broad, and very bushy, often held in a curve over the back.

Size

Clearly larger than a chipmunk, smaller than a cat.

Voice

Surprisingly vocal — sharp kuk-kuk barks and chatters, plus a buzzy alarm call given with flicks of the tail to warn others of danger.

Size

17 to 20 inches, including the tail

What to look for

A gray, bushy-tailed rodent bounding across the ground or spiraling up an oak. The big, flicking tail and the habit of freezing, then dashing, are dead giveaways.

Easily confused with

Fox Squirrel — Florida's fox squirrel is much larger, often black-and-tan with a dark face and pale nose and ears; the gray squirrel is smaller and uniformly gray.

Where to find it here

In and under the oaks and other large trees — on the ground foraging, racing up trunks, or leaping between branches. Active throughout the day.

Year-round resident, active in every season and through most of the daylight hours.

How it lives

Active all year — it does not hibernate — and the day goes on foraging, caching food, and chasing other squirrels through the branches. It stays alert the whole time for hawks and owls.

Mostly acorns, nuts and seeds, plus buds, flowers, fungi, berries and the occasional insect or egg. Feeders and picnic leftovers get taken as readily.

Dreys

The leafy ball high in a fork is a squirrel nest, built of leaves and twigs.

What it does here

A keystone tree-planter: by burying and forgetting nuts, gray squirrels help oaks and other trees regenerate. They are also important prey for hawks, owls, and snakes, linking the treetops to the rest of the food web.

Oaks and other nut trees — the squirrel eats their acorns and nuts and, by burying and forgetting them, helps plant new trees.

Native to the eastern United States, including all of Florida. It is a familiar year-round resident of forests, parks, and neighborhoods anywhere there are trees, especially oaks.

The gray squirrel's whole lifestyle revolves around food storage. In fall it gathers acorns and other nuts and buries them one by one across its territory — a strategy called scatter-hoarding — then recovers them through winter using a sharp memory and an even sharper sense of smell.

It is far from perfect at the recovery part, and that is good news for the forest. The acorns a squirrel forgets are effectively planted, buried at just the right depth to sprout. In a very real sense, some of the oaks shading this park owe their start to generations of forgetful squirrels.

They are also built for life in the trees. A gray squirrel can rotate its hind feet nearly 180 degrees, letting it grip the bark and head down a trunk face-first, and its long tail serves as a counterbalance, a rudder in mid-leap, a sunshade, and a blanket.

Take care

What to do

Enjoy them, but do not hand-feed. Fed squirrels lose their wariness, can become nippy, and a human diet is not good for them.
