Eastern Gray Squirrel

Sciurus carolinensis
On this page

At a glance

  • Eastern Gray Squirrels spend much of the day moving between the ground and tree canopy, feeding heavily on acorns, nuts, seeds, buds and fruit.
  • Instead of storing all its food in one place, a Gray Squirrel buries nuts separately across its home range — a strategy called scatter-hoarding.
  • Some buried acorns are never recovered. Those that survive can germinate, making squirrels important dispersers of oak seeds.
  • Its hind feet can rotate far enough backward to grip bark while the squirrel climbs down a trunk headfirst.

How to know it

Color

Usually gray above and whitish below. Some individuals can be darker or show reddish tones.

Tail

Long and very bushy, often held curved over the back when the animal pauses.

Size

Much larger than a chipmunk and smaller than a house cat.

Voice

Sharp barks, chatters and buzzy alarm calls, often accompanied by rapid tail flicking.

What to look for

Look for a gray, bushy-tailed squirrel feeding beneath trees, running along branches or spiraling around a trunk. Gray Squirrels can descend a trunk headfirst, a useful clue when you see one moving vertically.

Where to find it here

Look in and beneath large trees, especially oaks. Squirrels forage on the ground for fallen nuts, run along trunks and branches, and build leafy nests high in the canopy.

A year-round Florida resident active during daylight. The park's formal observation record is sparse and probably underrepresents this familiar species.

How it lives

Much of the year revolves around stored food. A squirrel carries individual nuts away from the parent tree and buries them in separate caches, returning later to recover many of them.

Its feet are adapted for climbing in either direction on vertical bark, and the long tail helps with balance while running and leaping through branches.

Active by day throughout the year. Gray Squirrels build leafy nests high in branches and also use tree cavities for shelter.

What it does here

An important disperser of acorns and other large seeds. Buried nuts that are not recovered may germinate away from the parent tree. Gray Squirrels are also prey for hawks, owls, snakes and other predators.

The oak and the squirrel

Trees provide nearly everything the Gray Squirrel needs. Oaks and other nut-bearing trees supply much of its food; branches provide travel routes and support leafy nests; cavities offer shelter. By carrying and burying acorns away from the parent tree, squirrels also influence where new oaks become established.

Take care

Do not handle or hand-feed squirrels. A wild squirrel can bite or scratch if cornered or accustomed to taking food from people. Keep dogs from chasing wildlife.

Watch without hand-feeding. Feeding encourages squirrels to approach people and to seek food from them persistently.

Conservation

Widespread and not currently considered threatened.
