Antlions and Owlflies

Myrmeleontidae

At a glance

  • Some antlion larvae build funnel-shaped traps in loose sand. The walls are steep enough to collapse under struggling prey, and the buried larva can flick sand upward to knock an escaping insect back toward its jaws.
  • A Bright Futures student first showed us these animals at Palma Sola. He noticed the small pits in the sand, explained what made them, and uncovered a larva for us to photograph.
  • Florida has 22 antlion species in nine genera, giving the state the richest antlion fauna in the eastern United States.

How to know it

Look for the pit

The easiest sign is a small funnel-shaped pit in dry, loose sand. The larva waits buried at the bottom, usually with only its curved jaws near the surface.

If you see the larva

The larva is squat and gray-brown, with a broad head and a pair of long, curved jaws. Many are around half an inch long.

Adult antlion

Adults look somewhat like drab damselflies, with long net-veined wings and clubbed antennae. They often come to lights at night.

Owlfly

Owlflies belong to the same family but hunt flying insects at dusk. They look dragonfly-like, with a wingspan to about four inches and antennae nearly as long as the body — no Florida dragonfly has antennae anything like that.

Size

Larvae are often around half an inch long. Adults are much larger, with long wings and slender bodies.

What to look for

Look for the pits rather than the animal. Small cones in dry sand under an overhang or at the base of a tree each mark one larva waiting below.

Where to find it here

Look for small funnel-shaped pits in dry, loose sand protected from rain — under overhangs, beneath trees, beside structures and along sheltered sandy path edges. Adult antlions may come to lights after dark.

Larval pits can be found year-round where dry sand stays protected from rain. Adults are most likely on warm nights.

When

Pits persist wherever sheltered dry sand does. Heavy rain erodes them, and they are rebuilt afterward.

How it lives

The larva waits buried at the bottom of the pit. When a small insect slides within reach, the antlion grips it with hollow jaws, injects digestive fluids, and draws the liquefied tissues back through the jaws.

Two different adults

Adult antlions are weak, erratic fliers that come to lights at night and spend the day motionless on a dry stem or strip of bark. Owlflies hunt on the wing at dusk and dawn much as dragonflies do, then perch by day with the abdomen cocked up, doing a convincing impression of a broken twig.

What it does here

Plants help create the sheltered ground these larvae need. Dry sand beneath broad tree canopies, exposed roots, overhangs and other rain-protected spots can remain suitable for pits when surrounding ground is wet.

Antlions live on every continent except Antarctica and do best in warm, dry, sandy country. Sandy peninsular Florida suits them, and every species here is native.

Owlflies were traditionally placed in their own family. Genetic studies now place them within the antlion family, which is why both groups may appear together in modern classifications.
