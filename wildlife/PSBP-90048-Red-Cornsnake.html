Red Cornsnake

Pantherophis guttatus
On this page

At a glance

  • It eases off when the fight is over. In laboratory feeding tests, cornsnakes loosened their squeeze once the prey stopped moving.
  • A buzz with no rattle. Cornered, it may vibrate its tail against dry leaves, and the leaves make the buzzing sound.
  • A climber that works nights. It climbs well and does most of its hunting after dark.
  • No venom. It kills larger prey by wrapping it in its coils.

How to know it

Blotches edged in black

Down the back runs a row of red, orange or brown blotches, each outlined in black, on an orange to brownish body. On the head and neck sits a dark mark shaped like a spearpoint. Adults are commonly about two and a half to four feet long, and young snakes can be browner than the bright adults.

Underneath

The belly is usually checkered black and white. You will only see it in a photo of a snake that has turned on its own; there is no need to pick one up to check.

Not the venomous ones

Compare it with two venomous snakes. A coralsnake wears rings of red, black and yellow that go all the way around its body, and its snout is black; a cornsnake wears blotches, not rings. A copperhead has hourglass-shaped bands, and in Florida copperheads live only in the Panhandle. Don't trust a color rhyme to tell you a snake is safe to touch.

Two other snakes on our list

The North American Racer is plain and dark as an adult. The Florida Watersnake is stouter, with crossbands and rough, ridged scales.

Where to find it here

The day it jumped

In October 2026 a group of us was weeding and putting in new plants along the bank of the Big Pond behind the Galleria. A cornsnake shot out of the plants already growing there and splashed into the water, which startled all of us. Then it stopped. It lay in the shallows so still it looked dead, and it stayed that way the whole time we worked, watching us, as if it were waiting for us to leave.

That was one snake on one day, not a regular spot. Cornsnakes spend most of their time under cover and hunt mostly after dark, so seeing one is luck.

How it lives

How the squeeze works

Larger prey gets wrapped in body coils; smaller items may simply be swallowed. In laboratory tests, researchers measured the pressure of cornsnakes' coils around pre-killed rodents while making the rodents twitch. When the twitching stopped, the snakes eased off. No two squeezes looked quite the same: the loops sat at different positions and angles.

Hiding and nesting

It lives in woodlands, fields, swamps and neighborhoods, sheltering under bark, among palm fronds and beneath logs. Approached, a cornsnake may freeze, slip away into cover, or buzz its tail. Females lay their eggs in protected spots such as rotting logs, burrows and loose debris.

What it does

On the menu: rodents, birds and their eggs, lizards and frogs. In the park it has been photographed both crossing the grass and swimming through shallow water.

A name with two stories

One account says the name comes from barns and corncribs, where these snakes hunted the mice and rats drawn to stored corn. Another says it comes from the checkered belly, which looks like kernels of maize. Both are told, and nobody can say for sure which is right.
