Eastern Amberwing

Perithemis tenera

At a glance

  • A tiny dragonfly with glowing amber-orange wings hovering low over the pond — one of the smallest dragonflies in North America.
  • At about an inch long it can resemble a wasp, and its abdomen-bobbing and wing movements strengthen the resemblance.
  • Males have entirely amber wings; females show patchy brown-and-clear wings instead.
  • The aquatic young hunt mosquito larvae and other small animals below the surface.

How to know it

Size

Very small for a dragonfly — about an inch long.

Wings and body

Males have entirely amber-orange wings over a reddish-amber body. Females have patchy brown-and-clear wings.

What to look for

Look for something too small to be a dragonfly and too still to be a wasp, perched on a reed at the water's edge with orange wings catching the sun.

Where to find it here

Low over the ponds and along their planted edges on warm, sunny days. Check reeds and stems rather than the open air.

A warm-season flier, most numerous from late spring through early autumn.

How it lives

Active by day in warm, sunny weather, patrolling and perching low around still water. Males defend small territories over the surface, and the species perches far more than many dragonflies.

Adults take midges, mosquitoes and gnats in flight. The aquatic young eat mosquito larvae and other small water creatures.

A perched amberwing bobs its abdomen and flicks its wings in a way that looks a great deal like a wasp tidying itself. The resemblance is thought to make birds think twice — a harmless dragonfly borrowing a wasp's reputation.

What it does here

Perches, and needs something to perch on

Unlike dragonflies that stay airborne, this one spends much of the day gripping a stem. Emergent pondside plants are its hunting platform as much as its breeding habitat.
