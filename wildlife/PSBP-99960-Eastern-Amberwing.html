Eastern Amberwing

Perithemis tenera
On this page

At a glance

  • A tiny dragonfly with glowing amber-orange wings, hovering low over the pond, is an eastern amberwing — one of the smallest dragonflies in North America.
  • At barely an inch long, it is easy to mistake for a wasp, and that is the point: it bluffs.
  • When perched, it wags its abdomen up and down and twitches its wings in a fair imitation of a stinging wasp, discouraging predators that would not bother a harmless dragonfly.
  • Like all dragonflies, it is a precise aerial hunter, snatching small insects out of the air on the wing.

How to know it

Size

Very small for a dragonfly, about 1 inches long.

Wings

Males have entirely amber-orange wings; females have patchy brown-and-clear wings.

Body

Reddish-amber in males.

Behavior cue

Wags its abdomen and twitches its wings while perched, wasp-style.

Voice

Silent.

Size

about 1 inches; roughly 1.5 inches

What to look for

A very small dragonfly with glowing orange wings, perched on pondside vegetation or hovering low over the water. The wasp-like abdomen-wagging while perched is a giveaway.

Where to find it here

Low over the ponds and along their planted edges on warm, sunny days. Look for a tiny dragonfly with orange wings perched on a reed or stem.

A warm-season flier, most numerous from late spring through early fall.

How it lives

It patrols and perches low around still water, and it perches often — unlike some dragonflies that stay in the air almost continuously. Males defend small territories over the surface.

Midges, mosquitoes, gnats and the like, caught in flight. The aquatic young eat mosquito larvae and other small water creatures.

What it does here

A small predator at both stages of life — adults thin out flying insects, naiads eat mosquito larvae — making it a natural check on biting-insect numbers around the pond.

Breeds in ponds with submerged and emergent plants, perching on pondside stems and reeds.

Native and widespread across the eastern and central United States. In Florida it is common near still and slow waters through the warm months.

The eastern amberwing is a study in small-package boldness. Males have wings washed a warm amber-orange, unusual among local dragonflies, and a body not much over an inch long.

That small size and orange-banded look pay off as mimicry. Perched amberwings bob the abdomen and flick the wings in a way that resembles a wasp tidying itself, and the resemblance is thought to make birds and other predators think twice — a harmless dragonfly borrowing a wasp's reputation.

For all the bluff, it earns its keep as a predator. Adults hawk tiny flying insects over the water, and the aquatic young (naiads) hunt mosquito larvae and other small prey beneath the surface, making amberwings quiet allies against biting insects.
