Evening Bat

Nycticeius humeralis
On this page

At a glance

  • Evening bats emerge soon after sunset, sometimes while there is still enough light to follow them with the eye. They often forage over water and along woodland edges with a relatively slow, steady flight.
  • Females usually have twins. Together, newborn pups can weigh about half as much as their mother does after giving birth — an unusually large reproductive investment for a mammal.
  • Evening bats do not use caves. They roost in tree cavities, behind loose bark and sometimes in buildings or other structures.
  • Newborn pups are blind and nearly hairless, but they develop quickly and can fly at about three weeks old.

How to know it

At dusk

A small dark-brown bat with relatively slow, steady flight, often hunting over water or along a woodland edge.

Up close

The fur is dark brown above and paler below, with dark wings and short ears. Evening bats resemble several other Florida bats and usually cannot be identified confidently from a brief view in flight.

Voice

Its hunting calls are ultrasonic and normally beyond human hearing. A bat detector can convert them into audible sound. Mothers and pups also make social calls inside the roost.

Size

About ten to eleven inches across the wings, with a body only a few inches long. It weighs roughly a quarter to half an ounce.

What to look for

Watch the flight rather than the markings. A small bat working slowly back and forth over the same stretch of water at dusk is the likeliest thing you will see.

Where to find it here

Around sunset, watch over ponds and other open water and along edges where trees or shrubs meet open ground. Evening bats often work back and forth through the same feeding area, making them easier to follow against the sky.

Watch around dusk, when there is still enough light to see bats against the sky. Evening bats occur in Florida year-round, although activity drops on cold nights when fewer insects are flying.

When

Present all year, with activity following insect availability. Colder nights bring fewer bats out.

How it lives

It comes out early, sometimes before the light has fully gone, and hunts in two bursts — one soon after dusk and another before dawn. The flight is slow and maneuverable, which suits picking insects out of cluttered air along a wood edge or over water.

They eat flying insects, especially beetles, along with moths, flies, mosquitoes and flying ants.

A colony of relatives

Females gather in maternity colonies to raise their young. Mothers recognize their own pups by scent and voice, although females have also been observed nursing pups that are not their own. Young males disperse; daughters may return to the colony where they were born.

What it does here

Evening bats are nocturnal predators of beetles, moths and other flying insects. Their dependence on tree cavities and loose bark also makes mature trees and standing dead wood important habitat.

Trees provide the daytime shelter these bats need. In Florida they roost in cavities and behind loose bark, and also use sheltered spaces at the bases of palm fronds. Keeping mature trees and standing dead wood supports the bats as well as the insects they feed on.

The evening bat ranges across the eastern and midwestern United States from the Great Lakes south through Florida and into Veracruz, Mexico. It is a native species throughout its North American range. In Florida, it is common statewide except for the Keys, and Manatee County sits well within the heart of its Florida distribution. Northern populations migrate south in fall to overwinter in the warmer southern portions of the range; Florida bats typically move far shorter distances and may remain active year-round during mild winters.

In autumn, evening bats from the northern part of the range build fat reserves and travel south; one banded bat was recovered more than 340 miles from where it was marked. Florida's bats largely skip the journey.

Take care

The risk

Healthy evening bats avoid contact with people entirely. Like any wild mammal, a bat found on the ground or behaving abnormally during the day should not be handled with bare hands — bats are a potential rabies vector, and any bite warrants prompt medical evaluation.

What to do

Watch foraging bats from a comfortable distance at dusk. Do not attempt to handle any bat, whether it appears healthy or not. If you find one on the ground or clinging to a low surface during daylight, do not touch it; alert park staff.
