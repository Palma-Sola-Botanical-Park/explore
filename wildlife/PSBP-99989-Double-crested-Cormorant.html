Double-crested Cormorant

Nannopterum auritum
On this page

At a glance

  • A dark, heavy-bodied waterbird that dives completely underwater after fish.
  • It chases fish down with its webbed feet rather than spearing them from ambush like an Anhinga.
  • Adults grow the two small head tufts of the name only briefly in breeding season. Most of the year there is no crest to see.

How to know it

Color

Dark brown to blackish, with orange-yellow bare skin around the base of the bill.

Bill

Long and narrow with a distinct hook at the tip.

In the water

Swims low, and disappears under again and again while feeding.

The bird it gets confused with is the Anhinga. The hooked bill and the short tail settle it.

Easily confused with

Anhinga — Anhingas are slimmer, with longer fan-shaped tails and straight daggerlike bills. Double-crested Cormorants are heavier-bodied, with shorter tails and bills that hook at the tip.

Where to find it here

Scan the open pond water for a dark bird that keeps vanishing, then check snags, banks and other exposed perches where one may be resting with its wings held open.

In Florida year-round, but with only a few park records — an occasional visitor here.

How it lives

Cormorants pursue fish underwater rather than waiting for them. Their large webbed feet provide most of the propulsion, and the hooked bill grips the catch.

Because the plumage becomes waterlogged, a bird often rests afterward on an exposed perch with its wings spread while the feathers dry.

What it does here

A cormorant catches fish by running them down underwater, driving itself with big webbed feet rather than ambushing from above.

The spread-wing pose afterward is the cost of that. Cormorant feathers wet through more readily than most waterbirds', which cuts buoyancy and helps underwater, but leaves the bird needing to dry out before it flies well.

Where they nest in numbers, cormorants reshape the trees they use: droppings build up under a colony heavily enough to damage and eventually kill them, changing what grows underneath. At Palma Sola they are visitors, not a colony.
