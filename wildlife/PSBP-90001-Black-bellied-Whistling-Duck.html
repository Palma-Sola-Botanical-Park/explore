Black-bellied Whistling-Duck

Dendrocygna autumnalis
On this page

At a glance

  • Eleven Black-bellied Whistling-Ducks wintered near Sarasota in 1981–82. Several stayed through summer, the flock grew, and the species eventually spread across nearly all of Florida. The founding flock is thought to have reached Sarasota on its own, possibly across the Gulf from the Yucatán.
  • Black-bellied Whistling-Ducks regularly perch in trees, on fence rails, and even on utility lines. Their long legs and upright stance are part of why they can look more goose-like than most ducks.
  • They usually nest in tree cavities. Within a day or two of hatching, the ducklings climb to the entrance and drop to the ground, then follow their parents to feeding areas.
  • Unlike most ducks, both parents take turns incubating the eggs and both care for the ducklings.

How to know it

Size and shape

A tall, long-legged duck with a long neck and unusually upright stance. On land it can look more goose-like than most ducks.

Markings

A bright pink bill and long pink legs, a chestnut breast and back, a black belly, and a pale gray face with a neat white eye-ring. A bold white stripe crosses the wing. Males and females look alike.

Juveniles

Duller and browner, with a gray bill and pale legs, and without the sharp black belly. The white wing stripe is still there and still useful.

In flight

In flight, the neck stretches forward and the long legs trail behind the tail. A broad white wing patch becomes conspicuous.

Voice

Listen for a clear, high whistle, especially from birds in flight. A moving flock can produce a chorus loud enough to reveal the birds before you see them.

Size

about 20 inches long, with a wingspan close to three feet

What to look for

Start with the pink bill and long pink legs. In flight, look for the broad white wing patch and the legs extending beyond the tail. A flock's whistling often gives the birds away before you see them.

Easily confused with

Fulvous Whistling-Duck — Browner overall, and without the bold white wing patch. Young Black-bellied Whistling-Ducks lack the pink bill of adults, so the wing pattern is the more reliable difference.

Where to find it here

Look around shallow ponds and wet grassy edges, especially where trees stand close to the water. This species readily uses retention ponds, parks, golf courses, flooded fields and other developed landscapes. Flocks often rest near water during the day or perch in nearby trees.

Look year-round. Through the day they often rest near water; early morning and late afternoon bring more local movement. Much of their feeding happens after dark.

When

Breeding occurs in Florida, so pairs and family groups with ducklings may be seen during the warmer part of the year.

How it lives

Highly social, often gathering in noisy flocks. They spend much of the day resting near water and do much of their feeding after dark.

On land they walk easily on those long legs rather than waddling. In the air the flight is slow and shallow-winged, with the feet trailing well behind the tail.

What it does here

Nest sites are the limiting resource. Black-bellied Whistling-Ducks compete for the same scarce tree cavities that Wood Ducks want, and both species take readily to nest boxes where natural hollows are short. Eggs and ducklings are heavily preyed on by snakes, raccoons and other wetland hunters.

Trees provide both nest sites and daytime perches. The ducks usually nest in natural cavities, while grasses, sedges and aquatic plants around wetlands provide much of their food.

Too many eggs for one mother

Females sometimes lay in one another's nests. When several contribute to the same cavity, it can hold fifty or more eggs — far beyond what a single pair could produce, let alone incubate.
