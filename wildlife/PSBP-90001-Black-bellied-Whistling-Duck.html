Black-bellied Whistling-Duck

Dendrocygna autumnalis

At a glance

  • Florida's entire population traces back to a single flock of eleven birds that wintered near Sarasota in 1981. The founding group is believed to have crossed the Gulf of Mexico from the Yucatan Peninsula. From that foothold, the species now breeds across virtually the entire state.
  • The pink bill and legs are impossible to miss — no other duck in Florida combines that electric pink hardware with a chestnut body and jet-black belly. Males and females are identical in plumage, which is unusual for ducks and more typical of geese and swans.
  • This species thinks nothing of perching in trees, on telephone lines, or along fence rails — habits that earned it an earlier name, the tree duck. The long legs that let it do this also give it an upright, almost goose-like stance on the ground.
  • Both parents share incubation duties equally — a trait far more common in geese and swans than in ducks. They also form lifelong pair bonds, and the oldest banded individual on record lived at least ten years and seven months.

How to know it

Size and shape

A large, long-legged, long-necked duck that stands more upright than most — roughly the size of a Cattle Egret. Length 18.5 to 21 inches; wingspan about 30 to 37 inches. The upright, goose-like posture is the first thing you notice.

Plumage

Adults are rich chestnut-brown on the breast, back, and neck cap, with a jet-black belly and tail. The face and upper neck are pale gray, with a neat white eye-ring. Wings are dark with a bold white stripe formed by the secondary feathers — striking in flight, visible as a white band when the wings are folded.

Bill and legs

Bright pink bill and long pink legs; these are the most instantly field-identifiable features at any distance. Males and females are identical in appearance.

Juveniles

Young birds are duller and browner overall, with a gray bill and pale legs and lack the sharply defined black belly of adults. The white wing stripe is still present and useful for identification.

In flight

Broad wings, long neck stretched forward, and long legs trailing behind the tail create a distinctive gangly silhouette unlike any other local waterfowl. The white wing patch flashes prominently, and the birds call almost continuously while airborne.

Voice

One of Florida's more audible waterbirds. The signature call is a high-pitched, multi-note whistle — often described as a clear waa-choo or a rapid series of peeping whistles — delivered on the wing and on the ground alike. Flocks in flight produce a continuous chorus that carries well and typically announces their presence before they come into sight. On the ground, birds maintain a running soft chatter of peeps and chirps among themselves.

Size

18.5 to 21 inches; wingspan 30 to 37 inches

What to look for

Look for a large, upright duck with a pink bill and pink legs — nothing else in Florida combines those features. At rest, the chestnut breast and black belly are diagnostic; in flight, watch for the broad white wing stripe and the dangling pink legs extending beyond the short tail. Juveniles are duller but still show the white wing mark. The whistling call, heard constantly when flocks are moving, is often the first clue they are nearby.

Where to find it here

Check any open pond or wet margin in the park, especially near larger trees that might provide perch sites. This species spends much of the day loafing rather than foraging, so scan the shoreline edges and the branches overhanging water. At dusk, watch for flocks lifting off and heading toward feeding areas — and listen first, since the whistling call announces them reliably. They are tame and approachable by waterfowl standards.

Year-round residents in Manatee County and present at all hours, though most active foraging occurs after dark. During daylight, they are most visible in the early morning and late afternoon when loafing along water edges or making short local flights. In Florida, ducklings are most conspicuous in August and September, the species being a relatively late nester. Numbers may swell locally in late summer and fall as post-breeding flocks congregate.

When

Year-round resident throughout peninsular Florida, including Manatee County. Single-brooded in Florida; ducklings are conspicuous in August and September. Flocks may be larger and more visible in late summer and fall as family groups consolidate.

How it lives

Highly social all year, in flocks that can run to hundreds. And it perches — in trees, on fence posts, on telephone lines and low snags over water, which is unusual enough for a duck that the old name for it was tree duck.

Feeding happens mostly at night. Through the day flocks loaf along wetland edges, preening and resting. In flight they are slow and shallow-winged, with the feet trailing out behind the tail — a reliable way to name one at distance.

Mostly plants: seeds of grasses, smartweed, sedges, amaranth and aquatic plants, plus sorghum, millet, corn and rice. Insects and snails are less than a tenth of it. They dabble at the surface or tip up for what is below.

Pairs for life

Pairs form in winter, with neck-stretching, bill-dipping and water-flicking displays, and stay together. Both parents incubate and both raise the brood.

What it does here

As primarily herbivorous grazers, these ducks consume seeds and aquatic plant material in wetland and agricultural settings, influencing local plant communities and dispersing seeds. Eggs, ducklings, and adults are preyed upon by raccoons, rat snakes, opossums, and raptors, placing the species in the middle of the local food web. Their use of tree cavities can bring them into competition with Wood Ducks for nesting sites, though the two species often coexist. In turn, their readily adopted nest boxes provide a model for supporting cavity-nesting waterbirds in managed urban and suburban wetlands.

The Black-bellied Whistling-Duck's original breeding range ran from southern Texas and Mexico south through Central America to northern Argentina. Beginning in the late 1970s, birds began expanding northward and eastward on their own, and Florida's founding flock — thought to have crossed the Gulf of Mexico from the Yucatan — arrived near Sarasota in 1981. From those eleven birds, the species spread steadily; the Florida Breeding Bird Atlas II documented breeding records across 342 quadrangles statewide, with the central peninsula especially well occupied. FWC now treats the species as a year-round resident throughout peninsular Florida, and Manatee County sits squarely in the heart of its established range. The species is considered a self-colonizing naturalized resident and is treated as native by Florida wildlife managers.

Something about this duck defies easy categorization. It looks like a duck, but its goose-like proportions, upright posture, long pink legs, and habit of perching in trees all set it apart.

Add the fact that both sexes are identically patterned and share incubation duties equally, and you have a bird that sits somewhere between a duck and a goose in its life history — which is exactly where the whistling-ducks as a group belong evolutionarily.

Nesting is an exercise in opportunism. The preferred site is a natural tree cavity, 4 to 20 feet above the ground, but nest boxes are adopted readily, and the species has also nested in chimneys, barns, and on the ground in dense low vegetation near water.

Females sometimes practice egg-dumping — laying eggs in a neighbor's nest — so a single cavity nest may hold far more eggs than one pair could incubate alone; clutches of 50 or more eggs from multiple females have been recorded. Ducklings leave the nest within a day or two of hatching, dropping to the ground and immediately feeding themselves, though they remain with both parents for weeks.

The Florida expansion of this species is one of the more remarkable range-change stories in recent North American ornithology. From a single flock near Sarasota in 1981, now thought to have originated in the Yucatan Peninsula, the birds colonized south and central Florida steadily, with breeding first confirmed in Hardee County in 1990.

The reason for the expansion is not fully understood — most other wetland species have seen range contractions over the same period — but adaptability to urban ponds, golf courses, suburban parks, and agricultural fields has clearly helped.
