Brown Spitting Spider

Scytodes fusca
On this page

At a glance

  • Instead of trapping prey in a web, a spitting spider shoots a sticky mixture from its fangs that pins a small animal to the surface. The spider then moves in to bite.
  • The unusually high, rounded front of the body houses enlarged glands involved in producing the spider's sticky prey-catching fluid.
  • Spitting spiders have six eyes arranged in three pairs rather than the eight eyes found in many familiar spiders.
  • It is a slow hunter. Rather than chasing fast prey, it approaches within range and immobilizes the target before closing in.
  • A female carries her egg sac beneath her body, held in her mouthparts, rather than leaving it attached to a web.

How to know it

Size and shape

A small brown spider about a quarter-inch long, with long slender legs and a conspicuously high, rounded front body section.

Color

Brown to chestnut overall, with darker mottling on the rounded front of the body and patterned markings across the abdomen.

Eyes

Six small eyes are arranged in three pairs. This can help confirm a spitting spider in a close photograph, but the eyes are difficult to see without magnification.

Similar species

Other spitting spiders can look very similar. The high rounded front of the body and six-eye arrangement help identify the family, but confirming Scytodes fusca may require a close photograph or specialist examination.

Size

About a quarter-inch long, not counting the legs.

What to look for

Look for a small mottled-brown spider with long thin legs and an unusually high, rounded front body section. During the day it may rest on a wall, beneath an overhang or in another sheltered spot. The six eyes are useful only in a close photograph.

Easily confused with

Other spitting spiders — Other Scytodes look very similar. The high rounded front of the body and the six-eye arrangement identify the family, but confirming Scytodes fusca may require a close photograph or specialist examination.

Where to find it here

Check sheltered surfaces such as the undersides of benches, covered structures, exterior walls, beneath overhangs and under loose bark. During the day the spider may remain motionless in a protected corner; it hunts after dark.

Most active at night. During the day it may be found resting in sheltered places. Florida's warm climate allows activity through much of the year, but Palma Sola has too few records to establish a local seasonal pattern.

When

Established in Florida and potentially present year-round in sheltered habitat. Palma Sola records are too limited to estimate local frequency or seasonality.

How it lives

It hunts on foot rather than building a prey-catching web. Most activity occurs at night; by day it often rests in a sheltered place.

The spider approaches prey slowly rather than chasing it. Once a small animal is immobilized, the spider moves in to bite and feed.

What it does here

A tropical species now found widely around the world, often in and around buildings. It is introduced and established in Florida.

A predator of small insects and other spiders in sheltered habitats. It in turn can be eaten by larger spiders, lizards and other small predators.

Outdoors, spitting spiders can shelter under loose bark, inside tree cavities and among dense plant debris. Plants provide cover and hunting surfaces rather than food; the spider itself is entirely predatory.

Introduced and established in Florida. It is not currently treated as a major invasive species or management concern.
