Melon Aphid

Aphis gossypii

At a glance

  • In warm climates, melon aphids can reproduce for long periods without mating. Females give birth to live female nymphs, which mature quickly and begin producing daughters of their own. Under favorable conditions, a newborn aphid can mature in about a week.
  • Melon aphids vary from pale yellow through green to nearly black, so color alone is unreliable. Look instead for the pair of dark tubes, called cornicles, projecting from the rear of the abdomen.
  • Ants often collect the sugary honeydew aphids excrete. In return, the ants may defend the colony from lady beetles, parasitoid wasps and other aphid enemies.
  • A swollen brown aphid that looks dry and papery may be a 'mummy.' A tiny parasitoid wasp developed inside it, killed it, and may eventually emerge through a round hole in the shell.

How to know it

Size

Tiny — usually around one-sixteenth of an inch long, with a soft, pear-shaped body. A hand lens or phone macro camera makes identification much easier.

Color

Color varies widely, from pale yellow through yellow-green and olive to very dark green. Do not identify this species by color alone.

Cornicles

Two dark tubes called cornicles project from the rear of the abdomen. Their dark color is useful when identifying Melon Aphids, but other body features and the host plant may also be needed to confirm the species.

Winged and wingless forms

Most aphids in a settled colony are wingless. Crowding or declining plant quality can trigger production of winged females that disperse to new plants.

What to look for

Check the undersides of leaves and tender new shoots for clusters of tiny aphids. Sticky honeydew, black sooty mold or ants moving along the stems can reveal a colony before you notice the aphids themselves. With magnification, look for dark cornicles at the rear of the body. Brown, swollen aphid 'mummies' are evidence that parasitoid wasps have found the colony.

Easily confused with

Other aphids — Many aphid species look similar at this size. Body color alone is not enough to identify a Melon Aphid; close examination of body structures and the host plant may be needed for a confident identification.

Where to find it here

Check tender new growth and the undersides of leaves, especially on hibiscus, citrus, peppers, cucumbers, melons and related plants. Ants traveling repeatedly up and down a stem may lead you to an aphid colony. Sticky honeydew or black sooty mold on leaves can also reveal aphids feeding above.

Melon Aphids can occur year-round in southwest Florida. Colonies build quickly when suitable plants are producing tender new growth and conditions favor reproduction.

When

Possible year-round in southwest Florida. Numbers can rise quickly when host plants produce tender new growth, then fall as plants change or predators, parasitoids and weather reduce the colony.

How it lives

Aphids cluster on tender shoots and the undersides of leaves, inserting slender mouthparts into the plant to reach sugar-rich phloem sap. Much of the excess sugar is excreted as sticky honeydew.

When a colony is disturbed, an alarm chemical can cause nearby aphids to move away or drop from the plant.

What it does here

The Melon Aphid now lives across tropical, subtropical and temperate regions worldwide, and nobody is certain where it started. Centuries of travelling with cultivated plants have obscured its original range.

Melon Aphids feed lady beetles, lacewing larvae, flower-fly larvae and tiny parasitoid wasps. They can also spread plant viruses as they probe one plant after another, sometimes transmitting a virus even when they do not remain on the plant long enough to establish a colony.

Melon Aphids feed on an unusually broad range of plants. In Florida their hosts include cucumbers, melons and gourds; hibiscus and okra; citrus; peppers and eggplant; and many ornamentals and weeds. They concentrate on tender new growth and leaf undersides, where they tap into phloem sap. Heavy colonies can curl or distort young leaves, while their honeydew supports the black sooty mold often seen below an infestation.
