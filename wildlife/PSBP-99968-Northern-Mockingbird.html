Northern Mockingbird

Mimus polyglottos
On this page

At a glance

  • A slim gray bird singing a long series of changing phrases from an exposed perch is likely a Northern Mockingbird, Florida's state bird.
  • A male may learn around 200 song types over its lifetime, incorporating imitations of other birds and frogs into its own long performances.
  • Unpaired males may sing well into the night during the breeding season, sometimes from brightly lit perches.
  • Mockingbirds defend nests and feeding territories aggressively, sometimes swooping at cats, hawks or people that approach too closely.

How to know it

Color

Plain gray above and pale below, with narrow white wing bars.

Wing flash

Large white wing patches show dramatically in flight and during wing-flashing displays on the ground.

Tail

Long, with white outer feathers.

Voice

A long series of varied phrases, usually repeating each phrase several times before switching to another. Mockingbirds imitate many other birds and frogs and continue adding sounds throughout life.

What to look for

Look for a slim gray bird with a long tail, conspicuous white wing patches and a habit of singing from exposed perches. A long song made of short phrases repeated several times before changing is characteristic.

Behavior cue

Often sings from exposed treetops, poles, fences or wires and forages on short grass.

Where to find it here

Look in open areas with scattered shrubs and trees. Mockingbirds often sing from the highest exposed perch available, then drop to lawns and other short vegetation to forage.

A year-round Florida resident. Mockingbirds are common in parks and neighborhoods regionally, but Palma Sola currently has only one formal wildlife record.

How it lives

Mockingbirds spend much of their time in open and semi-open habitat, singing from exposed perches and dropping to lawns or low vegetation to forage. They defend nesting and feeding territories aggressively.

The wing flash

While foraging on the ground, a mockingbird often opens its wings in quick flashes that expose the large white patches. One hypothesis is that the movement startles insects, but researchers do not yet know the display's exact function.

Insects and other small invertebrates dominate during warmer months; fruit and berries become more important at other times of year.

What it does here

An insect predator and fruit-eater. Seeds from some fruits pass through the bird and can be dispersed away from the parent plant.

Berries and open ground

Mockingbirds depend on both open ground and dense vegetation. Fruiting shrubs and small trees provide food, while thick shrubs and low trees provide nest sites and defensive cover. Plantings that combine berry-producing plants with open lawn or ground layer suit the species particularly well.

Take care

No meaningful danger. A nesting bird may swoop close if you approach its nest, and may scold or swoop at a pet nearby.

If a mockingbird repeatedly swoops or scolds you, move farther away; a nest or fledgling may be nearby. Do not feed or approach the nest.

Conservation

Widespread and currently of low conservation concern.
