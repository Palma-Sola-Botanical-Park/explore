White Peacock

At a glance

  • The white peacock is a pale, almost ghostly butterfly — chalky white with faint tan markings, dark eyespots, and scalloped, orange-edged wings.
  • It stays low, flying just above the ground in damp, open places like pond edges, ditches, and wet fields.
  • Its caterpillars feed on low, creeping native plants — frogfruit and water hyssop — that grow right along the water's edge.
  • Males are territorial, patrolling a small patch of host plants and chasing off rivals and other insects that wander in.

How to know it

Color

Chalky white with faint light-brown markings.

Wings

Scalloped edges trimmed in orange, with small dark eyespots.

Flight

Low and close to the ground.

Size

2 to 2.75 inches

What to look for

A pale, whitish butterfly flying low over damp, open ground near water, showing orange-trimmed, scalloped wings and small dark spots. Its habit of staying near the ground around pond edges is a good clue.

Where to find it here

Flying low around the moist edges of the ponds and in damp open ground, especially near frogfruit and other low groundcovers.

Year-round resident, most numerous in the warm months. Look low to the ground near water.

How it lives

Active by day, flying low to the ground. Males defend small territories around host plants. Breeds through multiple generations across the warm months.

Caterpillars feed on low native plants, chiefly frogfruit and water hyssop. Adults sip nectar from low-growing flowers such as Spanish needles and frogfruit.

What it does here

A pollinator of low, moist-ground flowers and a specialist on creeping native groundcovers — a small but telling member of the pond-edge community.

Caterpillar host plants are low native groundcovers — frogfruit (Phyla nodiflora) and water hyssop (Bacopa monnieri) — the same plants that carpet moist pond edges.

Native to the southeastern United States, the Caribbean, Mexico, and South America. In Florida it is a common, year-round resident of low, moist, open habitats, especially in the central and southern parts of the state.

The white peacock is a butterfly of wet, open ground. You will rarely see it high in the air; instead it flutters low over damp fields, ditches, and pond margins, close to the small plants its caterpillars depend on.

Those host plants — frogfruit and water hyssop especially — are humble, low-growing natives that thrive at the water's edge, exactly the kind of groundcover a pond-side planting provides. Where they grow, white peacocks tend to follow.

Male white peacocks are notably territorial for a butterfly, staking out a small area around the host plants and actively driving off rival males and other insects that intrude — a lot of bustle from such a delicate-looking creature.
