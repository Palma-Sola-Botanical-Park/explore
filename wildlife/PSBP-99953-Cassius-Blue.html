Cassius Blue

Leptotes cassius

At a glance

  • A tiny pale-blue butterfly, barely bigger than a thumbnail, flickering low over the flower beds is a cassius blue — one of Florida's smallest and most common butterflies.
  • Males shine pale blue above; females are duller, with more dusky gray and brown.
  • Flip to the underside and you find a delicate maze of fine gray-and-white bands, with one or two small black-and-orange eyespots near the tail.
  • The hindwing eyespots, paired with tiny tails the butterfly wiggles, create a "false head" that fools a predator into striking the wrong end.

How to know it

Upperside

Males pale blue; females duller blue-gray with brown.

Underside

White to pale gray with fine gray bands and one or two small black-and-orange eyespots near the tail.

Size

Very small, wingspan around 1 inches.

Tails

Tiny threadlike tails on the hindwings.

Voice

Silent.

Size

about 0.75 to 1.25 inches

What to look for

A very small, pale powder-blue butterfly fluttering low around flowers. The fine-banded underside with little eyespots near a tiny tail confirms a cassius blue.

Where to find it here

Low around the flower beds and any plumbago plantings, fluttering close to the blooms. Look small — it is easy to miss.

Year-round in Florida's warm climate, most numerous in the warmer months.

How it lives

Active by day, with a low, fluttery flight close to the ground and flowers. Often perches with wings closed, slowly moving the hindwings so the false-head eyespots and tails twitch.

Adults sip nectar from small flowers. Caterpillars feed on the flowers and buds of legumes and on plumbago.

What it does here

A small pollinator of low flowers and prey for spiders, birds, and other insects — a minor but constant thread in the garden food web.

Caterpillar host plants include legumes (such as peas and wild beans) and plumbago (leadwort) — common garden plants, which keeps this butterfly close to home.

Native to the southeastern United States, the Caribbean, and south into the American tropics. In Florida it is a common, year-round resident of gardens, parks, and weedy open ground.

The cassius blue is a member of the gossamer-wing family — small, fragile butterflies often no bigger than a fingernail. Males are a soft, pale blue above; females wear more gray and brown, so the two can look like different butterflies at a glance.

Turn one over and the underside tells the real story: a fine pattern of gray-and-white bands and a couple of small black-and-orange eyespots near the rear edge of the hindwing. Combined with tiny tails that the resting butterfly slowly wiggles, those spots mimic a head and antennae at the wrong end of the body. A bird aiming for the "head" gets a harmless beakful of wingtip, and the butterfly escapes.

It is an easy butterfly to host. Its caterpillars feed on the buds and flowers of legumes and on plumbago, both common in Florida landscaping, which is part of why the cassius blue is such a constant presence in gardens.
