Bald Eagle

At a glance

  • America's national bird since 1782 — and one of the country's great conservation success stories. In 1963, only about 487 breeding pairs remained in the lower 48 states; by 2007, that number had climbed to nearly 9,800.
  • Florida has the densest bald eagle population of any state outside Alaska. An estimated 1,488 nests were recorded across 55 Florida counties in the 2023–24 breeding season alone.
  • The largest bird's nest ever recorded was built by bald eagles just up the coast near St. Petersburg. Examined in 1963, it measured 9.5 feet across and 20 feet deep and was estimated to weigh more than two tons.
  • "Bald" doesn't mean hairless. The name comes from an old English word for white or white-headed — and young eagles spend their first several years mostly brown and mottled with white, gradually acquiring the adult pattern.

How to know it

Easily confused with

Osprey — The osprey is smaller, white underneath with a dark eye-mask and crooked wings; the bald eagle is much bigger and bulkier, adults with a white head and tail.

Adults

Massive dark brown body with an unmistakable white head and tail; the heavy hooked bill, eyes, and powerful legs are all brilliant yellow. Those enormous yellow feet aren't just for show — the rough skin and hooked talons give the eagle a secure grip on slippery fish.

Young birds

No white head — instead a large, dark brown bird with mottled white on the underwings and belly. Young eagles spend their first several years in this mottled brown plumage, gradually acquiring the adult pattern.

In flight

Very large, with broad, plank-like wings held flat. Adults show a notably large head and bill that project well beyond the leading edge of the wing. Turkey vultures, by contrast, hold their wings in a shallow V and rock from side to side.

Voice

The real call is surprisingly high and chirpy. The dramatic eagle scream commonly heard in movies is usually a red-tailed hawk call dubbed over the footage.

Size

28 to 40 inches; 6 to 7.5 feet

What to look for

A big dark raptor with a brilliant white head and tail, perched high in a tall tree near water or soaring on broad, flat wings. An all-brown bird of similar size with white mottling is likely a young bald eagle. If the wings tilt in a V-shape and the bird rocks from side to side, that's a turkey vulture.

Where to find it here

Overhead or perched high in a large tree near water. A genuine thrill when it happens, but an occasional visitor rather than an everyday sight.

Present in Florida year-round, but Florida's breeding schedule is the reverse of northern populations. Eagles return to local territories in October, with egg-laying peaking between December and early January and young fledging by May. Most breeding adults — especially in the southern peninsula — stay in Florida year-round; it is subadults and non-breeding birds that head north for summer. Late fall through spring is the best window for a park sighting.

When

Florida eagles invert the northern nesting calendar: pairs return to territories in October, lay eggs December–January, and fledge young by May. Most breeding adults remain year-round; subadults and non-breeders migrate north for summer. Park sightings are occasional flyovers or perched birds, most likely late fall through spring.

How it lives

Active by day. Hunts from a perch or on the wing, soars on thermals, and roosts and nests near water. In Florida, pairs begin nest building in fall, lay most eggs in December and early January, and young begin leaving the nest in spring. Florida's official nesting season runs October 1 through May 15. Most breeding adults remain in Florida year-round.

Fish are the primary prey, but bald eagles are opportunists. They hunt fish and waterbirds, take mammals when they can, scavenge carrion, and sometimes chase an osprey until it drops its catch — a behavior called kleptoparasitism.

What it does here

As a large predator and scavenger, the bald eagle occupies a high position in aquatic and coastal food webs — taking live prey and readily feeding on carrion.

Builds its massive nests in tall living trees near water.

Native to North America, found near water across most of the continent. Florida has the densest bald eagle population of any state outside Alaska, with an estimated 1,488 nests recorded in the 2023–24 breeding season — and a nesting calendar unlike anywhere else in the lower 48.

Bald eagles are opportunistic predators and scavengers, not specialized fish hunters. Fish are important, but they also take waterbirds and small mammals, feed readily on carrion, and sometimes rob ospreys of a catch by chasing them until the smaller bird drops the fish — a behavior called kleptoparasitism.

Pairs often stay together for years, returning to the same territory and usually the same nest. If a mate dies, the survivor can pair with another eagle. Because pairs add new material each season, nests can become enormous over time — the largest bird's nest ever recorded was built by bald eagles near St. Petersburg, Florida.

Florida's nesting calendar is almost the reverse of what you see across much of the northern range. Pairs begin nest building in fall, most eggs are laid in December and early January, and young begin leaving the nest in spring, with some remaining around the nesting territory well into summer. For management purposes, FWC defines the Florida bald eagle nesting season as October 1 through May 15.

Most breeding adults remain in Florida year-round; it is subadults and non-breeding birds that head north for summer.

In Florida, bald eagles strongly favor living native pines for nesting — 75 percent of surveyed Florida nests were built in living native pines. Nearly all Florida nests are within 1.8 miles of water. The tree is more than a perch: it has to support a nest that may grow enormous over years of reuse.

The eagle's recovery is one of America's great conservation success stories. In 1963, only about 487 breeding pairs were known in the lower 48 states. After DDT was banned in 1972 and federal protections took hold, the population rebounded dramatically — reaching nearly 9,789 breeding pairs by 2007, when the species was removed from the federal endangered-species list.

Florida removed the bald eagle from its state list of imperiled species in 2008. The eagle remains protected under the Bald and Golden Eagle Protection Act and the Migratory Bird Treaty Act.

Take care

What to do

Never approach or disturb an active nest. If you spot one, observe from a distance with binoculars or a long lens and move on. The Bald and Golden Eagle Protection Act protects not just the bird itself but its nest and breeding behavior — human activity that substantially interferes with nesting can constitute illegal disturbance.
