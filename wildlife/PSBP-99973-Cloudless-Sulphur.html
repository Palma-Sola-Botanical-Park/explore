Cloudless Sulphur

Phoebis sennae
On this page

At a glance

  • A large, clear lemon-yellow butterfly crossing gardens and open spaces with a quick, restless flight.
  • The species name sennae comes from Senna, the group of pea-family plants its caterpillars eat.
  • Its long tongue reaches into deep tubular flowers that many other insects cannot use, including many red and orange blooms.
  • In autumn they join a broad southward movement of butterflies down the Florida peninsula.

How to know it

Color

Large and lemon-yellow. Males clear yellow; females yellow to whitish with a dark wing border and a small dark spot.

Size

Among the larger common yellow butterflies, with a wingspan up to about three inches.

Flight

Fast and restless, rarely settling for long.

What to look for

A flickering patch of pure yellow crossing a lawn or bed. Most of the yellow butterflies moving quickly across open ground here are this one.

Where to find it here

Watch open, sunny areas and nectar flowers, especially anywhere Senna or Cassia is growing.

Present year-round in Florida, with more on the wing during the autumn movement south.

How it lives

Active by day with a fast, restless flight, breeding through several generations a year.

Puddling

Males often gather on damp ground, sipping water and dissolved minerals — a source of salts they cannot get from nectar.

What it does here

Named for its host plant

The caterpillars eat Senna and Cassia — pea-family plants including native privet senna and partridge pea. The butterfly is literally named after them, and without those plants in the landscape it cannot complete its life cycle here.

A caterpillar feeding on yellow Senna blossoms often turns yellow itself and vanishes into its meal.

In autumn they join a broad southward movement of butterflies down the peninsula. An extra-long proboscis lets the adults drink from long tubular flowers, including many red and orange ones, that shorter-tongued butterflies cannot reach.

Take care

What to do

Native Senna — privet senna or partridge pea — gives the caterpillars a home.
