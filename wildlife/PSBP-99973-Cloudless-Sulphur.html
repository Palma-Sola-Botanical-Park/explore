Cloudless Sulphur

Phoebis sennae
On this page

At a glance

  • The cloudless sulphur is a large, clear lemon-yellow butterfly that crosses gardens and open spaces with a quick, restless flight.
  • Its very name points to its plant partner: the species name 'sennae' comes from Senna, the group of plants its caterpillars eat.
  • It carries an unusually long tongue, letting it sip nectar from deep, tubular flowers — often red ones — that many other butterflies cannot reach.
  • Each fall, cloudless sulphurs from farther north stream southward into and through Florida, a quieter migration than the monarch's but a real one.

How to know it

Color

Large and lemon-yellow; males clear yellow, females yellow to whitish with a dark wing border and a small dark spot.

Size

Among the larger common yellow butterflies.

Flight

Fast and somewhat erratic.

Size

2.25 to 3 inches

What to look for

A big, bright yellow butterfly moving quickly across open ground or nectaring at tubular flowers. The clear, unmarked yellow of the male is the easiest clue; females are paler with darker edges.

Where to find it here

Flitting across open, sunny areas and visiting nectar flowers, especially anywhere Senna or Cassia plants are growing. Most numerous in late summer and fall.

Year-round resident, with a noticeable increase during the fall southward migration.

How it lives

A fast, restless flight, several generations a year, and a general southward drift in fall. Males gather at damp ground to puddle — sipping water and the minerals in it.

Caterpillars feed on senna and cassia. Adults take nectar from many flowers but favor long tubular blooms, especially red ones, which suit that long tongue.

What it does here

A wide-ranging pollinator that reaches deep flowers other insects cannot, and a specialist on Senna plants that ties it to the pea family.

Caterpillar host plants are Senna and Cassia (pea family) — including native privet senna (Senna ligustrina) and partridge pea — the very plants the butterfly is named for.

Native to the Americas and common across the southern United States, ranging north in summer. In Florida it is a year-round resident and breeder, with numbers swelling in fall as northern butterflies move south.

The cloudless sulphur is one of Florida's most familiar butterflies — those flickering patches of pure yellow crossing a lawn or garden are usually this species.

Its life is tied to plants in the genus Senna (and the closely related Cassia), members of the pea family. The caterpillars feed on their leaves and flowers, and a caterpillar munching yellow blossoms often turns yellow itself, blending neatly into its meal. The butterfly's scientific name, Phoebis sennae, even records the connection.

In autumn, cloudless sulphurs join a broad southward movement of butterflies down the peninsula. With an extra-long proboscis, the adults can drink from long, tubular flowers — they are especially fond of red ones — that shorter-tongued butterflies cannot use.
