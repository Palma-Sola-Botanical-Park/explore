Polydamas Swallowtail

Battus polydamas
On this page

At a glance

  • A big dark swallowtail with a band of gold across each wing and no tails at all. Every other dark swallowtail you might meet here trails a pair.
  • Its caterpillars eat pipevines and nothing else. We have pipevine right in the heart of our butterfly garden climbing up the artwork, and the caterpillars have found it.
  • Poke a caterpillar and a forked orange horn pops out from behind its head, and the smell of it turns ants around.
  • The first thing a hatchling eats is its own eggshell, coating and all.
  • It never stops fluttering while it drinks, and that is how it reaches nectar at the bottom of long tubular flowers.

How to know it

Gold rim, no tails

From above it is black with a broad band of yellow spots near the edge of each wing and a row of smaller spots along the rim, hence the other name, Gold Rim. The hindwings are rounded and plain where other swallowtails carry a tail. The Pipevine Swallowtail, its only close relative here, has tails and a blue sheen; the dark female Tiger, the Spicebush and the Black Swallowtail all have tails too. The wings span three and a half to four and a half inches, and the female is the bigger one.

Turn it over

Underneath, the hindwing carries a row of wavy red lines near the edge with yellow spots outside them, and the body is dotted red-orange down each side. The red is the thing to look for when the wings are closed.

The caterpillar

Full grown it is about two inches long, dark brown to maroon with rows of fleshy orange spikes tipped in black, and glossy. A paler tan form turns up now and then. Small ones feed in a huddle; big ones go off alone.

Where to find it here

The Butterfly Garden. The adults come to drink there, and in late October a caterpillar was feeding on the pipevine climbing the painted wooden sculpture that a volunteer built for the garden in 2025 with places for vines to grow.

How it lives

Pipevine or nothing

The female lays small clutches of yellow-orange eggs on pipevine stems, more rarely on a new leaf, and bands each egg with a hard, bright coating from a gland of her own. The hatchlings eat the shell first, coating included, then the tender new growth. A hungry brood can strip a vine to the stems and eat the flowers for good measure. After each molt the caterpillar eats its shed skin too.

The horn

Behind the head sits a forked orange organ, folded away until something bothers the caterpillar. Then blood pressure shoves it out, and muscles haul it back in. It carries a sharp-smelling secretion, and in a lab test that secretion turned ants around. It is not a magic shield: a parasitic wasp has been watched laying her eggs regardless.

A bad meal, by design

Pipevines carry bitter compounds called aristolochic acids. The caterpillars store them, the adults keep them, and the bright colors on both are the usual sign of an insect that tastes terrible. On Martinique, caterpillars of this butterfly steeped in rum were once sold as a folk remedy; the sale is now banned. Our own Dutchman's Pipe is the odd one out: by the chemists' accounts it carries none of those acids, and caterpillars grow up on it all the same.

Chrysalis to wing

A full-grown caterpillar leaves the vine to pupate, hanging from a silk pad in a silk girdle. The chrysalis comes in green or brown; in one study, caterpillars that had gone short of food mostly made brown ones. The adult usually breaks out in the morning and is flying by afternoon. In South Florida it is on the wing in every month. This far north it thins out in winter.

What it does

A regular at the bar

Watch one at the flowers and it hardly settles: the wings keep fanning the whole time it drinks. In our photographs it is working a porterweed spike, and Tropical Sage, which grows in the garden too, is another flower it is known to visit.

A caterpillar worth reporting

If you find one of these spiky caterpillars on a pipevine here, take a picture and tell us which vine it was on. The one on the butterfly sculpture is where the first turned up; our Brazilian Dutchman's Pipe, on the boundary fence along 17th Avenue forty yards to the north, is the other place to check. We are still sorting out which pipevines we have and which ones the butterfly is using.
