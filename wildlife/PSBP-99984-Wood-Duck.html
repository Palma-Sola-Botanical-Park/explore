Wood Duck

Aix sponsa
On this page

At a glance

  • Most waterfowl nest on the ground, but the wood duck nests up high — inside hollow tree cavities, often old woodpecker holes in cypress and oaks along wooded, swampy shorelines.
  • Unlike most ducks, wood ducks have strong, sharp claws on their webbed feet — built for gripping bark and perching high in the canopy, which is exactly where you may spot one along the wooded edges of the pond.
  • The day after hatching, the ducklings make a leap of faith — jumping from the high nest hole, sometimes dozens of feet down, and bouncing unhurt before following their mother to the water.
  • A century ago wood ducks were nearly gone, hit hard by overhunting and the loss of bottomland forest; hunting limits and a huge nest-box effort brought them back.

How to know it

Male

Boldly patterned — a glossy green and chestnut head with white stripes, a swept-back crest, and red eyes.

Female

Soft gray-brown with a distinctive white teardrop around the eye.

Shape

A slim duck with a boxy, crested head, often holding the head low.

Voice

The female gives a loud, rising 'oo-eek' squeal when flushed; the male a softer, thin whistle.

Size

19 to 21 inches; about 28 to 30 inches

What to look for

Look for a slim, boxy-headed duck moving through deeply shaded, wooded water rather than out in the open. Wood ducks favor tucked-away spots near overhanging branches — and if approached too closely, they'll flush fast with a loud, rising squeal.

Where to find it here

On quieter, shadier stretches of the ponds and wooded edges, often in pairs near overhanging cover rather than out in open water.

Year-round resident, with more birds present in winter. Most likely along sheltered, wooded water.

How it lives

A duck that perches in trees, which is not a thing ducks generally do. It nests in cavities well above the ground — a hollow trunk or a nest box — and the ducklings jump.

Usually in pairs or small groups near shaded water with cover close by. Seeds, fruit and aquatic plants, plus insects. Acorns are the thing in fall and winter.

What it does here

Spreads the seeds of wetland and bottomland plants and is part of the food web as both forager and prey. Its reliance on tree cavities makes it a flagship for protecting wooded wetlands.

Eats acorns, seeds, fruits, and aquatic plants, and nests in tree cavities (often old woodpecker holes) in wooded wetlands.

Native to North America, common across the eastern United States and the Pacific Northwest. In Florida it is a year-round resident, and winter brings additional birds from the north.

The wood duck is a bird of wooded swamps and shaded water, and its whole life is tied to trees. It nests in natural cavities, often old woodpecker holes, which sets it apart from the ground-nesting ducks of open marshes.

That cavity-nesting habit leads to a remarkable sight. A day or so after hatching, the tiny ducklings climb to the cavity entrance and jump, tumbling to the ground or water below and bouncing unharmed thanks to their light, downy bodies, then trailing their mother off to feed.

By the early 1900s, unregulated hunting and the clearing of bottomland forests had pushed wood ducks to the brink. Hunting limits under the Migratory Bird Treaty Act, habitat protection, and a huge volunteer nest-box effort turned the species around — one of conservation's quieter success stories.

Take care

What to do

Watch from a distance and do not feed it. Wood ducks are shy and stick to cover; bread and snacks harm waterfowl health.
