Great Horned Owl

Bubo virginianus
On this page

At a glance

  • Look below a regular owl roost for compact pellets of fur and bone. Owls swallow much of their prey whole and later cough up the parts they cannot digest.
  • The deep series of hoots heard after dark is one of the easiest ways to detect a Great Horned Owl.
  • Its diet is exceptionally broad for a raptor, ranging from mice and rabbits to skunks, waterbirds and other birds of prey.
  • Great Horned Owls begin nesting unusually early. In Florida, nesting can start in December, often in an old nest originally built by a hawk, crow, heron or squirrel.

How to know it

Size

A large, bulky owl with a wingspan around four feet.

Ear tufts

Two prominent feather tufts on the head. They are not ears.

Color

Mottled brown and gray, with a pale throat, a broad facial disc and bright yellow eyes.

Voice

A deep series of resonant hoots. Pairs may call back and forth, with the female's voice usually higher than the male's.

What to look for

Look for a large, bulky owl with prominent ear tufts and yellow eyes. By day it may roost against a trunk or inside dense foliage; after dark, listen for deep hooting.

Easily confused with

Eastern Screech-Owl — Also has ear tufts and yellow eyes but is dramatically smaller — only about eight or nine inches tall. Great Horned Owls are large, heavy-bodied birds with wingspans around four feet.

Where to find it here

Listen around large trees at dusk and after dark. By day, scan substantial trunks, dense crowns and old nest structures for a resting bird.

A year-round Florida resident, but mainly nocturnal, so most detections are by voice. Palma Sola currently has too few formal observations to define a local seasonal peak.

Nearly every Great Horned Owl record from around Palma Sola falls between December and February. Most winters, photographers appear along the park's northern edge with long lenses, waiting on a nest nearby — if you see the tripods, someone has already found the owl.

How it lives

Usually hunts from a perch at night, flying down or out after prey. Mostly mammals and birds, including rabbits, rodents, skunks and waterbirds, with reptiles, amphibians and other prey also taken.

A borrowed nest

Great Horned Owls usually take over an existing nest rather than building one. An old stick nest made by a hawk, crow, heron or squirrel may be occupied, and tree cavities and broken snags are also used.

It builds nothing of its own. A Great Horned Owl takes over the old nest of a hawk, heron or squirrel, and that is much of why it nests so early — the nest has to be claimed before whoever built it comes back for it.

Pairs often duet through the winter nights, and it is the female whose voice is pitched higher.

What it does here

A large nighttime predator that takes mammals, birds, reptiles and other animals across a wide range of habitats.

Big trees, old nests

Large trees provide both daytime roosts and nest support. Because the owl rarely builds its own nest, the presence of old stick nests, cavities and broken snags matters as much as the trees themselves.

Take care

The risk

Do not approach a nest or try to handle an owl. Adults defend themselves and their young with powerful talons.

What to do

Keep your distance, especially near a nest, and do not gather beneath a roost for photographs. If an owl becomes alert to you or leaves its perch, you are too close.

Rodent poison

An owl that eats poisoned rodents is poisoned in turn. Rodent bait is one of the main hazards to owls living near people.
