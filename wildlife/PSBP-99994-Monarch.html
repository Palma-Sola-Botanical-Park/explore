Monarch

Danaus plexippus

At a glance

  • The monarch is a familiar sight in Florida's open spaces and gardens — bright orange wings laced with heavy black veins.
  • Its caterpillars eat only milkweed, a plant whose toxins they store in their bodies, making both caterpillar and adult unpalatable, even poisonous, to predators.
  • Each fall, monarchs from the eastern U.S. and Canada fly thousands of miles to spend the winter in the mountains of central Mexico.
  • No single butterfly makes the whole round trip — the journey is a relay across several generations, with one long-lived late-summer generation doing the marathon south.

How to know it

Easily confused with

Viceroy — The viceroy is smaller and has an extra black line arcing across the hindwing that the monarch lacks.

Queen — The queen is a darker chestnut with less black veining than the monarch.

Gulf Fritillary — The gulf fritillary has narrower wings and brilliant silver spangles underneath; the monarch is broad-winged with heavy black veins and no silver.

Wings

Bright orange with heavy black veins and a black border dotted with white spots.

Male vs. female

Males have a small black scent spot on a hindwing vein and slightly thinner veins; females have thicker veins and no spot.

Caterpillar

Boldly banded in yellow, black, and white, found on milkweed.

Look-alike

The viceroy butterfly mimics the monarch but is smaller and has an extra black line across the hindwing.

Voice

Silent. Like other butterflies, monarchs make no calls.

Size

3.5 to 4 inches

What to look for

A large orange-and-black butterfly nectaring on flowers or drifting through the garden. To be sure it is a monarch and not a viceroy, check the hindwing — a viceroy has a curved black line crossing it that the monarch lacks.

Where to find it here

Around the flower beds and any milkweed plantings, especially on warm, sunny days. Check nectar-rich blooms for feeding adults and milkweed leaves for caterpillars.

Present much of the year in Florida, with both migratory and resident breeding monarchs. Most numerous during spring and fall movements and in warm, flowery months.

How it lives

Active by day, especially in warm sunshine. Adults flutter and glide between nectar sources; females search out milkweed on which to lay eggs. Migrating monarchs travel by day and roost in clusters at night. The full life cycle runs egg, caterpillar, chrysalis, adult.

Caterpillars eat only milkweed leaves. Adults sip nectar from a wide range of flowers and also take water and minerals from damp soil.

What it does here

A pollinator that moves pollen between flowers as it feeds, and a textbook example of a plant-insect partnership through its reliance on milkweed. It is also a flagship species for pollinator and habitat conservation.

Milkweed (Asclepias spp.) is the only food of its caterpillars and the anchor of its life cycle; adults also nectar at many flowers.

Native and widespread across North America, and now found in many warm regions worldwide. In Florida monarchs are present much of the year; the state hosts both migrating monarchs and a resident, year-round breeding population, especially in the south.

The monarch's life revolves around two things: milkweed and migration. Females lay their eggs only on milkweed, and the caterpillars eat nothing else. In doing so they take in the plant's bitter, heart-affecting toxins and become distasteful to birds and other predators — the bright orange is essentially a warning label.

The migration is what the monarch is best known for. Each fall, monarchs from the eastern half of the continent funnel down to a handful of mountain forests in central Mexico, where they gather in enormous numbers. The trip is really a relay: most monarchs live only a few weeks, but the generation that emerges in late summer is different, living six months or more to complete the long journey south and begin the way back.

Monarch numbers have fallen sharply in recent decades, mostly from the loss of milkweed and habitat. The species is listed as Vulnerable internationally, and U.S. wildlife officials proposed protecting it under the Endangered Species Act in 2024 — a decision that, as of early 2026, has been repeatedly delayed. It is a small part of why the park keeps native milkweed and nectar flowers growing: a rest stop and nursery along the way.

Take care

Conservation

A species of growing concern. The migratory monarch is listed as Vulnerable by the IUCN, and U.S. officials have proposed listing it as Threatened under the Endangered Species Act; as of early 2026 that decision remains delayed. Habitat and milkweed loss are the main drivers of its decline.
