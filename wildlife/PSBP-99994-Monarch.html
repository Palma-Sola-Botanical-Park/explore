Monarch

Danaus plexippus

At a glance

  • Monarch caterpillars can eat only milkweeds. Those plants are therefore not just nectar sources — they are the nursery on which the next generation depends.
  • Florida is different: some monarchs remain and breed through winter rather than joining the long-distance migration.
  • Many monarchs from eastern North America migrate thousands of miles to mountain forests in central Mexico, where they spend the winter in enormous colonies.
  • The round trip spans several generations. The late-summer migratory generation lives much longer than summer monarchs and makes the long southbound journey; later generations continue the return north.
  • Milkweeds contain defensive chemicals called cardenolides. Monarch caterpillars store some of them in their bodies, which can make the caterpillars and adult butterflies distasteful or toxic to some predators.

How to know it

Wings

Orange wings with heavy black veins and a black border marked with white spots.

Male and female

Males have a small black scent patch on each hindwing and slightly thinner black veins. Females lack the spots and usually have heavier veining.

Caterpillar

Yellow, black and white bands, with pairs of long dark filaments. Look for it on milkweed.

Look-alike

Viceroys are smaller and have an extra black line crossing the hindwing, which Monarchs lack.

Size

Wingspan about four inches.

What to look for

Look for a large orange-and-black butterfly feeding at flowers or searching around milkweed. Viceroys have an extra black line crossing the hindwing; Monarchs do not.

Easily confused with

Viceroy — The viceroy is smaller and has an extra black line arcing across the hindwing that the monarch lacks.

Queen — The queen is a darker chestnut with less black veining than the monarch.

Gulf Fritillary — The gulf fritillary has narrower wings and brilliant silver spangles underneath; the monarch is broad-winged with heavy black veins and no silver.

Where to find it here

Check flowering beds for adults and native milkweeds for eggs and caterpillars. Milkweed is required for breeding; nectar flowers fuel the adults.

Monarchs can occur in Florida through much of the year because the state supports both resident and migratory butterflies. Palma Sola records are still too limited to define a local peak.

When

Florida supports both resident and migratory monarchs. Palma Sola records are too few to define local seasonal peaks.

How it lives

Adults feed at flowers, while females search for milkweed on which to lay eggs.

Caterpillars eat milkweed. Adults drink nectar from many kinds of flowers. The life cycle runs from egg to caterpillar to chrysalis to adult.

On migration

Migratory monarchs travel during the day and may gather in trees at night during long journeys.

What it does here

Monarch caterpillars are specialist herbivores of milkweed, while adults visit many flowers for nectar and can carry pollen between blooms. Their milkweed-derived chemical defenses also affect which predators can eat them successfully.

Milkweed is essential: Monarchs lay their eggs on milkweeds, and the caterpillars eat no other kind of plant. Adults are much less specialized and feed from many nectar flowers. In Florida, native milkweeds are the better conservation choice — Palma Sola grows swamp milkweed, Asclepias incarnata. Tropical milkweed, Asclepias curassavica, stays green through winter, can encourage year-round breeding and increases opportunities for the OE parasite to spread; UF/IFAS now classifies it as invasive in Florida.

Native to North America and now established in several other parts of the world. Florida supports both migratory monarchs and populations that remain and breed in the state. Not every monarch seen here is on its way to or from Mexico.

Florida sits outside the simple textbook migration map. Some monarchs reaching the state remain and breed, and researchers are still working out how Florida's resident and migratory populations interact.

Take care

Conservation

The migratory monarch is listed as Vulnerable by the IUCN. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service has proposed listing the Monarch as Threatened under the Endangered Species Act, but no final rule has yet been issued, so that proposed federal protection is not currently in effect. Habitat loss, pesticide exposure, climate change and conditions at breeding, migratory and overwintering sites all contribute to concern.
