Yellow-bellied Sapsucker

Sphyrapicus varius
On this page
At a glanceHow to know itWhere to find it hereHow it livesWhat it does here

At a glance

  • It drills orderly rows of sapwells and returns to keep them open, drinking the sap and eating insects attracted to it.
  • Sapsucker wells also feed other wildlife. Hummingbirds and other birds, insects and sometimes mammals drink sap from holes they could not open themselves.
  • Yellow-bellied Sapsuckers breed far to the north and spend the winter in Florida, making them a seasonal woodpecker here rather than a year-round resident.
  • The yellow belly can be surprisingly faint. A bold white stripe along the folded wing is often a more reliable field mark.

How to know it

Easily confused with

Red-bellied Woodpecker — Red-bellied Woodpeckers are year-round Florida residents and are larger, with barred black-and-white backs and no long white stripe along the folded wing.

Downy Woodpecker — Downy Woodpeckers are smaller and lack the sapsucker's bold wing stripe and its rows of sapwells.

Summary

A medium-sized black-and-white woodpecker with a red forehead and a bold white stripe along the folded wing. Males have a red throat; females have a white throat. The pale belly may show only a faint yellow wash.

In flight

The white wing stripe flashes prominently in flight. Like other woodpeckers, it flies in an undulating series of flaps and glides.

Look for the holes

Even without the bird, neat horizontal rows of small holes in bark can reveal a sapsucker's presence.

Voice

Calls include a nasal, catlike mew. Drumming is irregular and stuttering rather than an even roll, and birds sometimes drum on resonant metal surfaces.

Size

About eight inches long, with a wingspan around fifteen inches.

What to look for

Look first for the bold white stripe along the folded wing and red on the forehead. Males also have a red throat. On trees, watch for orderly rows of small sapwells.

Where to find it here

Scan trunks of mature trees for neat horizontal rows of small holes. Once you find active sapwells, watch quietly from a distance; a sapsucker may return to feed or maintain them.

A winter visitor to Florida, generally present from fall into spring. Palma Sola currently has only one documented observation, so local peak timing and regularity are not yet known.

When

Winter visitor to Florida. Palma Sola has too few observations to define a local peak.

How it lives

Sapwells

It drills rows of small holes through bark and returns repeatedly to keep them open. Sap collects at the wells, providing food for the bird and attracting insects.

It also eats insects and spiders from bark and takes fruit and berries.

Like other woodpeckers, it climbs trunks using strong feet and a stiff tail for support.

What it does here

Sapsucker wells create a food source used by many other animals, including hummingbirds and insects. The bird also excavates nesting cavities on its breeding grounds that can later be used by other cavity-nesting animals.

Few birds alter living plants as visibly as a sapsucker. It drills and maintains sapwells in the bark of many kinds of trees, returning repeatedly to drink the flowing sap. Trees differ in how readily their sap flows, and individual birds may favor particular trees. The rows of old and active wells can remain visible long after the bird has moved on.

Native to North America. Yellow-bellied Sapsuckers breed mainly in northern forests and migrate south for winter, when they occur throughout Florida and farther into the Caribbean, Mexico and Central America.
