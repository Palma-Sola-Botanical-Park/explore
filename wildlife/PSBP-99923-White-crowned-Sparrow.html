White-crowned Sparrow

Zonotrichia leucophrys

At a glance

  • Crisp and dapper, with bold Oreo-cookie black-and-white head stripes and a bright pinkish-yellow bill - unmistakable, though an uncommon find this far south.
  • Not a local. These are winter visitors, dropping in after a long haul from breeding grounds way up in Canada and Alaska.
  • Watch the feet: they do a funny backward double-hop on the ground, kicking away leaves to scratch up seeds like a little feathered wind-up toy.
  • A brush-pile bird - look low, along weedy edges and thickets, not up in the trees.

How to know it

The crown

Adults wear unmistakable high-contrast black-and-white head stripes. A bird with brown-and-tan stripes instead isn't a different species - it's a youngster on its first trip south.

Posture

Sits noticeably upright when alert, showing off a sleek, pearly-grey breast.

The double-scratch

On the ground it feeds with the "double-scratch" - a quick two-footed backward hop that kicks leaves away to reveal hidden seeds. It's an inherited, instinctive move: even birds raised without leaf litter do it automatically.

Voice

A sweet, clear whistled song that opens with one or two long notes and ends in a buzzy trill. Even in winter you'll hear it practicing, along with a sharp chink call note from the bushes as birds keep track of one another.

Size

about 6.5 to 7 inches (a fairly large, long-tailed sparrow)

What to look for

Look low, not high. Scan brushy borders, overgrown fence lines, and low shrubbery near open grassy areas for an upright, boldly striped sparrow scratching in the leaf litter.

Where to find it here

Look low, not high. Brushy borders, overgrown fence lines, and the low shrubbery near open grassy areas - classic edge habitat, not the canopy.

An uncommon winter visitor, generally here from about October to April. Early morning is best, scanning the brushy edges. Don't expect a guaranteed sighting - finding one is a small birding win. Gone by late spring.

When

A winter visitor only - arrives around October from breeding grounds in Canada, Alaska, and the western mountains, and heads back north by April. Absent in summer.

How it lives

A ground bird of edges and weedy borders. It scratches through leaf litter with a two-footed backward hop, and stays low in brush rather than going up into the canopy.

Mainly seeds here in winter — weed seeds, grass seeds, berries, whatever spills from a feeder. It takes insects too, mostly on the breeding grounds up north.

What it does here

A winter seed-eater that helps disperse the seeds of weeds and grasses, and part of the seasonal influx of northern songbirds that pass the colder months in Florida.

Forages along brushy edges and weedy ground, feeding on the seeds of grasses, weeds, and low shrubs.

Breeds across the far north - Alaska, northern Canada, and high western mountains - and winters across much of the United States and Mexico. In Southwest Florida it's an uncommon winter visitor; you usually have to look for it, here from roughly October to April before it heads back north.

While many sparrows look like little brown streaks of camouflage, the adult White-crowned Sparrow stands out like it's wearing a tiny cycling helmet. It brings a bit of northern flair down to Florida for the colder months and makes itself right at home in our thickets and brushy edges.

It's a ground forager with a signature move: the "double-scratch," a quick two-footed hop backward that kicks leaf litter aside to uncover seeds. Even in winter, when it isn't nesting, you'll hear it practicing its song or trading sharp chink call notes with others from deep in the bushes.

And consider the trip it made to get here: some White-crowned Sparrows fly more than 2,500 miles from the Alaskan tundra to their wintering grounds - an astonishing haul for a bird that weighs about as much as a few quarters.
