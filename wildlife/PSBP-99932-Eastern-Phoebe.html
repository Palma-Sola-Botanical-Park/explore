Eastern Phoebe

Sayornis phoebe
On this page
At a glanceHow to know itWhere to find it hereHow it livesWhat it does here

At a glance

  • An Eastern Phoebe hunts from a perch, darting out after a flying insect and often returning to exactly the same branch.
  • The tail-wagging is nearly constant, and it is the quickest way to know one — few small birds pump their tail so persistently.
  • It says its own name: a burry two-note fee-bee, repeated from a favorite perch.
  • A winter visitor here. It breeds farther north and comes back each fall.

How to know it

Shape

An upright, medium-sized flycatcher, plain gray-brown above and pale below, with a small dark bill.

Tail

Wags almost constantly. This is the best field mark on the bird.

Voice

A burry, emphatic fee-bee, repeated from a perch.

Plain is the point. There is no wing bar, no eye ring and no color to fix on, so posture and the tail do the work.

Easily confused with

Great Crested Flycatcher — Great Crested Flycatchers are larger and show a yellow belly and rusty tail. The phoebe is plain gray-brown and wags its tail almost constantly.

Where to find it here

Watch exposed perches at the edge of open ground — fence lines, low branches, the corners of buildings. A phoebe comes back to the same perch after each sally, so once you have found one it stays findable.

When

A winter visitor, generally present from autumn into early spring.

How it lives

It picks an exposed perch, pumps its tail, darts out to take an insect in the air, and comes back to the same spot. Then does it again.

Flying insects almost entirely, with some small fruits in winter when insects are scarce. Usually alone.

What it does here

Open branch tips matter to a phoebe because they give an unobstructed launch over open air. Dense foliage can hold plenty of insects, but the bird needs somewhere to watch from and room to fly out after them.

An experiment in 1804

In 1804 John James Audubon tied silver threads to the legs of young Eastern Phoebes to see whether they would return the following year. Some did. The experiment is generally regarded as the first bird-banding experiment in North America.
