Eastern Phoebe

Sayornis phoebe
On this page
At a glanceHow to know itWhere to find it hereHow it livesWhat it does here

At a glance

  • A plain, grayish-brown bird that sits upright on an exposed perch, wags its tail, and darts out to catch a flying insect — that is an eastern phoebe.
  • The tail-wagging is almost constant and is the quickest way to recognize it — few other small birds pump their tail so persistently.
  • It calls its own name: a burry, two-note 'fee-bee' that it repeats from a favorite perch.
  • A winter visitor to Florida, it breeds farther north and returns each fall.

How to know it

Shape

Upright posture, medium-sized flycatcher. Plain grayish-brown above, pale below.

Tail

Wags persistently — the best field mark.

Bill

Small and dark — a flycatcher bill.

Voice

A burry 'fee-bee' repeated from a perch.

Voice

A burry, emphatic 'fee-bee' — says its name.

Size

About six inches long, with a wingspan around ten inches.

What to look for

A plain gray-brown bird on an exposed perch, wagging its tail and darting out after insects.

Easily confused with

Great Crested Flycatcher — Great Crested Flycatchers are larger and show a yellow belly and rusty tail. The phoebe is plain gray-brown and wags its tail almost constantly.

Where to find it here

Watch exposed perches at the edge of open ground — fence lines, low branches, the corners of buildings. A phoebe returns to the same perch after each sally, so once you find one it tends to stay findable.

A winter visitor to Florida, generally present from autumn into early spring.

When

Breeds to the north; winters in Florida.

How it lives

It picks an exposed perch, pumps its tail, darts out to take an insect in the air, and comes back to the same spot. Then does it again.

Flying insects almost entirely, with some small fruits in winter when insects are scarce. Usually alone.

What it does here

A predator of flying insects, taken on short sallies from a perch. Phoebes are in turn prey for small hawks and other predators.

Trees and shrubs matter to a phoebe as furniture rather than food. It hunts from an exposed twig with clear air in front of it, so open branch tips at the edge of a planting are more useful to it than dense cover. In winter it also takes small fruits when insects are scarce.

Native to eastern North America. Breeds across the eastern U.S. and southern Canada; winters in the southeastern U.S. and Mexico. A winter visitor in the Bradenton area.

The Eastern Phoebe was the first bird ever banded in North America. In 1804 John James Audubon tied silver thread to the legs of nestlings to see whether the same birds returned the following year. They did.
