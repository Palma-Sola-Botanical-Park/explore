European Starling

Sturnus vulgaris

At a glance

  • About 100 European Starlings were released in New York's Central Park in 1890 and 1891. That introduced population became established, and within decades starlings had spread across much of North America.
  • After the fall molt, fresh feathers have pale tips that give the bird a heavily spotted appearance. Through winter those tips gradually wear away, revealing the glossy green-and-purple breeding plumage underneath.
  • Starlings use a foraging technique called gaping. They push the closed bill into soil or leaf litter and then force it open, spreading the material apart to expose insects and other food hidden underneath.
  • Starlings are skilled mimics. Their long, varied songs can include imitations of other birds and sounds from their surroundings.

How to know it

Size and shape

A stocky bird, about eight inches long, with a short tail and long pointed bill. On the ground it walks rather than hops. In flight, the wings look pointed and triangular.

Breeding plumage

Glossy black with green and purple iridescence that changes with the light. The bill becomes yellow in breeding plumage and the legs are pinkish.

Non-breeding plumage

After the fall molt, fresh feathers are tipped in white and buff, giving the bird a heavily speckled appearance. The bill darkens to gray-brown. As winter progresses and feather tips wear away, the speckles gradually disappear.

Juveniles

Young birds are plain gray-brown with a dark bill and can look very different from the adults around them. They begin acquiring spotted adult plumage later in their first year.

In flight

The short tail and pointed triangular wings give the bird a distinctive shape in flight. Wingbeats are rapid, often interrupted by short glides.

Similar species

Often found alongside Common Grackles and other blackbirds, but starlings are stockier, shorter-tailed, and have a straight pointed bill rather than the curved bill of a grackle.

Voice

The song is a long mixture of whistles, rattles, warbles and harsher sounds. Starlings also imitate other birds, sometimes well enough to cause confusion about which species is calling.

Size

About eight inches long, with a wingspan a little over a foot.

What to look for

Look for a stocky, short-tailed bird walking across lawns and probing the ground with a pointed bill. Adults are glossy and dark in spring and heavily spotted after the fall molt. Common Grackles are larger, longer-tailed and have heavier bills.

Easily confused with

Common Grackle — Larger and longer-tailed than a European Starling, with a heavier bill. A starling has a compact body, short tail and more pointed wings.

Where to find it here

Look on lawns, short grass and other open ground, where groups walk while probing for food. Starlings also use garden edges, parking areas and other developed spaces. Check trees and structures for birds entering cavities, and watch for flocks gathering toward evening.

Present in southwest Florida year-round. Starlings are active by day and often forage in groups on open ground.

When

Present year-round in southwest Florida. Flock size and local abundance can vary through the year.

How it lives

Starlings are relentlessly social. They feed in groups on open ground, walking quickly and probing the turf as they go, and gather into large flocks to roost.

The diet shifts with whatever is going: invertebrates probed out of the soil, then fruit, seed and grain as the season turns. That flexibility is much of why the species has done so well everywhere it has been introduced.

What it does here

Starlings are native to Europe and western Asia and reached North America by deliberate release in the 1890s. From that start they spread across the continent, and introduced populations are now established on several others.

The real effect is on nesting. Starlings take cavities, and they compete with native cavity-nesters — woodpeckers, bluebirds, martins — for a limited supply of holes, usually winning because they are aggressive and start early. Large flocks can also do agricultural damage where they gather around crops or livestock.

Large starling flocks sometimes form murmurations, with hundreds or thousands of birds turning together in shifting shapes before entering a communal roost. The tight flocking may make it harder for a predator to isolate one bird.
