Mabel Orchard Orbweaver

Leucauge argyrobapta

At a glance

  • Its orb web is usually tilted close to horizontal rather than standing vertically. The spider hangs beneath the center with its belly facing upward.
  • When disturbed, the spider may drop from its web on a silk safety line and hide in the vegetation or leaf litter below.
  • Florida's Mabel Orchard Orbweaver was long treated as the same species as the Orchard Orbweaver farther north. Modern research separated the two, so older references may call Florida spiders Leucauge venusta.
  • A spider does not need to see an insect hit its web. Vibrations traveling through the silk tell it where struggling prey has landed.

How to know it

Size and shape

A small, slender spider with a long abdomen and thin legs. Females are about a quarter-inch long; males are smaller.

Top of the abdomen

The top of the elongated abdomen is silvery-white with darker longitudinal markings.

Underside

The underside has silvery, greenish and yellow markings with orange-red near the rear. That red-orange can cause momentary confusion with a black widow, but the body shape and the web are very different.

Legs and body

The long slender legs are greenish with darker joints. The front part of the body is pale greenish to yellow-brown.

Similar species

The closely related Leucauge argyra also occurs in Florida and can look very similar. Orange-red abdominal markings help distinguish Mabel Orchard Orbweaver, but close photographs may be needed for a confident identification.

Size

Females about a quarter-inch long; males smaller.

What to look for

Look for a small silver-bodied spider beneath a tilted orb web stretched between low branches and stems. The abdomen is elongated, the legs are slender and greenish, and orange-red markings occur underneath. The web is often built fairly low in shrubby vegetation.

Easily confused with

Leucauge argyra — The closely related Leucauge argyra also occurs in Florida and is very similar. Mabel Orchard Orbweaver typically shows orange-red markings that help separate the two, although photographs may be needed for a confident identification.

Where to find it here

Look among low, branchy vegetation along garden edges, shrubby paths and lightly wooded areas. The tilted orb web is often built from knee to waist height and becomes easiest to see when sunlight or dew catches the silk.

Look during warm, humid weather when webs are conspicuous among low vegetation. The species is common in Florida, but there are not enough Palma Sola observations to define a local seasonal peak.

When

Common in Florida during warm weather. Local seasonal timing at Palma Sola is not yet well established.

How it lives

The female waits beneath the center of her web and detects trapped prey through vibrations in the silk. She moves to the struggling insect, bites it and wraps it in silk.

The web catches small flying insects such as gnats, mosquitoes and flies.

Males wander in search of females rather than maintaining prey-catching webs. A male approaching a female may signal through vibrations in her silk.

What it does here

Shrubs and low tree branches provide the framework for the web. The spider stretches its supporting threads between stems and leaves, so dense, branchy vegetation creates many possible web sites. Orchard orbweavers are especially common in moist gardens, woodland edges and Florida citrus groves.
