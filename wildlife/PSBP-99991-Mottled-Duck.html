Mottled Duck

Anas fulvigula
On this page

At a glance

  • The mottled duck is Florida's own duck — a subspecies found almost nowhere else, living in the state's marshes and ponds all year long.
  • Unlike most ducks, it does not migrate; it is a true Florida resident, hatched, raised, and living out its life within the state.
  • Males and females look alike — warm mottled brown — so the easiest way to tell them apart is the bill: yellow-green on the male, orange-and-dark on the female.
  • Its biggest threat is an unexpected one: interbreeding with released domestic mallards, which is slowly diluting the wild Florida population.

How to know it

Easily confused with

Mallard (female) — A female mallard shows white in the tail and a white-bordered blue wing patch; the mottled duck is darker and more uniform, with little to no white and an unmarked buff face.

Plumage

Both sexes are warm, mottled brown overall, with a slightly paler, plainer head and neck and a darker body.

Bill

Male's is olive-green to yellow; female's is orange with dark blotches.

In flight

Shows a blue-purple wing patch (speculum), usually without the bold white borders a female mallard shows.

Voice

The female gives a mallard-like quack; the male a quieter, raspier call.

Size

19 to 22 inches; about 2.5 feet (30 inches)

What to look for

A brown duck that looks a bit like a female mallard but with a plainer, lighter head. Check the bill color to tell the sexes apart. Birds showing odd patches of white, a curled tail feather, or a hint of green on the head are likely mallard hybrids, not pure mottled ducks.

Where to find it here

On and around the ponds, often in pairs. Look for a plain-headed brown duck; check the bill color and watch for hybrid traits.

Year-round resident — one of the few ducks you can count on in Florida in any season. Most likely to be seen on the open ponds.

How it lives

Usually in pairs or small groups rather than big flocks, dabbling in shallow water and grazing along marsh edges. It nests on the ground in dense vegetation near water, mainly February through July.

Roughly forty percent animal matter — insects, snails, small fish, crayfish — and sixty percent plant, including the seeds, stems and roots of marsh plants. It feeds by dabbling and tipping up.

What it does here

A native marsh duck that spreads plant seeds and helps cycle nutrients in wetlands. Its decline is a warning sign about both wetland loss and the effects of releasing non-native animals.

Feeds on the seeds, stems, and roots of marsh plants, alongside aquatic invertebrates.

The Florida mottled duck is a non-migratory subspecies endemic to peninsular Florida — it lives only here, year-round. (A separate population of mottled ducks occurs along the Gulf Coast of Texas and Louisiana.)

Most dabbling ducks breed up north and only visit Florida in winter. The mottled duck flips that script: it stays put, nesting and raising young in Florida's fresh and brackish marshes from late winter into summer.

That homebody lifestyle is also its vulnerability. People release domestic and feral mallards onto Florida ponds — often with good intentions — and those mallards interbreed with mottled ducks. The hybrids (locally nicknamed muddled ducks) are fertile, so each generation blends the two further. Wildlife biologists rank this genetic mixing as the single biggest threat to the pure Florida mottled duck, and state law actually prohibits releasing mallards because of it.

The park's own records include a few of these mottled-duck-by-mallard hybrids, a small local window onto a statewide conservation problem.

Take care

What to do

We love all ducks — but local ponds stay healthiest when domestic mallards don't mix with our wild, non-migratory mottled ducks. Please don't feed the ducks (bread and snacks harm their health and encourage the very mallard releases that threaten this species), and never release pet or feed-store mallards, which is both harmful and illegal in Florida.

Conservation

Not federally listed, but a species of real conservation concern in Florida. Hybridization with released mallards, plus wetland loss, have put the pure Florida mottled duck on conservation watch lists.
