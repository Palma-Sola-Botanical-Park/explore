Eastern Lubber Grasshopper

Romalea microptera
On this page

At a glance

  • One of Florida's largest grasshoppers, an adult Eastern Lubber can reach about three inches long.
  • The wings are too small for sustained flight. Adults mostly walk and climb, with only short hops when disturbed.
  • The black, yellow, orange or red coloration is a warning. Lubbers contain defensive chemicals and can release a foul-smelling froth when threatened.
  • Lubbers eat many kinds of plants. In gardens, a large group can cause conspicuous damage even though the insect itself is native to Florida.

How to know it

Adult

Very large and heavy-bodied. Adults are usually black with yellow, orange or red markings, though coloration varies.

Nymph

Young lubbers are mostly black with a bright yellow, orange or red stripe running down the back, and look different enough that visitors often take them for another species.

Wings

Adults have short wings that do not support normal flight.

Size

Adults can reach about three inches long; females are larger than males.

What to look for

Look for a very large, slow-moving grasshopper walking through low vegetation or across paths. Adults are brightly patterned; young lubbers are mostly black with a colored stripe down the back.

Where to find it here

Look in sunny planting beds, weedy edges and low vegetation. Lubbers feed on a wide range of plants and are often easiest to notice walking across paths between feeding sites.

Most visible from spring through warm weather as nymphs grow into large adults. Local Palma Sola records are too limited to define a precise peak.

When

Nymphs appear in spring and grow into adults through the warm months. Palma Sola records are too limited to define a local peak.

How it lives

Lubbers cannot fly well. They move mainly by walking and climbing through vegetation.

Chemistry instead of escape

When threatened, they rely more on chemical defense than escape. Bright coloration advertises the defense, and a disturbed lubber may hiss and release a foul-smelling secretion.

What it eats in a garden

They eat leaves from many kinds of plants. When numbers are high, feeding can seriously damage garden beds.

What it does here

Lubbers eat a great deal and are eaten by very little. They carry chemical defenses that make them unpalatable to most predators, which is how an insect this large, this slow and this brightly marked can afford to sit out in the open.

Plants are its food. Eastern Lubbers feed on a wide variety of leaves and move readily between wild vegetation and cultivated beds. Members of the amaryllis family are documented favorites, and the park grows several — crinum and amaryllis among them. A few insects usually cause limited damage; large concentrations can defoliate individual plants.

Loggerhead Shrikes are among the few predators known to handle chemically defended lubbers. A shrike may impale prey on a thorn or barbed wire before eating it.

Take care

The risk

No meaningful danger when left alone. Do not handle or eat lubbers; a disturbed insect can release irritating, foul-smelling defensive secretions. Wash your hands if you come into contact with one.

What to do

Watch without handling. If lubbers are damaging park plants, leave control to park staff rather than crushing, spraying or moving them.
