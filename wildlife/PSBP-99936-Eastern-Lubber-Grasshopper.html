Romalea microptera

At a glance

  • The enormous, brightly colored grasshopper lumbering across the path — too big and too slow to be any other species — is an eastern lubber.
  • It can barely fly. Those tiny wings are essentially useless for flight, so it walks, climbs, and hops instead. The name 'lubber' comes from an old word meaning clumsy or slow — fitting for an insect that has traded speed for attitude.
  • The bold yellow, orange, and black adult pattern and the jet-black nymphal stripe are aposematic warning coloration, an honest advertisement to birds and reptiles that this insect is toxic and tastes terrible.
  • Most predators learn quickly to leave it alone. The loggerhead shrike, however, skewers the grasshopper on a thorn or barbed wire and waits up to several days for the toxins to break down before eating it.

How to know it

Size

Up to 3 inches — by far the largest grasshopper you will see.

Color

Adults are yellow to orange with bold black markings. Juveniles are strikingly different: glossy jet-black with a vivid yellow, orange, or red stripe running the length of the body and red-tinged legs. They look so unlike the adults that visitors routinely mistake them for a different species — they are not.

Wings

Tiny, vestigial — cannot support flight.

Behavior

Slow, deliberate walker. Does not jump far or fly.

Voice

A hissing sound when threatened.

Size

about 2.5 to 3 inches

What to look for

In spring, watch for small glossy black nymphs with a bright red, orange, or yellow stripe — unmistakably lubbers in their juvenile form. By summer, those same insects grow into large, boldly patterned yellow-and-black adults lumbering slowly along paths or through garden beds. Both stages are big, bold, and easy to spot.

Where to find it here

Paths, garden beds, and low vegetation. Hard to miss when present.

Most visible spring through fall; juveniles emerge in spring.

When

Juveniles appear in spring; adults most visible summer through fall.

How it lives

Walks slowly and deliberately. When threatened, hisses, spreads tiny wings, and produces toxic foam.

Broadly polyphagous — recorded feeding on plants from more than 38 families. In Florida gardens, plants in the family Amaryllidaceae (especially Amaryllis and Crinum lilies) are particularly favored, along with Agapanthus, Narcissus, oleander, and citrus. Can cause significant defoliation of ornamental beds.

What it does here

Herbivore with a wide host-plant range and a mild garden-pest reputation. Its potent chemical defenses make it toxic to most vertebrate predators, functioning as a chemical dead-end in the food web. The loggerhead shrike is its primary known avian predator, defeating the toxins by aging impaled carcasses before consumption.

Feeds on many garden and wild plants.

Native to the southeastern United States. Common in Florida year-round.

The eastern lubber is the unmistakable giant of Florida's grasshoppers — up to three inches long, painted in warning colors, and moving with a slow, confident waddle that says 'I dare you.' The name 'lubber' traces to an old English word for a clumsy, heavy person, which suits this insect perfectly.

That confidence is earned. When harassed, it hisses, spreads its tiny wings to flash bright colors, and forces air and fluid through its thoracic spiracles to generate a foul-smelling chemical foam containing plant-derived compounds including phenols, quinones, and terpenes. These are sequestered from the plants it eats and repel virtually every predator that tries it.

The bold adult coloration and the juvenile's black-with-red-stripe pattern are both examples of aposematic warning coloration — a visual signal that tells birds and reptiles the insect is unpalatable before they even attempt an attack.

The loggerhead shrike (Lanius ludovicianus) has found a workaround. It skewers the lubber on a thorn or barbed wire and leaves it exposed for up to several days, allowing the defensive chemicals to degrade, then eats the safe remains — sometimes discarding the chemical-laden thorax entirely.

Juveniles are jet-black with a distinctive yellow, orange, or red stripe and red-marked legs — looking so different from the adults that visitors frequently mistake them for a separate species. They emerge in spring, grow through five (occasionally six) nymphal instars, and reach full adult coloration in about 40 to 60 days.

Females deposit subterranean egg pods containing 30 to 50 eggs at a depth of roughly 1¼ to 2 inches in the soil, where they overwinter for six to eight months before hatching in spring. The lubber's one-generation-per-year cycle is tightly synchronized with Florida's seasonal plant growth.
