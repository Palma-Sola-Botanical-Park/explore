Monk Skipper

Choranthus capucinus

At a glance

  • One of the largest skippers you will see in the park — dark brown, broad-winged, and unusually calm. It tends to perch motionless on low vegetation for long stretches, making it one of the easiest butterflies here to actually study up close.
  • The name comes from the same source as cappuccino: Capuchin friars, whose brown robes the butterfly's rich chestnut coloring was thought to resemble.
  • Its caterpillars eat only palms, so wherever ornamental and native palms thrive, the Monk Skipper is likely not far behind. Florida's suburban palm gardens have suited it very well.
  • Originally a Cuban species, the Monk Skipper established itself in Florida from strays crossing the Florida Straits — the first confirmed breeding population appeared in 1947.

How to know it

Size

A large skipper — wingspan 1 7/8 to 2 3/8 inches. Females tend to run slightly larger than males.

Upperside

Males are dark, nearly black-brown with a gray stigma (scent patch) on the forewing and a warm orange-brown wash along the base of the leading edge. Females are somewhat paler brownish-black with a diffuse pale patch on the forewing.

Underside

Both sexes share a rich mahogany-chestnut red on the hindwing underside — the most distinctive mark when the butterfly is perched with wings folded. The head, thorax, and abdomen show blue-gray hair-scales.

Resting posture

Wings are typically held flat and folded, not spread, giving a broad, triangular outline reminiscent of a moth at rest. This is the most reliable field character for finding a perched individual.

Voice

Essentially silent, as with virtually all butterflies. Produces no song or call; the soft rustle of wing movement is the only sound associated with this species.

Size

Wingspan 1 7/8 to 2 3/8 inches; body length roughly 3/4 to 1 inches

What to look for

Look for a large, dark butterfly sitting very still on a palm frond or low shrub, wings folded flat. The mahogany underwing and blue-gray furry thorax distinguish it from smaller, more uniformly brown skippers. Females show a faint pale forewing patch; males are darker overall with a gray stigma. The calm, unhurried perching style is as much a field mark as any color pattern.

Where to find it here

Check the undersides of palm fronds and the broad leaves of shrubs throughout the park, especially near saw palmetto stands or any ornamental palm plantings. Garden beds with large tubular flowers — hibiscus in particular — are good spots to find adults nectaring. This is a butterfly that rewards slow walking rather than scanning; look for something sitting very still that you might otherwise take for a fallen leaf.

Present in Manatee County for most of the year, with adults recorded from at least March through December and possibly in warm winter months as well. Multiple broods mean there is rarely a long gap between flights. Adults are most active and visible during morning and afternoon hours on warm, sunny days.

When

Year-round in south Florida; in central Florida (including Manatee County) recorded March through at least December across multiple broods. May appear in January and February during mild winters.

How it lives

Unusually still for a skipper. It will perch motionless on a palm frond, a broad leaf or a garden flower for long stretches, and when it does move the flight is direct rather than the frantic darting most skippers manage.

The caterpillar's tube

It rolls a section of palm frond and binds it with silk into a tube, hides inside by day, and comes out to feed at night. Saw palmetto, cabbage palm, date palm and coconut palm all serve.

Adults drink from large tubular flowers — hibiscus is the documented favorite — reaching depths that shorter-tongued insects cannot. Three or four broods a year in Florida.

What it does here

The Monk Skipper is native to Cuba and the wider West Indies, where it remains widespread and common. Strays began reaching southern Florida in the mid-twentieth century, and an established breeding population was first confirmed in 1947. Today it is resident throughout peninsular Florida and the Keys, with the UF/IFAS Florida Butterfly Gardening database listing Manatee County among its documented counties. Farther south it flies year-round; in central Florida, including this area, adults are recorded from at least March through December.

As a caterpillar, the Monk Skipper is a minor consumer of palm foliage and becomes prey for birds, spiders, and parasitic wasps. As an adult, it functions as a pollinator of the flowers it visits, particularly larger tubular blooms. It sits within a straightforward food web linking Florida's ubiquitous native and ornamental palms upward to insectivorous birds and invertebrate predators.

Among Florida's skippers, the Monk is unusually easy to appreciate at a standstill. Rather than the rapid, darting flight typical of many family members, adults spend long periods perched with their wings folded flat against low leaves and stems — the posture giving it a distinctly moth-like silhouette.

That patience is your opportunity: get close, and you can see the rich chestnut-brown of the underwings and the blue-gray scaling on the thorax and abdomen.

The Monk Skipper is essentially tied to palms at every stage of its early life. Females lay eggs on palm fronds, and the caterpillars construct leaf-roll shelters by cutting a section of frond and binding the edges with silk — a tidy cylinder in which they rest during the day and from which they emerge to feed at night.

Florida's abundance of both native palms (saw palmetto, cabbage palm) and ornamental species has given the species a very comfortable home far from its Cuban origins.

Adults have notably long probosces for their size, well adapted for probing deep tubular flowers. Hibiscus is a frequently cited nectar source, and in Cuba adults have been recorded visiting lantana, blue porterweed, Spanish needles, and bougainvillea. At Palma Sola, any garden bed with large, open-throated blossoms is worth checking.
