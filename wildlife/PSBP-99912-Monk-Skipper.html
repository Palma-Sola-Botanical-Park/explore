Monk Skipper

Choranthus capucinus

At a glance

  • This is a large, dark skipper with rich chestnut coloring underneath the wings and bluish-gray scaling on the body.
  • A caterpillar folds or binds part of a palm frond into a shelter, resting inside and feeding on the surrounding leaf.
  • Its caterpillars feed on palms, including native saw palmetto and cabbage palm as well as several ornamental palms.
  • Native to Cuba and nearby Caribbean islands, the Monk Skipper became established in Florida by the late 1940s and is now widespread through the peninsula.

How to know it

Size

A large skipper with a wingspan around two inches.

Upperside

Dark brown to nearly black above, with females generally somewhat paler.

Underside

The underside is rich chestnut brown, while bluish-gray hairs can show on the head and body.

What to look for

Look for a large, dark skipper around palms and flowering garden plants. When perched, the rich chestnut underside and bluish-gray scaling on the body help separate it from smaller brown skippers.

Easily confused with

Other brown skippers — Several skippers in Florida are brown and easily confused. The Monk Skipper is notably large, with a mostly dark upperside and a rich chestnut underside; most look-alikes are smaller and differently marked below.

Where to find it here

Look around palm plantings and nearby flowering beds. Adults visit flowers, while eggs and caterpillars occur on palm fronds. Saw palmetto and cabbage palm are among the documented host plants.

In central Florida, adults are recorded through much of the year, especially from spring through late fall. Palma Sola currently has too few records to say how regularly the species occurs here.

When

Recorded from March through December in central Florida. Palma Sola has too few observations to define a local pattern.

How it lives

Adults perch on vegetation between flights and visit flowers for nectar. Males may use lookout perches and fly out rapidly toward other insects entering their area.

A shelter made of palm

A caterpillar folds and binds palm leaf tissue with silk to make a shelter, then feeds on the surrounding frond.

What it does here

The Monk Skipper is a Caribbean butterfly, native to Cuba, the Bahamas and neighbouring islands, that became established in Florida during the mid-twentieth century.

Palms are the center of the Monk Skipper's life cycle. Females lay eggs on palm foliage, and the caterpillars both eat the leaves and use them to build silk-bound shelters. Native saw palmetto and cabbage palm are hosts, along with several ornamental palms. Adults leave the palms to feed at flowers nearby.

Also known as
