Cape May Warbler

Setophaga tigrina
On this page

At a glance

  • The Cape May Warbler has an unusually shaped, semitubular tongue that helps it drink nectar from flowers and juice from fruit.
  • The scientific name tigrina refers to the dark streaks across the male's yellow breast.
  • On its northern breeding grounds, Cape May Warbler numbers rise when spruce budworms become abundant. In years with abundant food, females can lay unusually large clutches for a warbler.
  • Florida is mostly a stop along the journey. Most Cape May Warblers continue across the Caribbean to their wintering grounds.

How to know it

Male

Breeding males have yellow underparts heavily streaked with black, a chestnut cheek patch, dark eyestripe and a large white patch in the wing.

Female and nonbreeding

Females and immature birds are much duller, usually gray-olive above and pale yellowish below with fine streaking. A yellowish rump, short tail and slightly downcurved bill can help with identification.

Confusion species

Dull birds can resemble other migrating warblers. Cape May Warblers have a relatively short tail, fine streaking across the breast and sides, and a slender bill that curves slightly downward. Palm Warblers repeatedly pump the tail; Yellow-rumped Warblers show a brighter yellow rump.

Voice

The song is a very high, thin series of repeated notes. The call is a soft, high sip. The song can be difficult to hear for people who have lost sensitivity to high frequencies.

Size

About five inches long, with a wingspan around eight inches.

What to look for

In spring, look for a yellow bird with heavy dark breast streaking and a chestnut cheek patch. Duller birds are harder: look for the short tail, streaked underparts and slightly downcurved bill. Cape May Warblers often forage high in trees, so a clear view may take patience.

Easily confused with

Palm Warbler — Palm Warblers characteristically bob the tail. Cape May Warblers have a short tail, streaked breast and slightly downcurved bill.

Yellow-rumped Warbler — Yellow-rumped Warblers show a brighter yellow rump. A dull Cape May shows finer streaking below and a slimmer, slightly downcurved bill.

Where to find it here

Scan the upper parts of trees during migration, especially branch tips where birds search for insects. Also check flowering trees and shrubs: Cape May Warblers sometimes come lower to drink nectar. The weeping bottlebrush in the park is the kind of nectar-rich flowering tree worth checking.

Primarily a migrant through Florida, especially during spring and fall, with some birds wintering in southern Florida. Palma Sola has recorded the species in spring, but there are too few park observations to establish a local seasonal pattern.

When

Passage migrant through Florida and occasional winter visitor. Palma Sola records are currently too limited to define a local peak.

How it lives

Cape May Warblers usually forage high in trees, picking insects from foliage and branch tips and sometimes flying out to catch insects in the air. Migration occurs mostly at night.

Outside the breeding season they also visit flowers for nectar and may defend productive flowering plants from other nectar feeders.

What it does here

Cape May Warblers are important predators of spruce budworm caterpillars on their northern breeding grounds. During migration and winter they also feed at flowers, putting them in direct contact with pollen as they drink nectar.

This warbler changes its relationship with plants through the year. In northern spruce forests it searches needles and branch tips for spruce budworm caterpillars. During migration and winter it also visits flowers for nectar and punctures fruit for juice. Flowering trees and shrubs can therefore be good places to look for one in Florida.

Native to North America and the Caribbean region. Cape May Warblers breed in boreal forests across Canada and the northern United States, migrate through Florida, and winter mainly in the Caribbean.

Take care

Conservation

Globally classified as Least Concern, but long-term monitoring has documented substantial population declines. Its numbers also fluctuate with outbreaks of spruce budworm on the breeding grounds.
