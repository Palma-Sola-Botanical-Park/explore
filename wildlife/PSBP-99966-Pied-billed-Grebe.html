Pied-billed Grebe

Podilymbus podiceps
On this page

At a glance

  • A small, plump brown waterbird that dives and vanishes the moment you notice it.
  • It can sink rather than dive, compressing its feathers to lose buoyancy and slip under with barely a ripple.
  • The short, thick bill is unlike a duck's — in the breeding season it carries a black band that gives the bird its name.
  • It is rarely seen flying or on land. Almost everything it does happens on or under the water.

How to know it

Size and shape

Small and stocky, with a short neck and almost no visible tail. About a foot long.

Color

Plain brown overall, paler on the throat.

Bill

Short and thick rather than flat like a duck's, with a black band across it in the breeding season.

Voice

Surprisingly loud for its size in the breeding season — a far-carrying series of hollow kuk-kuk-kuk-cow-cow-cow calls from marshy cover.

What to look for

Watch for a small brown bird that keeps diving and reappearing somewhere else. Be patient; it spends a lot of time underwater.

Where to find it here

On the open water of the ponds, often near the weedy edges where it can slip into cover.

Most numerous in autumn and winter, with some present year-round.

How it lives

Dives frequently to feed or to escape, taking crayfish, small fish, aquatic insects and other small water creatures.

Sinking instead of diving

By compressing its plumage it can reduce its buoyancy and settle under the surface without the splash of a dive, sometimes leaving only the head above water.

It would rather sink than fly. A grebe escapes by submerging, and it can control how it rides — trapping or releasing air in its plumage to float high or settle until only the head shows.

The body that makes it a good diver makes it a poor walker. Lobed toes set far back drive it well underwater and leave it clumsy ashore, so it stays afloat almost all the time. Chicks ride on a parent's back, and go under with it when it dives.

What it does here

Cover, nest and calm water

Emergent and floating marsh plants do three jobs at once for a grebe: they screen it from view, they supply the material for its floating nest, and they keep the water still enough for a bird that hunts by sight below the surface.

Take care

No meaningful danger to people.

Watch from a distance and do not feed it. If a grebe keeps diving away from you, you are simply too close.

Conservation

Widespread and common on suitable waters.
