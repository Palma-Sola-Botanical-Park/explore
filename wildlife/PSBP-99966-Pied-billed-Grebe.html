Pied-billed Grebe

Podilymbus podiceps
On this page

At a glance

  • A small, plump brown waterbird that dives and vanishes the moment you notice it is a pied-billed grebe.
  • It would rather sink than fly. By squeezing air from its feathers, it can slip below the surface like a submarine, leaving barely a ripple.
  • The short, thick bill gets a black ring around it in the breeding season — the "pied" (two-toned) look that gives the bird its name.
  • Its feet are built for diving, not walking: the toes have paddle-like lobes instead of webbing, set far back on a body that is clumsy on land.

How to know it

Size and shape

Small and stocky, with a short neck and almost no visible tail.

Color

Plain brown overall, paler on the throat.

Bill

Short, thick, and pale, with a black vertical ring in breeding season.

Behavior cue

Dives constantly and sits low in the water.

Voice

Surprisingly loud for its size in the breeding season: a far-carrying series of hollow "kuk-kuk-kuk-cow-cow-cow" calls from marshy cover.

Size

12 to 15 inches; about 18 to 24 inches

What to look for

A small brown waterbird riding low on a pond, repeatedly diving and popping back up somewhere unexpected. The stubby, ringed bill separates it from ducks and other divers.

Where to find it here

On the open water of the ponds, often near the weedy edges. Watch for a small brown bird that keeps diving and reappearing — and be patient, because it spends a lot of time underwater.

Most numerous in fall and winter, with some present year-round. Active throughout the day on the ponds.

How it lives

It dives rather than flies. A grebe feeds by going under, and escapes the same way — you rarely see one in the air or on land at all.

Crayfish, small fish, aquatic insects and other small water creatures, all caught below the surface. Usually alone or in small numbers, and quick to slip into weedy cover.

What it does here

A small diving predator that helps keep crayfish, insect, and small-fish numbers in check on quiet, vegetated waters.

Lives among emergent and floating marsh plants, which give it cover, nesting material, and the still water it prefers.

Native across the Americas. In Florida it is a common winter visitor and a year-round breeder on ponds, lakes, and marshes with plenty of vegetation.

The pied-billed grebe is a diver first and a flier last. Rather than burst into the air like a duck, it usually escapes trouble by submerging — and it can control how it sinks, trapping or releasing air in its plumage to ride high or settle low in the water, sometimes leaving only its head showing like a tiny periscope.

Those diving skills come from a body that is all wrong for land. Its lobed toes and far-back legs make it a powerful underwater swimmer but an awkward walker, so it spends nearly all its time on the water.

Grebe chicks are striped like little humbugs and often ride on a parent's back, even diving along while hitching a ride. Adults also do something odd and useful: they eat their own feathers, which seems to help protect the stomach and form pellets they later cough up.

Take care

What to do

Watch and enjoy, and please do not feed park wildlife. Grebes are shy — if one keeps diving away, you are simply a bit too close.
