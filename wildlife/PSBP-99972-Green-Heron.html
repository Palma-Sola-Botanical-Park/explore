Green Heron

Butorides virescens
On this page

At a glance

  • The green heron is a small, stocky, secretive heron — far smaller than the great blue — with a velvet-green back, rich chestnut neck, and a dark cap it can raise into a short crest.
  • It is one of the very few birds in the world known to use tools: it drops bait onto the water to lure fish within striking range.
  • Its 'bait' can be a twig, an insect, a feather, even a bit of bread, and it will reposition the lure if it drifts — a genuinely clever piece of hunting.
  • It hunts by stealth, crouched low and still on a branch or bank at the water's edge, often half-hidden in vegetation.
  • Watch for a bird that looks almost neckless — until it strikes: the neck shoots out like a coiled spring, far longer than it seemed, to snatch prey from the water.

How to know it

Size

Small and stocky — roughly a third the size of a great blue heron.

Color

Dark green-gray back, deep chestnut neck and breast, dark cap, and yellow to orange legs.

Posture

Often crouched low and horizontal at the water's edge.

Neck

Usually held tucked in, so the bird looks neckless and hunched — but it extends suddenly and surprisingly far when the heron strikes.

Voice

A sharp, explosive 'skeow' call, usually given as the bird flushes and flies off.

Size

16 to 18 inches; 25 to 27 inches

What to look for

A small, dark, hunched heron at the edge of a pond, often partly hidden in vegetation or on a low branch. If you see a heron seemingly fishing with a dropped twig or insect, it is almost certainly this species.

Where to find it here

At the quiet, vegetated edges of the ponds, crouched low on a bank or overhanging branch — easy to miss until it moves or flushes with a sharp call.

Year-round resident, but much more active and vocal in the spring and summer breeding season — quieter and more stealthy in winter. Often solitary and well hidden, so it rewards a patient, careful look at pond margins.

When

Quieter and more stealthy in winter.

How it lives

It hunts alone, by day, crouched and still on a bank or an overhanging branch, and it is easy to walk straight past. Small fish mostly, plus insects, frogs and other small aquatic animals.

It uses bait

A green heron will drop a feather, an insect or a scrap onto the water and wait for a fish to come up and look. Very few birds anywhere use a tool this way.

Unlike most of the herons here, it usually nests on its own rather than in a colony, in a tree or shrub near water.

What it does here

A small-fish predator of pond edges and one of the few tool-using birds — a reminder that resourcefulness shows up in unexpected, everyday animals.

Hunts from overhanging shrubs and low branches at the water's edge and nests in waterside trees — so dense pond-edge vegetation is part of how it lives and feeds.

Native to North and Central America. In Florida it is a common, year-round resident of small, vegetated wetlands, pond edges, and quiet, shaded water.

Where the great blue heron is tall and conspicuous, the green heron is small and easy to miss, often tucked into the vegetation at the edge of a pond, body stretched low over the water as it waits.

What sets it apart is its use of tools. The green heron will drop a small object — a twig, an insect, a feather, sometimes a crust of bread — onto the water's surface to draw curious fish close, then strike. It will even pick the lure back up and move it if it drifts out of position. Tool use is rare among birds, shared with only a handful of species, which makes this common little heron quietly remarkable.

Unlike many herons it usually nests alone rather than in big colonies, building a stick platform in a tree or shrub near water.

Take care

What to do

Watch quietly from a distance and do not feed it. This shy heron flushes easily, and crowding it ends the hunt.
