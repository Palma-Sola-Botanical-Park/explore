At a glance

  • A small brown lizard doing push-ups on a fence or low tree trunk is often a Brown Anole.
  • It is native to the Caribbean, not Florida, and is now widespread across the state.
  • Where Brown Anoles become common, native Green Anoles often shift upward into shrubs and trees.
  • Experiments have shown that Green Anole populations pushed onto higher, narrower perches can evolve larger toepads within only a few generations.

How to know it

Color

Brown, tan, or gray, usually patterned with triangles, diamonds, or a pale stripe down the back. The one thing it will never do is turn bright green.

Throat fan

Males flash a reddish-orange dewlap edged with yellow. Male Green Anoles have a pink to reddish dewlap, so the throat fan can be a useful clue — but use it alongside body shape and perch height rather than on its own.

Body

Stocky, short-snouted, with ridged scales that give the back a slightly rough look.

Voice

Brown Anoles are essentially silent to human observers. Most communication is visual: head bobs, push-ups and dewlap displays.

Where it sits

Brown Anoles commonly perch low on fences, walls, trunks and the ground. Where both species occur, Green Anoles often use higher vegetation.

Easily confused with

Green Anole — Green Anoles are slimmer, can change from green to brown, and generally use higher vegetation where both species occur. Brown Anoles remain brown or gray, are stockier, and often show darker back patterns. Adult males of both species have colorful dewlaps, so use several clues rather than throat color alone.

Where to find it here

Look anywhere the sun reaches low surfaces: fence rails, tree trunks, sidewalk edges and planting beds. Warm, sunny areas are especially productive.

They are present year-round, but cold weather can keep them hidden. Warm, sunny periods bring them out to bask and display.

In this park

The Brown Anole is among the most frequently recorded animals in Palma Sola's wildlife observations, with records going back to 2018. If you are looking for a lizard, this is the one you are most likely to find.

How it lives

Active by day and usually close to the ground. Males defend small territories and signal with head bobs, push-ups and flashes of the dewlap. These displays communicate with rival males and potential mates.

They eat insects, spiders and other small animals they can overpower. If seized by a predator, an anole can shed part of its tail; a replacement grows gradually afterward.

What it does here

Brown Anoles were introduced to Florida from the Caribbean and are now widespread through much of the state.

Brown and Green Anoles overlap in diet and perch use. Where Brown Anoles become abundant, Green Anoles often shift upward into shrubs and trees. Brown Anoles may also prey on young Green Anoles.

Evolution you can watch

In experimental island populations where Brown Anoles were introduced, Green Anoles quickly shifted to higher, narrower perches. Within only a few generations, those populations also evolved larger toepads with more adhesive structures, improving their grip on those perches.

Take care

Watch without feeding or handling. Brown Anoles are already abundant at the park and do not need supplemental food.
