At a glance

  • The Virginia Opossum is the only marsupial native north of Mexico. When badly frightened, it may collapse into an involuntary, death-like state lasting anywhere from minutes to several hours. It is not deciding to put on an act.
  • Pregnancy lasts only about thirteen days. The newborns are roughly honeybee-sized and must crawl into the pouch and claim one of thirteen teats to survive.
  • Opossums have fifty teeth — more than any other land mammal in North America.
  • Wild opossums usually live only one or two years, remarkably short for a mammal their size.

How to know it

Size and build

About house-cat length but usually much lighter — Florida adults average only around four pounds. The build is stout and low, with short legs and a lumbering gait.

Markings

The body is a grizzled gray-brown: long white guard hairs over dark-tipped underfur give it an unkempt look. The face is strikingly white with a pale pink nose, small dark eyes, and large, naked black ears.

Hands and feet

Five long pink fingers on each front foot, and on the hind feet a clawless thumb that works the way ours does. Watch one balanced on a rim or a branch and the hands do as much of the holding on as the tail.

The tail

No other Florida mammal has a tail quite like this one — long, hairless, pink and scaly, and prehensile enough to grip a branch. It is the feature that ends most ID questions quickly.

The walk

On the ground, the opossum moves with a slow, deliberate, rolling shuffle. It is one of the few mammals here that simply does not seem built for speed.

If it appears dead

A frightened opossum may lie on its side, go limp, stare glassily and barely respond. That can be its involuntary playing-dead response rather than illness or injury. Give it space and time rather than testing it by touching it.

Where to find it here

Opossums move through almost any habitat the park offers — wooded edges, dense plantings, brushy corners, and anywhere food can be had. They shelter by day in hollow logs, dense vegetation, brush piles, or any dark enclosed space, emerging after dark to forage.

When

Mostly nocturnal. They usually emerge around dusk and do most of their wandering during the first half of the night. Not strictly, though: Helen Lewis's photographs on this page were taken bright and early, before any foot traffic had started at the pond.

Our trash enclosures are one reliable place to find signs of a visit. If you see one frozen near the bins or tucked into a corner looking thoroughly dead, give it some space before drawing conclusions.

How it lives

Opossums are solitary wanderers rather than territorial homeowners. Their ranges overlap freely, and they often shift den sites as they move through whatever habitat offers the best foraging — frequently not using the same shelter on consecutive nights.

They are opportunistic omnivores in the fullest sense: fruit, insects, earthworms, snails, small vertebrates, bird eggs, carrion, and the contents of a garbage can all qualify as dinner. The approach is to sample everything and specialize in nothing, which has served them well across a remarkable range of habitats.

When threatened, the opossum's first response is usually to hiss, bare its fifty teeth, and try to escape. If that fails and the stress becomes acute, it collapses into thanatosis — a genuine involuntary physiological response, not a calculated bluff. The animal goes limp, breathes shallowly, and may emit a foul-smelling fluid. It cannot simply decide to stop.

Females usually raise one or two litters a year. The young are born in under two weeks and finish developing in the pouch. After about two months they leave the pouch and ride on the mother's back before dispersing — and rarely travel more than a mile from where they were born.

What it does here

Opossums will eat almost anything they can find: fallen fruit, insects, worms, eggs, small animals, carrion and human food scraps. In a botanical park, fruiting trees and shrubs give them plenty of reasons to wander through after dark.

We thought one was dying. It wasn't.

High school volunteers and staff once found an opossum trapped inside a trash bin, unable to climb back out. It was limp and lethargic enough that everyone thought it was seriously sick. They tipped the bin out in a quiet corner of the park, trash and all, and left the animal alone. Two hours later the opossum was gone. The trash got cleaned up — and everyone eventually realized they had probably just been completely fooled by an opossum doing what opossums do.

They are surprisingly resistant to the venom of pit vipers such as rattlesnakes and cottonmouths — species they share the Florida landscape with. Resistant, not immune, but enough to make them among the more capable snake-hunting mammals around.

Predators here include owls, coyotes, and large snakes — the same nocturnal cast this animal shares the dark with.

Take care

A cornered opossum may hiss, bare its teeth, or bite. If one appears dead, do not poke, pick up, or otherwise test it. Give it space and let the animal recover and leave on its own. If it is clearly injured or remains in a dangerous spot, contact a wildlife rehabilitator.
