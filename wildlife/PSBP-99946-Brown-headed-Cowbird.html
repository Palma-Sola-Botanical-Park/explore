Brown-headed Cowbird

Molothrus ater
On this page
At a glanceHow to know itWhere to find it hereHow it livesWhat it does here

At a glance

  • A stocky blackbird with a glossy brown head (male) and a drab gray-brown body (female) — smaller and chunkier than a grackle.
  • It is North America's most notorious brood parasite: it lays its eggs in the nests of other birds and leaves them to do all the work.
  • Over 200 species have been documented raising cowbird young, often at the expense of their own chicks.
  • The strategy evolved on the Great Plains, where cowbirds followed bison herds and could not stay in one place long enough to raise their own young.

How to know it

Male

Glossy black body with a rich chocolate-brown head.

Female

Plain gray-brown all over — easy to overlook.

Shape

Stocky, short-tailed, with a thick conical bill.

Behavior

Often on the ground near livestock or in mixed blackbird flocks.

Voice

Male gives a liquid, gurgling 'glug-glug-gleee.' Female makes a dry chatter.

Size

7 to 8 inches; about 12 inches

What to look for

A stocky blackbird with a brown head (male) or a plain gray-brown bird (female), often feeding on the ground in open areas.

Where to find it here

Open lawns and ground near the park edges; sometimes in mixed flocks with grackles.

Year-round in Florida, though numbers may fluctuate seasonally.

How it lives

Feeds on the ground. Males display with a gurgling song and a bow. Females scout nests of other species.

Seeds, grain, and insects — forages on the ground, often near livestock.

What it does here

Brood parasite — a significant pressure on small songbird nesting success. Also an insect and seed consumer.

Forages on seeds from grasses and weeds.

Native to North America. Year-round resident in much of the southeastern U.S. including Florida.

The brown-headed cowbird is native, but it is one of the most controversial native birds in conservation. By parasitizing the nests of smaller songbirds, it can depress breeding success for species already under pressure.

The female is a stealth operator — she watches other birds build nests, slips in to lay an egg when the owner is away, and moves on. The cowbird chick often hatches first and grows faster, outcompeting the host's own young.

Despite its reputation, the cowbird is doing what it evolved to do. The real problem is that habitat fragmentation puts more songbird nests within reach of cowbirds than ever before.
