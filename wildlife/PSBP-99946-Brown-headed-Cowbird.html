Brown-headed Cowbird

Molothrus ater
On this page
At a glanceHow to know itWhere to find it hereHow it livesWhat it does here

At a glance

  • A stocky blackbird with a glossy brown head (male) and a drab gray-brown body (female) — smaller and chunkier than a grackle.
  • It is North America's most notorious brood parasite: it lays its eggs in the nests of other birds and leaves them to do all the work.
  • Over 200 species have been documented raising cowbird young, often at the expense of their own chicks.
  • The strategy evolved on the Great Plains, where cowbirds followed bison herds and could not stay in one place long enough to raise their own young.

How to know it

Male

Glossy black body with a rich chocolate-brown head.

Female

Plain gray-brown all over — easy to overlook.

Shape

Stocky, short-tailed, with a thick conical bill.

Behavior

Often on the ground near livestock or in mixed blackbird flocks.

Voice

Male gives a liquid, gurgling 'glug-glug-gleee.' Female makes a dry chatter.

Size

About seven to eight inches long, with a wingspan around a foot.

What to look for

A stocky blackbird with a brown head (male) or a plain gray-brown bird (female), often feeding on the ground in open areas.

Easily confused with

Common Grackle — Grackles are larger and longer-tailed with pale eyes. Cowbirds are stocky and short-tailed, and the male's chocolate-brown head is unlike anything on a grackle.

Where to find it here

Look on open lawns and short grass, often walking rather than hopping, and often mixed in with grackles and other blackbirds.

Present in Florida year-round, though numbers rise and fall through the seasons.

How it lives

It feeds on the ground, often around livestock, taking seeds, grain and insects. Males display with a bow and a strange liquid gurgle of a song.

What the females are doing

Watching other birds. A cowbird builds no nest of her own — she scouts the nests of other species and lays in them.

What it does here

A seed and insect eater, and a brood parasite whose eggs are raised by other species. Where host songbirds are already under pressure, that parasitism can add to it.

Cowbirds feed mainly on the seeds of grasses and weeds, so open, seeding ground is their habitat rather than dense planting. The birds they parasitize are mostly shrub- and tree-nesting songbirds, which means the edge between open ground and woody cover is where the two meet.

Native to North America. Year-round resident in much of the southeastern U.S. including Florida.

Some host species have learned to answer. Yellow Warblers will build a new nest floor over a cowbird egg and start again, occasionally producing a stack of several storeys.
