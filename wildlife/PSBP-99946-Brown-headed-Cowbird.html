Brown-headed Cowbird

Molothrus ater
On this page
At a glanceHow to know itWhere to find it hereHow it livesWhat it does here

At a glance

  • A stocky blackbird — the male glossy black with a chocolate-brown head, the female plain gray-brown.
  • It builds no nest. The female lays her eggs in other birds' nests and leaves them to raise her young.
  • More than 200 bird species have been documented receiving cowbird eggs.

How to know it

Male

Glossy black with a rich chocolate-brown head.

Female

Plain gray-brown all over, and easy to overlook.

Shape

Stocky and short-tailed, with a thick conical bill.

Voice

The male gives a liquid, gurgling glug-glug-gleee. The female answers with a dry chatter.

Usually on the ground in the open, walking rather than hopping, and often mixed in with grackles.

Easily confused with

Common Grackle — Grackles are larger and longer-tailed with pale eyes. Cowbirds are stocky and short-tailed, and the male's chocolate-brown head is unlike anything on a grackle.

Where to find it here

Open lawns and short grass, often among other blackbirds.

Present year-round, though numbers rise and fall through the seasons.

How it lives

It feeds on the ground, often around livestock, taking seeds, grain and insects. Males display with a bow and a strange liquid gurgle of a song.

What the females are doing

Watching other birds. A cowbird builds no nest of her own — she finds the nest of another species, lays an egg in it and leaves. The host incubates it and raises the chick alongside its own, or in place of them.

What it does here

Cowbirds historically followed roaming bison herds across open country. Laying in another bird's nest allowed them to reproduce without staying in one place while the herds moved on.

Some hosts have an answer. Yellow Warblers will build a new nest floor straight over a cowbird egg and start again, occasionally ending up with a nest several storeys tall.

Here they feed mostly on grass and weed seeds out in the open, while the songbirds they parasitize nest in shrubs and trees. The edge between open ground and woody cover is where the two meet.
