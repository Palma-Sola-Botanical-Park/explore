Great Blue Heron

Ardea herodias
On this page

At a glance

  • Standing close to four feet tall with a wingspan that can stretch past six feet, the great blue heron is the largest heron in North America — and one of the most recognizable birds in Florida.
  • Its main tool is patience: it stands motionless in the shallows, sometimes for many minutes, then snaps its coiled neck forward faster than the eye can follow.
  • It hunts fish in the shallows but is just as happy stalking open fields and grassy banks for frogs, snakes, and rodents, so you may spot one far from any water.
  • Travel south toward the Florida Keys and you may meet an all-white version of this same bird — long thought to be its own species, now treated simply as a different color form.

How to know it

Easily confused with

Great Egret — The great egret is all white, slimmer, and a bit smaller, with a yellow bill and black legs; the great blue is gray-blue and larger.

Size

Very large — around four feet tall with a roughly six-foot wingspan.

Color

Blue-gray body, a mostly white face with a black plume stripe over the eye, and a heavy yellowish bill.

In flight

Neck folded into an S, long legs trailing behind, slow deep wingbeats.

White morph (south Florida)

Entirely white, but with the same size, shape, and heavy bill.

Voice

Mostly silent, but when alarmed or flushed it lets out a deep, harsh, far-carrying squawk.

Size

about 4 feet tall (up to ~4.5 feet); wingspan 5.5 to 6.6 feet; weight roughly 5 lb

What to look for

A tall blue-gray bird standing motionless or wading slowly in the shallows. In flight, the folded neck is the giveaway that separates herons from cranes and storks, which fly with necks outstretched.

Where to find it here

Along the pond banks and shallows, standing still or wading slowly. Sometimes seen well away from water, stalking the lawns for prey.

Year-round resident. Visible throughout the day, usually hunting alone.

How it lives

It hunts alone and defends a feeding territory, though it nests in colonies. Mostly by day, but it will hunt at dusk and after dark too.

Two hunting styles

It shifts between a slow stalk and standing statue-still, reading the situation to decide which will work.

Mainly fish, but a true generalist — frogs, snakes, turtles, large insects, and small mammals like rats and gophers, taken on land as readily as in water.

What it does here

A top predator of shallow waters and wetland edges, helping keep fish, amphibian, and rodent numbers in balance across a wide range of habitats.

Native across most of North and Central America. In Florida it is a common, year-round resident found at virtually any body of water, from wild marshes to suburban retention ponds.

For all its size, the great blue heron is built around patience. It will stand statue-still in shallow water, sometimes for many minutes, watching, before its coiled neck snaps forward to catch a fish.

It is also more versatile than most herons. Away from the water it will stalk fields and lawns for rodents, snakes, and large insects, which is why you might see one far from any pond.

South Florida is home to the striking great white heron, an all-white form once classified as its own species. Today scientists treat it as a white morph of the great blue heron — same bird, different wardrobe — most common in the Keys and Florida Bay.

Take care

What to do

Keep your distance and do not feed it. A hunting heron needs quiet and space; getting too close sends it flying off hungry.
