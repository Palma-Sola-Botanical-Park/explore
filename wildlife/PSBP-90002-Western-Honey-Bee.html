Western Honey Bee

Apis mellifera

At a glance

  • A forager that finds a good patch of flowers can tell other workers where it is. Inside the hive, she performs a waggle dance whose direction points toward the food relative to the sun, while the length of the waggle run conveys distance.
  • A worker's jobs change as she ages. Young workers clean cells, feed larvae and build comb inside the colony. Older workers become guards and foragers, spending the last part of their lives outside collecting nectar and pollen.
  • Honey bees are not native to the Americas. European colonists brought them to North America in the 1600s, and managed and free-living colonies are now widespread.
  • A colony reproduces by swarming. The old queen leaves with a large part of the workers, and they cluster nearby — often as a hanging mass on a branch — while scouts search for a cavity to move into.

How to know it

Easily confused with

Southern Paper Wasp — The paper wasp has a narrow waist, a nearly hairless body, and folds its wings lengthwise at rest. A honey bee is furry, thick-waisted, and often carrying pollen on its hind legs.

Pollen baskets

Look at the hind legs of a foraging worker. Honey bees pack collected pollen into flattened baskets there, sometimes forming conspicuous yellow, orange or cream-colored loads.

Size

Workers are about half an inch long — roughly the size of a watermelon seed. The bee on a flower will almost always be a worker.

Color

Usually brown to orange-brown, with dark and paler bands across the abdomen and dense hairs on the thorax. Color varies considerably among honey bees.

Similar insects

Bumble bees are generally larger and much furrier. Paper wasps have a narrow waist, less body hair and longer-looking legs. Pollen packed onto the hind legs is a strong clue that you are looking at a bee.

Size

Workers are about half an inch long. Queens are somewhat longer; males are stockier and have much larger eyes.

What to look for

Start with the hind legs. A worker carrying a packed yellow or orange pollen load is easy to recognize.

Where to find it here

Look wherever many flowers are open at once. Flowering shrubs, trees and garden beds can draw workers throughout the day; pollen packed onto the hind legs is often visible without getting very close.

Honey bees forage year-round in southwest Florida whenever flowers and weather permit. Warm, dry days with many plants in bloom offer the best chance of seeing them.

When

Foraging follows flowering and temperature rather than the calendar. Activity slows on the coldest days but does not stop.

How it lives

Workers communicate largely through scent. A forager can also perform dances on the comb that direct nestmates toward profitable food.

Nectar supplies carbohydrates and is stored as honey. Pollen supplies protein and other nutrients, especially for developing young. Workers also bring water back to the colony.

What it does here

Native to Europe, the Middle East and Africa, where many subspecies evolved across a wide range of climates. No subspecies is native to the Americas. Colonists brought the bee to North America beginning in the early 1600s, and managed and free-living colonies are now established across the continent.

Honey bees pollinate many crops and garden plants. In North America they are introduced pollinators, and dense managed or free-living colonies can compete with native bees for flowers. The size of that effect varies among habitats and bee communities.

Flowers supply nectar and pollen, but honey bees collect other plant materials too. Workers gather sticky resins from buds and bark and turn them into propolis, which helps seal and line the nest. Different plants become important as flowering changes through the year.

Western Honey Bees are introduced in Florida, not native. They are not generally managed as an invasive pest, but high densities of honey bees can compete with native bees for floral resources and may contribute to pathogen transmission.

Queens make sounds. A newly emerged queen produces a high-pitched tooting, answered by muffled quacking from queens still sealed in their cells — part of how a colony settles which queen will lead it after a swarm.

A colony behaves like a larger organism. Tens of thousands of workers regulate temperature, rear young, store food, defend the nest and exchange information without a central individual directing every action.

Take care

The risk

A honey-bee worker can sting if trapped or if its colony is threatened. A sting usually causes temporary pain and swelling, but severe allergic reactions can occur. If a stinger remains in the skin, remove it promptly. Trouble breathing, swelling of the face or throat, faintness, or other signs of a severe allergic reaction require emergency medical care.

What to do

Foraging bees on flowers are usually focused on collecting food. Watch without swatting or handling them. Do not approach or disturb a colony or large cluster of bees; alert park staff instead.
