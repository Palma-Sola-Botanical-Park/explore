Western Honey Bee

Apis mellifera

At a glance

  • A single colony can hold 50,000 to 80,000 workers, all daughters of one queen. That queen may lay up to 2,000 eggs a day at peak season — more than her own body weight in eggs over her lifetime.
  • The waggle dance is one of the animal kingdom's most remarkable communication systems. A returning forager traces a figure-eight on the comb, waggling her abdomen on the straight run to encode both the direction and distance of a food source relative to the position of the sun.
  • Worker bees are all sterile females. A newly emerged worker spends her first few weeks cleaning cells, nursing larvae, and building comb — foraging comes only in the last weeks of her short life, when the flight muscles that power it will literally wear out.
  • The western honey bee is not native to the Americas. Every honey bee you see in Florida traces its ancestry to European (and in some areas Africanized) stock brought here by beekeepers beginning in the early 1600s.

How to know it

Size and shape

Workers are about 0.4 to 0.6 inches long — roughly the size of a watermelon seed. Queens are noticeably longer, 0.7 to 0.8 inches, with an elongated abdomen that extends well past the wingtips at rest. Drones (males) are stockier than workers, with dramatically large compound eyes that meet at the top of the head.

Coloration

Generally brown to orange-brown with alternating dark and pale (yellowish to amber) bands across the abdomen. The thorax is covered in dense tan or golden-brown hairs. Overall color varies somewhat by subspecies — Italian bees tend toward brighter amber, while Carniolan bees are darker and grayer — but the banded abdomen is consistent.

Pollen baskets

Foraging workers often carry conspicuous balls of compacted pollen on the corbiculae (pollen baskets) on their hind legs. These can be yellow, orange, white, or even greenish depending on the plant visited, and are one of the easiest field marks for a forager in flight.

Similar species

Bumble bees are larger and rounder, with a fuzzy appearance and often a yellow-and-black pattern. Sweat bees and other small native bees are considerably smaller. Yellowjackets and paper wasps are similar in size but have a narrow waist, much less body hair, and hold their wings flat or folded lengthwise at rest.

Voice

The familiar buzz is produced by the rapid vibration of the flight muscles, not the wings directly — honey bees can buzz with wings folded. Individual foragers at flowers produce a soft, steady hum. A disturbed or defensive colony is considerably louder, with a higher-pitched, urgent tone that experienced beekeepers recognize immediately. Inside the hive, queens produce distinct piping calls — high-pitched tooting from a newly emerged queen and muffled quacking from queens still in their cells — used in the competitive process of establishing a new queen after swarming.

Size

Workers 0.4–0.6 inches; queens 0.7–0.8 inches; drones slightly stockier than workers

What to look for

Look for a medium-sized, hairy, banded brown-and-amber bee working flowers methodically, often pausing long enough at each bloom to pack pollen onto its hind legs. Honey bees tend to be unhurried and focused compared to the darting flight of wasps. The pollen-laden hind legs are a reliable field mark on foragers. At rest on a flower, the broad hairy thorax and the distinctly banded abdomen separate this species from wasps and most other bees in the park.

Where to find it here

Any flowering plant in the park is a reasonable starting point, particularly those with open, accessible blooms. Watch for workers moving methodically from flower to flower, often with visible pollen loads on their hind legs. Bees are especially concentrated on mass-flowering shrubs and herbs. Listen as well — the steady hum of foraging workers is often the first clue before you spot them.

Year-round in Manatee County. Because Florida winters are mild and some plants flower in every month, foraging workers are active on warm days throughout the year. Peak foraging activity tracks peak bloom: late winter through spring and again in fall. Workers forage during daylight hours, typically ramping up mid-morning after temperatures rise and tapering off in late afternoon.

When

Active as foragers in every month in Manatee County given Florida's mild winters. Foraging activity tracks flowering plant availability and air temperature; workers are most conspicuous during warm, sunny mornings. Activity may slow on the coldest winter days but does not cease.

How it lives

Colonies are eusocial and highly organized, with labor divided among the queen (egg-laying), workers (foraging, nursing, building, and defense), and drones (mating). Workers shift roles as they age: early weeks are spent on in-hive duties including cell cleaning, larval feeding, wax production, and guard duty; foraging begins only in the final weeks of a worker's life. Colonies reproduce by swarming — the old queen leaves with roughly half the workers to found a new colony while daughter queens compete for the original hive. Pheromones govern colony cohesion; the queen's mandibular pheromone alone suppresses worker reproduction and signals her presence throughout the nest.

Workers collect nectar and pollen from flowering plants, converting nectar into honey for long-term colony energy storage and using pollen as the colony's protein source for raising larvae. Individual foragers may visit hundreds of flowers on a single trip. Colonies also gather propolis — a sticky resin from plant buds — used to seal gaps and varnish the hive interior, and collect water for cooling and diluting stored honey.

What it does here

The western honey bee is native to Europe, the Middle East, and Africa, where more than 20 recognized subspecies evolved across a range of climates and habitats. No subspecies is native to the Americas. European colonists first imported the bee to North America around 1622, and subsequent waves of importation introduced additional European races throughout the 1800s. Feral populations now exist across the continental United States, and Florida's warm climate allows colonies to remain active and productive year-round — a feature that makes the state a major hub for commercial beekeeping. Africanized honey bees, hybrids of African and European races, have also spread into southern Florida.

As generalist foragers visiting a very wide range of flowering plants, honey bees are significant pollinators of both wild plants and agricultural crops. In Florida gardens and natural areas, they contribute to the reproduction of many native flowering plants. At the same time, researchers note that high densities of honey bees can reduce resources available to native solitary bees, which are often more efficient pollinators of specific plant species. Within the local food web, honey bee colonies are targeted by a range of predators and parasites — in Florida these include skunks, opossums, raccoons, and the small hive beetle (Aethina tumida), a Florida-relevant pest that can devastate weak colonies.

The western honey bee is introduced throughout the Americas and is not native to Florida. Apis mellifera is not listed as an invasive species by the Florida Invasive Species Council (FISC); only the Africanized hybrid (Apis mellifera scutellata) appears on Florida invasive species lists. Even so, ecologists note that high densities of managed and feral honey bee colonies can compete with Florida's native solitary bees for floral resources and nesting cavities, and the majority of research concludes that managed honey bees negatively affect wild native bees through competition and pathogen transmission. The species is so deeply embedded in agriculture and the broader landscape that it is not a target for control, but its ecological impact on native pollinators is an active area of research.

Few animals organize collective life as elaborately as the honey bee. A mature colony is essentially a superorganism: tens of thousands of workers share a single reproductive queen, divide labor by age, regulate hive temperature to within a degree or two of 95°F, and collectively decide when and where to swarm.

No individual bee directs any of this — it emerges from chemical signals, dances, and physical contact among colony members.

The waggle dance is the centerpiece of that communication. When a forager finds a rich patch of flowers, she returns to the dark interior of the hive and performs a figure-eight run on the vertical comb surface. The angle of the straight 'waggle run' relative to vertical mirrors the angle of the food source relative to the sun; the duration of the waggle run encodes distance.

Bees following the dancer decode both signals and fly directly to a patch they have never visited.

Florida's climate gives honey bees almost no true dormant period. Unlike colonies in northern states that cluster through winter and stop laying, Florida queens often lay eggs in every month of the year, and workers forage whenever temperatures cooperate.

That productivity is a boon to beekeepers — and it means you are likely to encounter foragers at flowers in this garden on any warm day of the year.

Take care

The risk

Workers will sting when they perceive the colony as threatened, or occasionally when an individual bee is inadvertently squeezed or stepped on. The sting is painful and the barbed stinger lodges in skin, continuing to pump venom — remove it quickly by scraping rather than pinching. For most people a sting causes temporary local pain and swelling. A small percentage of people have severe allergic reactions (anaphylaxis), which requires immediate medical attention. Solitary foragers away from the nest are generally non-aggressive and rarely sting unprovoked.

What to do

Foragers visiting flowers in the park are focused on their work and pose little risk if left alone. Observe them closely — they are remarkably tolerant of a slow, quiet approach. Avoid swatting, which triggers a defensive response. Give any swarm or established hive a wide berth and alert park staff. Never disturb a known nest.
