Western Honey Bee

Apis mellifera

At a glance

  • Honey bees are not native to the Americas. European honey bees were brought to North America by colonists beginning in the 1600s and eventually became established across the continent.
  • A worker's jobs change as she ages. Young workers clean cells, feed larvae and build comb inside the colony. Older workers become guards and foragers, spending the last part of their lives outside collecting nectar and pollen.
  • A forager that finds a good patch of flowers can tell other workers where it is. Inside the hive, she performs a waggle dance whose direction points toward the food relative to the sun, while the length of the waggle run conveys distance.
  • A colony reproduces by swarming. The old queen leaves with a large part of the workers, and they cluster nearby — often as a hanging mass on a branch — while scouts search for a cavity to move into.

How to know it

Easily confused with

Southern Paper Wasp — The paper wasp has a narrow waist, a nearly hairless body, and folds its wings lengthwise at rest. A honey bee is furry, thick-waisted, and often carrying pollen on its hind legs.

Pollen baskets

Look at the hind legs of a foraging worker. Honey bees pack collected pollen into flattened baskets there, sometimes forming conspicuous yellow, orange or cream-colored loads.

Size

Workers are about half an inch long — roughly the size of a watermelon seed. The bee on a flower will almost always be a worker. Queens are longer and stay inside the nest; males are stockier, with very large eyes.

Color

Brown to orange-brown, with alternating dark and pale bands across the abdomen and dense tan hairs on the thorax. The shade varies between strains, but the banding is consistent.

Telling it from a wasp

Bumble bees are larger, rounder and furrier. Yellowjackets and paper wasps are about the same size but have a narrow waist, much less body hair, and hold their wings folded lengthwise at rest.

Voice

The familiar buzz comes from rapid vibration of the flight muscles. A bee can vibrate those muscles even without flying, though the ordinary hum around flowers usually comes from active flight.

Size

Workers 0.4–0.6 inches; queens 0.7–0.8 inches; drones slightly stockier than workers

What to look for

Start with the hind legs — a worker carrying pollen is unmistakable. Honey bees also work flowers methodically, rather than darting the way wasps do.

Where to find it here

Look wherever many flowers are open at once. A shrub or bed in heavy bloom can attract many workers, and pollen loads on the hind legs are often visible at close range.

Honey bees can forage throughout the year in southwest Florida whenever weather and flowers permit. Workers are most active on warm, sunny mornings.

When

Foraging follows flowering and temperature rather than the calendar. Activity slows on the coldest days but does not stop.

How it lives

A worker's jobs change as she ages. Her first weeks are spent inside — cleaning cells, feeding larvae, building comb — and only in her last weeks does she become a guard and then a forager, working flowers outside the nest.

Workers coordinate through scent and through dances performed on the comb. Colonies reproduce by swarming: the old queen leaves with a large part of the workers, and the cluster waits nearby while scouts look for a cavity to move into.

Nectar, stored as honey, is the colony's energy supply. Pollen provides protein, mostly for developing larvae. Workers also collect resin from buds and bark to make propolis, and water to cool the nest and thin stored honey.

What it does here

Native to Europe, the Middle East and Africa, where many subspecies evolved across a wide range of climates. No subspecies is native to the Americas. Colonists brought the bee to North America beginning in the early 1600s, and managed and free-living colonies are now established across the continent.

Honey bees are major pollinators of crops and many garden plants. Because they were introduced to North America and can occur at very high densities, they can also compete with native bees for nectar and pollen. The effect varies by habitat and bee community.

Honey bees depend on flowering plants for nectar and pollen, but their plant collecting does not stop there. Workers also gather sticky resins from buds and bark and turn them into propolis, which they use to seal small gaps and coat surfaces inside the nest. Because honey bees visit many plant species rather than specializing on one, the flowers attracting them can change throughout the year.

The western honey bee is introduced throughout the Americas and is not native to Florida. Apis mellifera is not listed as an invasive species by the Florida Invasive Species Council (FISC); only the Africanized hybrid (Apis mellifera scutellata) appears on Florida invasive species lists. Even so, ecologists note that high densities of managed and feral honey bee colonies can compete with Florida's native solitary bees for floral resources and nesting cavities, and the majority of research concludes that managed honey bees negatively affect wild native bees through competition and pathogen transmission. The species is so deeply embedded in agriculture and the broader landscape that it is not a target for control, but its ecological impact on native pollinators is an active area of research.

A colony behaves like a larger organism. Tens of thousands of workers regulate temperature, rear young, store food, defend the nest and exchange information without a central individual directing every action.

The waggle dance works in darkness. The dancer's angle relative to vertical corresponds to the direction of food relative to the sun, and the duration of the run conveys roughly how far away it is.

Queens make sounds too. A newly emerged queen produces a high-pitched tooting, answered by muffled quacking from queens still sealed in their cells — part of how a colony settles which queen will lead it after a swarm.

Take care

The risk

A honey-bee worker can sting if trapped or if its colony is threatened. A sting usually causes temporary pain and swelling, but severe allergic reactions can occur. If a stinger remains in the skin, remove it promptly. Trouble breathing, swelling of the face or throat, faintness, or other signs of a severe allergic reaction require emergency medical care.

What to do

Foraging bees on flowers are usually focused on collecting food. Watch without swatting or handling them. Do not approach or disturb a colony or large cluster of bees; alert park staff instead.
