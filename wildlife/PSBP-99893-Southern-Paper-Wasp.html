Southern Paper Wasp

Polistes bellicosus

At a glance

  • The nest is literally made of paper — workers scrape weathered wood fibers from fence posts and dead stems, chew them into pulp, and press them into the thin hexagonal cells where eggs are laid. No glue, no staples; just wasp saliva and patience.
  • A colony lives for roughly eight months, then falls apart. Only mated females survive winter — tucked under bark or in sheltered spots — and each one that makes it to spring has a shot at founding a brand-new nest.
  • Paper wasps can sting repeatedly. Unlike honeybees, they do not leave the stinger behind, so a single wasp disturbed at her nest can deliver more than one sting before retreating.
  • Colonies sometimes relocate mid-season. When predators destroy a nest, the foundress may simply rebuild up to roughly 35 feet away and start over — researchers have documented satellite nests accounting for more than a third of all nests built between May and July.

How to know it

Size and shape

A slender, long-legged wasp roughly 5/8 to 3/4 inches long. The narrow 'waist' — the petiole connecting thorax to abdomen — is the most consistent shape feature separating paper wasps from bees at a glance. Long legs dangle visibly in flight.

Coloration

Both sexes have lemon-yellow abdominal segments. Females have a single fine black longitudinal line running down the center of the mesothorax (the middle body segment); males have two lines along the sides of the mesothorax. Both sexes have yellow mandibles. Overall the insect looks yellow and black, slender, and wasp-waisted.

Sex differences

Females and males are morphologically very similar and nearly the same size. The difference in the number and placement of the black lines on the mesothorax — one central line in females, two lateral lines in males — is the most reliable field mark, though it requires a close view.

The nest

Open-celled, umbrella- or fan-shaped, and gray-brown. Cells face downward from a single central stalk. The whole structure looks like a small piece of coarse gray cardboard with holes in it. Nests are typically small to medium — a few dozen to perhaps a hundred cells at peak season — and are built in sheltered spots: under eaves, on shrub stems, beneath fence rails.

Similar species

Several other Polistes species share the park. The red wasp (P. carolina) is larger and heavily reddish-brown. P. exclamans is reddish-brown with pale yellow banding and much more common in suburban settings. Yellowjackets are similar in size but hold their wings flat against the body at rest and have a rounder, less elongated abdomen. Honeybees are hairy and never have the narrow wasp waist.

Voice

Essentially silent to human ears except for the soft buzz of wingbeats in flight. There are no songs, calls, or stridulations. The only reliable auditory cue near a nest is the low, dry hum of multiple wasps flying or fanning their wings — easy to miss until you are quite close.

Size

Approximately about 5/8 to 3/4 inches

What to look for

Look for a slender, long-legged, yellow-and-black wasp with a visibly pinched waist. The lemon-yellow abdomen and yellow mandibles are the key color marks; the narrow petiole clinches the paper-wasp ID. In flight, watch for the dangling hind legs — a reliable paper-wasp trait. The open, papery, downward-facing comb nest, usually tucked under an overhang or attached to a shrub stem, is often easier to spot first.

Where to find it here

Check the undersides of eaves on any park structure, beneath the edges of signage and picnic shelter overhangs, and on the stems of shrubby plants in sunny, open areas. Foragers are most visible working low-growing flowers for nectar or patrolling vegetation for caterpillars. Scan slowly along shrub stems — nests are small and easily overlooked until you are within a few feet of them.

Active by day from roughly late February or March through November in Manatee County, with colony activity peaking in mid- to late summer when worker numbers are highest. Foragers are out during warm daylight hours; activity drops on cool or overcast days. Adults that will overwinter as future foundresses can be found resting in sheltered spots on mild winter days.

When

Colony cycle runs approximately March through October or November in Manatee County. Mated queens overwintering in bark or other shelter may be encountered on warm winter days, but active foraging colonies are a warm-season phenomenon. Florida's mild winters mean the shoulder months can extend somewhat compared to populations farther north.

How it lives

Colonies are small by social-insect standards — a few dozen to roughly a hundred adults at peak. A single foundress (or a small group of co-foundresses) initiates the nest in spring; once the first workers emerge, they take over foraging, building, and defense while the dominant female focuses on egg-laying. Workers communicate through pheromones, especially alarm signals near the nest. When a nest is destroyed by a predator, the foundress sometimes rebuilds at a new site — satellite nests up to roughly 35 feet from the original have been documented, with such relocations accounting for a substantial share of all nests built in early summer. Wasps are most active and most defensive during the mid-summer peak when the colony is largest.

Adults fuel their own flight on nectar and other sugary plant secretions. For the larvae, workers hunt soft-bodied insects — caterpillars above all, plus flies and beetle larvae — sting or paralyze them, chew them into a protein paste, and deliver it directly to the cells. UF/IFAS notes that Florida paper wasps specifically target corn earworms, armyworms, and similar garden pests.

What it does here

Paper wasps are a meaningful check on caterpillar populations — including species that damage garden and agricultural plants — and UF/IFAS Extension recognizes them as beneficial predators for exactly this reason. Adults visiting flowers for nectar transfer pollen incidentally, adding a secondary pollinator role. In the broader food web, the wasps themselves are prey for birds, spiders, and praying mantises, and their nests host a suite of parasitoid insects.

Polistes bellicosus is native to the southeastern United States, with its core range centered on coastal Texas and the Gulf Coast states. Confirmed specimens have been recorded throughout Florida — both the peninsula and the panhandle — as well as north to the Carolinas. The species is native and well established in Florida, where the warm climate and long growing season support colonies active from late winter through early winter most years.

The colony cycle of P. bellicosus follows the familiar Polistes rhythm. Overwintered females — called foundresses — get things going in March, building open-celled nests low in vegetation and laying the first eggs. By May the first brood is emerging, mostly workers who take over foraging and nest defense so the foundress can focus on laying.

Late summer is when the colony begins winding down rather than ramping up. The last eggs go in around August or September, and the resulting males and future queens emerge in late September and October. Within a few weeks they disperse from the nest, mate, and the new queens find sheltered overwintering spots. The founding queen and the workers die as temperatures fall.

The nest itself is worth a close look if you spot one from a safe distance. Cells along the outer edge are roughly circular; inner cells are more hexagonal — a geometry that emerges from the physics of packing circles, not from any blueprint the wasps follow. The whole structure hangs open-faced, cells pointing downward, held in place by a single paper stalk.

Take care

The risk

Workers sting readily when the nest is approached or disturbed, and — unlike honeybees — can sting more than once. Stings are painful and produce local swelling. A small percentage of people have severe allergic reactions; anyone who knows they are sensitive to wasp venom should carry appropriate medication and give any nest a wide berth. Foragers away from the nest are much less defensive and rarely sting unless grabbed or pinned against skin.

What to do

Foragers working flowers or vegetation away from the nest are calm and easy to watch at close range — approach slowly and avoid swatting. Give any nest a respectful buffer of several feet and do not touch or prod it. If you find a nest in a high-traffic area of the park, alert staff rather than attempting to remove it yourself.
