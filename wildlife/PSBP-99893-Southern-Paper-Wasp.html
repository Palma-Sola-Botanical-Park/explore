Southern Paper Wasp

Polistes bellicosus
On this page

At a glance

  • Adult paper wasps drink nectar, but their larvae are fed animal prey. Workers hunt caterpillars and other soft-bodied insects, chew them up and carry the food back to the nest.
  • Paper wasps scrape fibers from weathered wood and dead plant stems, chew them into pulp and use the material to build their nest.
  • Unlike a hornet or yellowjacket nest, a paper-wasp nest has no enclosing paper shell. The open cells remain visible, usually facing downward beneath a sheltered attachment point.
  • A female can begin a nest herself. After the first workers mature, they take over much of the foraging, nest building and defense.

How to know it

Size and shape

A slender wasp about three-quarters of an inch long, with a narrow waist, long legs and long wings folded along the body at rest. The legs often trail below the body in flight.

Coloration

Southern Paper Wasps have extensive yellow markings along with darker reddish-brown or black areas. Color and pattern vary, and several other Florida paper wasps can look similar.

The nest

The nest is a single exposed comb of papery cells, usually hanging with the openings facing downward. There is no outer paper covering. Nests may be attached to vegetation or sheltered parts of structures.

Similar species

Several paper-wasp species occur in Florida and can be difficult to separate without a close view. Red Paper Wasps are much more reddish overall. Yellowjackets are more compact, with shorter-looking legs and a heavier body. Honey bees are hairier and lack the extremely narrow wasp waist.

Size

About three-quarters of an inch long.

What to look for

Look for a slender wasp with long legs and a narrow waist. Paper wasps often trail their legs in flight. Their exposed, downward-facing paper comb is even easier to recognize, but do not approach a nest for a closer look.

Easily confused with

Other Florida paper wasps — Several Florida paper wasps are similar, and species-level identification may require a close photograph or examination of small color-pattern details. Red Paper Wasps are generally much redder overall; yellowjackets are more compact and shorter-legged.

Where to find it here

Watch flowering plants for adults drinking nectar or searching foliage for caterpillars. Nests may be attached beneath sheltered overhangs or among woody vegetation. If you notice a nest, watch from a distance rather than moving closer.

Most easily seen during warm weather, when adults forage by day on flowers and vegetation. Nest activity changes through the year, and sheltered adults may survive periods when active colonies are less conspicuous.

When

Most visible during warm months when colonies are active and adults are foraging. Seasonal timing varies with climate and colony history.

How it lives

A female may begin a nest alone or with other females. Once workers emerge, they perform much of the foraging, nest building and defense while reproduction becomes concentrated in the dominant egg-laying female.

Adults feed mainly on nectar and other sugary liquids. Prey taken for the larvae is chewed into small pieces before it is carried home, then delivered cell by cell to the grubs waiting in the comb.

What it does here

Plants provide food, hunting grounds and nest material. Adults visit flowers for nectar, while workers search foliage for caterpillars to feed the larvae. Paper for the nest begins as fibers scraped from weathered wood and dead stems. Southern Paper Wasps have also been recorded nesting on woody plants including baccharis, yaupon holly and blackberry, though those records come from Texas rather than Florida.

The cells near the outer edge of a growing nest may be more rounded, while cells packed among neighbors become more nearly hexagonal.

Take care

The risk

Paper wasps can sting repeatedly when defending a nest. Stings are painful, and some people can have a severe allergic reaction. Give nests plenty of space and never touch, shake or spray one. A wasp feeding away from its nest is much less defensive, but it can still sting if handled or trapped against the skin.

What to do

Watch foraging wasps without touching or swatting at them. Give any nest plenty of space and do not approach it for photographs or identification. Report nests in high-traffic areas to park staff rather than disturbing them.
