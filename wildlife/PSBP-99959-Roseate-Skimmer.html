Roseate Skimmer

Orthemis ferruginea
On this page

At a glance

  • A dragonfly with a vivid rose-pink to purple body cruising the pond edge is a male roseate skimmer — one of the few truly pink dragonflies you will see.
  • The color belongs to mature males. Females and young males are a more modest brown, so the showy pink is a male's badge.
  • It is a creature of warm, often disturbed water, and is common around ponds, ditches, and canals across the southern states.
  • Like every dragonfly, it is a superb flier and hunter, catching small insects in mid-air with a basket formed by its legs.

How to know it

Color

Mature males are rose-pink to red-violet; females and young are brown and tan.

Wings

Mostly clear, sometimes with a faint amber tint at the base.

Body

Slender, typical skimmer shape.

Behavior cue

Perches on low stems and patrols low over the water.

Voice

Silent.

Size

about 1.7 to 2 inches; roughly 2.5 to 3 inches

What to look for

A medium dragonfly with a rose-pink or purplish body working a pond edge — the pink is unmistakable on a male. Brown individuals with the same shape and habits are females or young males.

Where to find it here

Around the ponds and any ditches or basins, perched on low stems or patrolling the shoreline on warm days. Watch for the pink.

A warm-season flier, common from spring through fall.

How it lives

Males patrol low over still water and hold territory along the shoreline, dropping onto a low perch between sorties.

Small flying insects, taken on the wing. The young live underwater and eat mosquito larvae and other small water creatures — which is most of a dragonfly's life.

What it does here

A predator at both life stages, helping keep flying insects and mosquito larvae in check around still waters.

Breeds in ponds and ditches with vegetated margins, perching on low pondside plants.

Native to the southern United States and south into the American tropics. In Florida it is a common warm-season dragonfly of ponds, ditches, and other still waters.

The roseate skimmer stands out for a color most dragonflies never wear. Mature males develop a rosy-pink to red-violet abdomen and thorax, a striking look against the greens and browns of a pond edge. Females and immatures are brown and tan, which is why the pink reads as a male's signal.

It is a hardy, adaptable insect, at home on ponds, roadside ditches, retention basins, and other still or slow water, and it has spread comfortably through the warm parts of the country.

As a predator it follows the dragonfly playbook: adults hunt small flying insects on the wing, gathering prey in a basket made of their bristly legs, while the aquatic young hunt small creatures (including mosquito larvae) underwater before climbing out to transform.
