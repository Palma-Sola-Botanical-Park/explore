Tufted Titmouse

Baeolophus bicolor
On this page

At a glance

  • A small gray bird with a pointed crest, a black smudge just above the bill and big dark eyes. Look for the peach wash along its sides.
  • Its feet are a vise. It pins a big seed between its toes and hammers it open with its short, stout bill.
  • It saves for later. In fall and winter it shells seeds and tucks them into cracks in the bark to come back to.
  • A year-round Floridian. It lives here in every season, mostly in the northern two-thirds of the state, thinning out to the south.

How to know it

Crest, forehead, eyes, flanks

Silvery gray above and white below, with a pointed crest, a black patch just above the bill and large dark eyes. The peach wash on the sides settles it, though a bird in worn feathers can show much less of it.

Listen for the whistle

A clear whistle, peter-peter-peter, repeated over and over, carries through the branches. Learn it and you may hear the bird before you see it.

Not a jay, not a cardinal

The Blue Jay and the Northern Cardinal wear crests too, but both are bigger and boldly colored. The Blue-gray Gnatcatcher is small and gray like a titmouse, but it has no crest, a thin bill, a white ring around the eye and a long tail edged in white.

How it lives

Hair from the source

The nest usually goes into an existing hole, such as an old woodpecker hole or a nest box: a cup of leaves, moss, grass and bark, lined with hair and other soft fibers. The titmouse does not always wait to find that hair lying around. It has been documented pulling hair straight from living mammals, and in one published account a titmouse plucked tufts from a raccoon sleeping in a tree. Other accounts describe it plucking from dogs and even from people. Why it takes the trouble, nobody has shown.

Two parents at work

The female sits on the eggs while the male brings her food. Once the chicks hatch, both parents feed them.

What it does

Upside down if it has to be

It works over leaves, cones and the undersides of branches, sometimes hanging sideways or upside down to reach. Caterpillars, beetles and other insects make up much of its warm-season food, and seeds, nuts and berries fill out the rest.

Part of the crowd

It joins mixed flocks with chickadees, nuthatches and woodpeckers. When other birds start an agitated chatter, it tends to come in for a look, and it may join a group mobbing a predator. If you hear small birds scolding in a tree, look for a gray crest in the middle of the fuss.
