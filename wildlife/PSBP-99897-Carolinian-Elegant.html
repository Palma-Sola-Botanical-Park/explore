Carolinian Elegant

Meromacrus acutus
On this page

At a glance

  • Its black-and-yellow pattern makes it look like a wasp or bee, but it is a fly and has no stinger.
  • Like other hoverflies, it can hold nearly still in mid-air before darting to another position.
  • Larvae of this type develop in wet, decaying organic material inside water-filled tree cavities and breathe at the surface through a long rear siphon.
  • Adults feed at flowers, taking nectar and pollen and carrying pollen between blooms as they move.

How to know it

Size

A relatively large hoverfly, about half to three-quarters of an inch long.

Overall pattern

Black and yellow, with a wasp-like banded abdomen and yellow markings on the dark thorax.

Wings

As a true fly, it has one functional pair of wings rather than the two pairs found in wasps and bees.

Size

About half to three-quarters of an inch long.

What to look for

Look on flowers for a large black-and-yellow fly that resembles a wasp. Hovering in place, large fly-like eyes and a single functional pair of wings help reveal what it really is.

Where to find it here

Check flowering plants in sunny openings and along woodland edges. Adults are usually encountered singly, either visiting flowers, hovering nearby or resting on sunlit foliage.

Look during warm weather when adults are active around flowers. Palma Sola has too few records to define a local seasonal pattern.

When

Active in warm weather. Palma Sola records are too limited to define a local seasonal pattern.

How it lives

Adults visit flowers for nectar and pollen and can hover almost motionless before shifting position.

The larval stage develops in wet, decaying organic material, including water-filled cavities in trees.

What it does here

Adults can carry pollen between flowers while feeding. Larvae consume organic material in wet tree cavities, while adults and larvae also become food for other animals.

This fly depends on plants in two very different ways. Adults visit flowers for nectar and pollen, while the larval stage uses wet cavities in aging or decaying trees. Mature trees with rot holes therefore provide habitat that younger, perfectly sound trees may not.

Native to the Americas and recorded in Florida. The species occurs in warm wooded habitats where mature trees and flowering plants occur together.

The larva breathes through a long rear siphon that reaches the water surface while the body remains submerged, acting like a snorkel.
