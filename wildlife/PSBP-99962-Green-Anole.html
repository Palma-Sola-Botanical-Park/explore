Green Anole

Anolis carolinensis
On this page

At a glance

  • A slender lizard that can shift from bright green to brown, flashing a pink throat fan, is the green anole — Florida's only native anole.
  • Despite the color-changing, it is not a chameleon. It switches between green and brown with mood and temperature, not to match its background.
  • It has been quietly pushed upward. Since the brown anole arrived from the Caribbean, green anoles spend more time high in shrubs and trees, out of the newcomer's way.
  • Pressure from the brown anole is driving fast change: green anoles on brown-anole turf have been measured evolving larger, stickier toepads for gripping higher, smoother perches.

How to know it

Easily confused with

Brown Anole — The brown anole cannot turn green, has an orange-red throat fan, and stays low; the green anole can be green or brown, has a pink fan, and perches higher.

Color

Bright green to brown, able to switch between the two; the only local anole that turns true green.

Throat fan (dewlap)

Males flash a pink dewlap during displays.

Build

Slender, with a long tail and a pointed snout; smoother-scaled than the brown anole.

Perch

Often higher up — in shrubs and trees — than the ground-loving brown anole.

Voice

Silent. Like other anoles, it communicates with head-bobs and its colorful dewlap rather than sound.

Size

5 to 8 inches, including the tail.

What to look for

A slim lizard that is bright green (or a green-brown in between), often up in shrubbery, with a pink throat fan in displaying males. The ability to turn true green is the clincher — the brown anole never can.

Where to find it here

Up in the shrubbery and on tree trunks and vines, a step higher than the brown anoles on the fences and ground. Look for a green lizard with a pink throat fan.

Year-round resident, most active and most often green on warm, sunny days.

When

Most often green on warm, sunny days.

How it lives

A confident climber that spends its day up in shrubs, vines and tree trunks, higher than the brown anole usually goes. Males hold territory with push-up displays and flashes of a pink dewlap.

Small insects and other invertebrates — flies, beetles, spiders, crickets — hunted among leaves and branches. It basks to warm up, and can drop its tail to escape a predator and regrow it later.

What it does here

A native insect-eater and, in turn, prey for birds, snakes, and larger lizards. Its push toward higher perches is one of Florida's clearest stories of an introduced species reshaping a native's life.

Lives among shrubs, vines, and trees, using leafy cover for hunting, basking, and shelter from the ground-dwelling brown anole.

Native to the southeastern United States, including all of Florida. It is the only anole native to the state, found in gardens, woodland edges, and shrubby areas.

The green anole is often called the "American chameleon," but the nickname oversells it. It can turn from vivid green to brown and back, yet the change tracks temperature, light, and mood rather than its surroundings — a cold or stressed anole tends toward brown, a warm relaxed one toward green.

For a long time it was the only anole in Florida. Then the brown anole arrived from Cuba and the Bahamas, wanting the same food and the same sunny perches. Rather than disappear, green anoles largely shifted upward, spending more time in shrubs and tree canopies where browns are scarcer.

That shift has left a mark you can measure. In areas shared with brown anoles, green anoles have been found evolving larger, stickier toepads over just a handful of generations — a textbook case of rapid evolution, prompted by an introduced neighbor, happening right in Florida gardens.
