Northern Cardinal

Cardinalis cardinalis
On this page

At a glance

  • Males are bright red with a pointed crest and black face mask; females are warm buff-brown with red tinges. Both carry the same stout orange-red bill.
  • Both sexes sing. In many North American songbirds only the male does.
  • That heavy conical bill is a seed-cracker, opening tough seeds many birds cannot manage.
  • Cardinals hold the same territory all year rather than migrating, so a pair may be the same birds you saw last season.

How to know it

Male

Bright red overall, with a pointed crest, black face mask and stout orange-red bill.

Female

Warm buff-brown with reddish tinges on the crest, wings and tail, and the same heavy orange bill.

Size

About eight or nine inches long, with a wingspan around a foot.

Voice

Clear whistled phrases — cheer-cheer-cheer, birdy-birdy-birdy — sung by both sexes, plus a sharp metallic chip note.

What to look for

Look low in shrubs and along woodland edges rather than high in the canopy. The whistles carry further than the bird is visible.

Where to find it here

In shrubs, hedges and woodland edges, often near the ground. Listen for the loud clear whistles of either sex.

A year-round resident, active by day in every season.

How it lives

Usually in pairs or small family groups, foraging on or near the ground and in shrubs. Cardinals do not migrate and defend the same territory through the year.

Mostly seeds and grain, with fruit, berries and insects added — insects especially while raising young.

The female sings as readily as the male, which is unusual among songbirds, and a pair will trade phrases back and forth. In courtship the male passes her a seed beak-to-beak.

The heavy conical bill does the work of a nutcracker, splitting seed coats too tough for most birds its size.

What it does here

Seeds and thickets

Cardinals need two things from plants at once: seed- and berry-bearing shrubs and trees for food, and dense shrubbery to nest in. A planting that supplies both — fruiting natives with thick cover beneath — is what keeps a pair resident rather than passing through.
