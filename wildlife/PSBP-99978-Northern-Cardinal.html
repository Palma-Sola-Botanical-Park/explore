Northern Cardinal

Cardinalis cardinalis
On this page

At a glance

  • The male northern cardinal is one of the most recognizable birds in the eastern U.S. — bright red all over, with a pointed crest and a black mask around a stout orange bill.
  • The female is easy to overlook by comparison: warm buff-brown with red highlights, but every bit a cardinal, crest and all.
  • Unlike most songbirds, the female sings too, often answering the male in a back-and-forth from the shrubs.
  • The thick, cone-shaped bill is a seed-cracking tool, which is why cardinals are such reliable visitors to feeders and seed-bearing plants.

How to know it

Male

Bright red overall, with a pointed crest, black face mask, and stout orange-red bill.

Female

Warm buff-brown with reddish tinges on the crest, wings, and tail, and the same orange bill.

Both

A tall crest and a short, thick, cone-shaped bill.

Voice

Clear, whistled songs — phrases like 'cheer-cheer-cheer' and 'birdy-birdy-birdy' — sung by both sexes, plus a sharp metallic 'chip' note.

Size

8 to 9 inches; 10 to 12 inches

What to look for

A crested songbird with a heavy orange bill — vivid red if a male, soft brown with red accents if a female. Listen for clear, whistled 'cheer-cheer-cheer' and 'birdy-birdy-birdy' phrases from both sexes.

Where to find it here

In shrubs, hedges, and woodland edges, often near the ground. The male's red is easy to spot; listen for the loud, clear whistles of both sexes.

Year-round resident, active by day in every season.

How it lives

Around all year, usually in pairs or small family groups, foraging on or near the ground and in shrubs, and coming readily to feeders.

Mainly seeds and grain, plus fruit, berries and insects — especially in summer, when the insects go to the growing young.

Fighting its own reflection

In breeding season a male will attack a window, a car mirror, a hubcap — anything that shows him another cardinal on his territory.

What it does here

A seed- and fruit-eater that helps disperse the seeds of native plants, and an insect-eater that helps keep bug numbers down while raising young.

Eats the seeds, fruits, and berries of many native shrubs and trees and nests in dense shrubbery — so seed- and berry-bearing native plantings draw cardinals in.

Native and widespread across the eastern and central United States; a common, year-round resident throughout Florida. It does not migrate, holding the same territory all year.

The cardinal is a year-round neighbor rather than a seasonal visitor — pairs often hold the same territory through every season, which is part of why they feel so familiar.

They are unusual among songbirds in that the female sings as readily as the male, and the two will trade phrases back and forth. During courtship you may also catch a tender behavior called mate-feeding, where the male passes a seed to the female beak-to-beak.

Everything about that heavy, conical bill is built for seeds: it lets the cardinal crack tough seed coats many birds cannot, so it thrives wherever seed-bearing plants and feeders are found.
