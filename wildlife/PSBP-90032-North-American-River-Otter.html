North American River Otter

Lontra canadensis

At a glance

  • An otter's long stiff whiskers can detect the movements of prey in dark or murky water — no eyesight required. They are packed with nerve endings and sensitive enough to track a fish's pressure wave through the water.
  • What looks like play usually isn't idle. Mud-sliding, chasing, and water wrestling appear to strengthen social bonds and give young otters practice at the quick turns they need to catch fish.
  • Otters leave piles of droppings — called spraint — at fixed spots on prominent logs and rocks near the water. Each deposit carries a chemical signature that tells other otters who was here and when.
  • In Florida, otter densities are highest in swamp forest and lowest in open freshwater marsh.

How to know it

Body shape

Long, low, and streamlined — the body is cylindrical, built for pushing through water. Short legs, a thick neck, and a long tail that tapers to a point. On land the posture is arched and waddling; in water the animal moves with surprising speed.

Fur and color

Rich dark brown on the back and sides, fading to a lighter, sometimes silvery-gray on the chin, throat, and belly. The fur is dense and water-repellent.

Head and face

Small, flat head with a broad muzzle and prominent long whiskers. The ears are small and round and can close when the animal dives.

Feet

All four feet are webbed. Tracks show five toes and a partial heel pad that looks somewhat horseshoe-shaped — distinctive in soft mud near the water.

What to look for

Watch for a sleek, dark shape moving low and fast at the water's surface, often with a rolling dive that briefly shows the tapered tail. Fresh spraint on a waterside log is a reliable sign that otters are using the area even when none are visible.

Where to find it here

It helps to be an early riser

A favorite local photographer, Helen, has no trouble finding otters — her photos are all over this page. Her secret seems to be arriving bright and early. The volunteer writing this paragraph has walked this park for more than five years and still hasn't seen one.

Scan the water's edge

Most sightings so far are at the south end of Big Pond, around the weir (a dam-like structure) back there. Otters seem to like it — and when they're done, they have an easy way out: a small gully and waterway that runs between the park and the neighboring development, letting them slink off out of sight.

When

Otters are shy around people and most active at dusk, overnight, and in the early morning — quiet, low-traffic times give the best chance of a sighting. A family group with young pups can appear at any hour.

How it lives

A generalist hunter

Fish are the core of the diet, supplemented heavily by crayfish and other crustaceans where they are available. Frogs and the occasional small turtle round out the menu. Otters shift opportunistically toward whichever aquatic prey is easiest to catch.

Mostly a night shift

Mostly crepuscular and nocturnal, though a family group may be active any time of day. They forage alone or in small groups, often swimming in a porpoise-like rolling motion at the surface.

A chemical bulletin board

Scent marking at fixed latrine sites is a regular activity, and the deposits accumulate into a record of who is using a stretch of water. Play — sliding, wrestling, chasing — is common in adults as well as young.

Not a quiet animal

Otters have a wide vocal range: chirps and whistles when socializing or searching for family members, growls and snorts when agitated, and a loud, carrying scream when seriously alarmed. A family group moving along the bank can be audible before it's visible.

Denning

Otters den in burrows along the water's edge but almost never dig their own — they move into holes left by other mammals or settle into natural hollows. Entrances at or below the waterline discourage most predators.

What it does here

A read on the water, cautiously

Because contaminants from fish and other aquatic prey accumulate upward through the food chain, otters are useful animals for researchers monitoring watershed health generally. That's a reason to value otters showing up here — it isn't a guarantee about this specific pond, since otters travel and use a range of waterways, some more altered than others.

Take care

Give them room

Wild otters rarely approach people, but a cornered or injured animal can bite hard. Give any otter on land plenty of room and do not attempt to handle one.

Pets near the water

An otter defending a food item or a den site may confront a dog at the water's edge. Keep pets leashed near ponds where otters are active.
