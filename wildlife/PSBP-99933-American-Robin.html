American Robin

Turdus migratorius
On this page

At a glance

  • The American robin — brick-red breast, dark back, yellow bill — is one of the most recognized birds in North America, but in Florida it is a winter visitor, not a year-round neighbor.
  • It arrives in large flocks in fall, feeding on berries and worms across lawns and open ground, then heads north to breed.
  • Despite its name, it is not closely related to the European robin — early colonists just thought the red breast looked familiar.
  • A robin on a Florida lawn in January is on vacation; by April it is usually back up north nesting.

How to know it

Breast

Warm brick-red to orange.

Back

Dark gray-brown.

Bill

Yellow.

Behavior

Runs across lawns, pauses, tilts head, then pulls up a worm.

Voice

A rich, caroling song ('cheerily, cheer-up, cheerio') — but mostly heard on the breeding grounds, not in Florida.

Size

9 to 11 inches; about 14 to 16 inches

What to look for

A plump, upright bird with a red breast hopping across a lawn. In Florida, a winter visitor in flocks.

Where to find it here

Open lawns and under fruiting trees in winter.

Winter visitor — typically November through March.

When

Breeds far to the north; present in Florida only in the cold months.

How it lives

On a lawn it runs a few steps, stops, and cocks its head — not listening, as it looks, but getting one eye on the ground to catch a worm moving.

Earthworms and insects through the warm months. In winter the diet swings almost entirely to fruit, and robins that fed alone all summer turn up in flocks.

What it does here

Berry disperser in winter; earthworm and insect predator in summer.

Heavy berry consumer in winter — disperses many native and ornamental fruit species.

Native to North America. Breeds across the U.S. and Canada; winters in the southern U.S. including Florida. A seasonal visitor here.

The American robin is arguably the most familiar bird in North America — but in Florida it is a seasonal guest, arriving in flocks from late fall and departing by spring.

In winter it shifts from its summer diet of earthworms and insects to one dominated by berries and fruit, and large flocks can strip a fruiting tree in hours.

Its presence on a Florida lawn is a good seasonal marker: if you see robins, it is probably winter.
