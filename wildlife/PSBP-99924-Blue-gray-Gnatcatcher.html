Blue-gray Gnatcatcher

Polioptila caerulea
On this page

At a glance

  • A Blue-gray Gnatcatcher is almost constantly moving, flicking its long black-and-white tail as it searches the outer branches of trees for small prey.
  • Despite the name, gnats are not an important part of its known diet. It eats small insects and spiders picked from foliage or caught in short flights.
  • Both parents build a small cup nest from plant fibers, bark and grasses, bind it with spider silk, and cover the outside with bits of lichen and bark.
  • A pair may build as many as seven nests in one breeding season, often recycling material from an earlier nest after a nesting attempt fails.

How to know it

Easily confused with

Ruby-crowned Kinglet — Ruby-crowned Kinglets are similarly tiny and active but have shorter tails and no white outer tail feathers.

Size

A tiny, slender songbird about four inches long, with a tail that looks unusually long for its body.

Plumage

Blue-gray above and whitish below, with a thin white eye ring and a long black tail edged with white. Breeding males develop a narrow black line across the forehead; females and nonbreeding males lack it.

Behavior

Usually in motion, hopping and sidling through outer foliage while repeatedly cocking or flicking the long tail.

Voice

The call is a thin, nasal note often given repeatedly while the bird forages. The breeding song is a soft series of wheezy notes and may include imitations of other birds.

Size

About four inches long, with a wingspan around six inches.

What to look for

Look for a very small gray-and-white bird moving quickly through the outer foliage of trees. The long black-and-white tail is often cocked or flicked from side to side. A thin nasal call may reveal the bird before you see it.

Where to find it here

Look along wooded edges and wherever trees have open outer branches. Blue-gray Gnatcatchers usually search the foliage rather than the trunk, moving quickly from branch to branch. Listen for the thin nasal call and then follow the movement.

Blue-gray Gnatcatchers occur in Florida through much of the year, with both resident and migratory birds contributing to the state's population. Palma Sola has too few observations to define a reliable local seasonal pattern.

When

The species occurs in Florida in multiple seasons, but Palma Sola currently has too few records to establish local abundance or migration peaks.

How it lives

It searches the outer foliage of trees and shrubs, hopping along branches, hovering briefly and making short flights after prey. The long tail is frequently cocked or flicked as it forages, and flashing the white outer tail feathers may help startle insects into moving.

It eats small insects and spiders taken from leaves, branches and the air.

The nest

Pairs build with plant fibers, bark and grasses, bind the nest with spider silk and cover it with lichen. After a nest fails, they may dismantle it and reuse the material elsewhere.

What it does here

A small predator of insects and spiders in tree and shrub foliage. Gnatcatchers in turn become prey for larger birds, snakes and other predators.

Trees and shrubs provide both hunting habitat and nesting material. Gnatcatchers search leaves and outer branches for prey, while breeding pairs build their nests from bark, grasses and other plant fibers. Lichen camouflages the outside, and spider silk binds the structure together.

Native to North America. Blue-gray Gnatcatchers breed across much of the United States and southern Canada, with many moving south toward the southern United States, Mexico and the Caribbean outside the breeding season. Florida supports birds through much of the year.
