Blue-gray Gnatcatcher

Polioptila caerulea
On this page

At a glance

  • A tiny, hyperactive, blue-gray bird with a long black-and-white tail it constantly flicks — like a miniature mockingbird on caffeine.
  • Despite the name, gnats are not a significant part of its diet. It hunts leafhoppers, beetles, caterpillars, flies, small wasps, and spiders — sometimes shaking a leaf-covered twig to flush prey into the open.
  • Its nest is a masterwork: a compact cup of bark strips and grasses, bound together with spider silk and camouflaged on the outside with pieces of lichen — so convincing it looks like a natural tree knot.
  • A pair can build up to seven nests in a single breeding season, often dismantling earlier attempts and reusing the materials when predation or mite infestations cause failures.

How to know it

Size

Tiny and slender — about 4 to 4.5 inches long, slightly larger than a Ruby-throated Hummingbird and smaller than a House Wren.

Plumage

Blue-gray above, whitish below, with a bold white eye ring. The long tail is black with white outer feathers that make it appear mostly white from below. Breeding males add a narrow black V-shaped line across the forehead extending above the eyes; females and non-breeding birds lack this marking and appear slightly grayer.

Behavior

Constantly in motion — flitting through outer foliage, cocking and flicking its tail from side to side. The tail action is one of the best field marks: no other small Florida songbird fidgets quite this persistently.

Voice

Most commonly heard as a thin, nasal, whining call — often written as pzzzz or zeee — delivered in short bursts. The song is a soft, rambling series of wheezy warbles and slurs, sometimes incorporating mimicked fragments of other species' songs, earning this bird its 'Little Mockingbird' nickname. Songs are heard mainly during the breeding season; the insistent call notes are given year-round and are typically the first sign of the bird's presence.

Size

4–4.5 inches; wingspan about 6.3 inches

What to look for

Look for a tiny, restless bird high in the mid-story or canopy, its long tail pumping side to side almost non-stop. The white eye ring, white tail edges, and blue-gray upperparts are diagnostic. Listen for the thin, whiny pzzzz call — it usually announces the bird before you see it.

Where to find it here

Scan the mid-story and canopy of wooded areas, especially along edges and near water. Listen first — the thin, nasal call note cuts through background noise and reliably betrays the bird's location before it comes into view. Binoculars are essential; the bird moves fast and stays high. Early morning is the most productive time, when feeding activity peaks.

Present year-round at Palma Sola Botanical Park, though the individuals here in summer (breeders) differ from those present in winter (migrants from further north). Numbers swell during spring migration (April) and again in fall (September–October). Most active and vocal from shortly after sunrise through mid-morning. Song is heard primarily in the breeding season (spring–early summer); call notes give it away throughout the year.

When

Florida hosts year-round residents plus distinct wintering and breeding populations; individual birds present in summer differ from those present in winter as the species is fully migratory across North America.

How it lives

Intensely active foliage gleaner; barely pauses between perches. Forages by hopping and siduling along outer branches, hovering briefly to snatch prey, and making short aerial sallies. The tail is habitually cocked upright or flicked rapidly side to side — both during foraging and territorial display. Fiercely territorial during the breeding season: rivals can be chased up to 70 feet, and confrontations sometimes escalate into breast-to-breast aerial skirmishes. Outside the breeding season, individuals are more tolerant and will join mixed-species foraging flocks.

Primarily small insects and spiders: leafhoppers, treehoppers, plant bugs, leaf beetles, caterpillars, flies, small wasps, and spider egg cases. Ironically, gnats form no significant part of the diet. Prey is gleaned from outer foliage, snatched in mid-air, or flushed by shaking leaf-covered twigs.

What it does here

An important insectivore of the forest mid-story and canopy, suppressing populations of foliage-dwelling insects including several agricultural pests. Also serves as prey for small raptors and snakes, placing it squarely in the middle of the local food web. Its lichen-decorated nest is sometimes dismantled and rebuilt so many times per season that it becomes a net exporter of spider silk and plant fibers back into the habitat.

The Blue-gray Gnatcatcher breeds across most of the eastern United States and parts of the West, extending into southern Ontario, Mexico, and Cuba. Florida is exceptional: the species is a year-round resident here, with local breeders joined by waves of migrants passing through in spring (peaking around April) and fall (peaking around September). Winter concentrations are highest in south Florida and along the Gulf Coast. The breeding range has been shifting northward since the early twentieth century, tracking rising average temperatures — a trend that continues today.

Everything about the Blue-gray Gnatcatcher reads as superlative for its size. At just 4 to 4.5 inches long and weighing barely more than a nickel, it is one of the smallest songbirds in North America — yet it forages with relentless energy, flitting through the outer foliage of trees and shrubs, hovering briefly to snatch prey, and sallying into the air after escaping insects. The perpetually flicking white-edged tail is both an identification mark and a foraging tool: flashing those white outer feathers startles insects into flight, making them easier to catch.

The species has earned the nickname 'Little Mockingbird' for good reason. Its nasal, wheezy, rambling song weaves snippets of other birds' vocalizations into a continuous warble that can be surprisingly difficult to pin down. The more often-heard call — a thin, insistent pzzzz or zeee — is usually your first clue the bird is nearby, long before you spot it high in the canopy. Males hold two distinct song types: a softer version used in courtship and territory establishment, and a louder, more urgent version deployed in direct confrontations.

Breeding pairs are devoted architects. Both sexes construct an exquisite cup nest, typically saddled on a horizontal branch 20 to 40 feet up, built from plant fibers, bark strips, and feathers, bound with spider silk, and tiled on the outside with crustose lichens for camouflage so effective the nest resembles nothing so much as a natural knot on the branch. When nests fail to predation or parasitism — which happens often — the pair simply disassembles the structure and starts again nearby, sometimes logging up to seven nest attempts in a single season.
