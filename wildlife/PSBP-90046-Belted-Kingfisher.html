Belted Kingfisher

At a glance

  • Here, the female gets the extra color. Both sexes wear a blue-gray band across the chest, but the female adds a wide rusty band across her belly — and nobody is entirely sure why.
  • You will probably hear it before you see it: a loud, rattling call that carries down a waterway well ahead of the bird.
  • It spots the fish first, then dives with its eyes closed.
  • Some stay in Florida year-round, while fall and winter bring additional birds south from colder parts of the continent.

How to know it

Size and shape

About thirteen inches long — roughly robin-sized, but built nothing like one. The oversized head, shaggy crest, and heavy, dagger-like bill give it a top-heavy look. Look for that silhouette perched above open water, or hovering bill-down before a dive.

Markings

Blue-gray above, white below, with a white collar and a broad blue-gray band across the breast. Both sexes share these markings. Females add a wide rusty band lower on the belly — the surest way to tell them apart.

In flight

The big head and short tail are recognizable at a distance. Look for a large white patch in the outer wing, which can show clearly as the bird crosses open water.

Voice

A loud, dry rattle — hard to mistake for anything else once you know it. The bird calls freely in flight and uses it to announce its claim on a stretch of water.

Where to find it here

Look for a lookout. A bare branch, wire, or piling over open water is exactly the kind of place a kingfisher likes to sit and watch for fish. Scan the edges of ponds and any open water where something extends over the surface with a clear view down.

When

Present through the year, but more reliably seen in the cooler months when birds from farther north join the local population.

How it lives

Belted Kingfishers are usually solitary and territorial, often claiming a stretch of shoreline or waterway. It watches from a perch above the surface — branch, wire, piling, anything with a clear view below — and drops headfirst when it spots a fish or crayfish. It may also hover in place, bill angled downward, before committing to the plunge.

Back on the perch with a catch, it pounds the fish against the branch to stun it, then tosses and swallows it headfirst. Adults later cough up a compact pellet of bones and scales — the parts their stomach cannot process.

Young kingfishers start out differently: nestlings can digest the bones and scales in their fish meals whole. By the time they leave the burrow, that changes, and they begin producing pellets like the adults.

Their nest is a tunnel, not a nest in a tree. Both parents dig into an earthen bank with their bills, excavating a burrow that typically runs three to six feet into the bank before opening into a chamber. The tunnel slopes upward as it goes, which keeps rain from pooling at the end.

The chamber itself is bare earth — no lining — but as the season goes on, regurgitated fish bones and scales accumulate on the floor and end up providing a measure of insulation.

What it does here

A kingfisher hunts by sight, which means it needs water clear enough to pick out a fish from above. One working our ponds is a small vote of confidence in the water itself — and it takes small fish and crayfish out of them daily, a steady predator on a pond that has few others.
