Smooth Rattlebox

Crotalaria pallida

At a glance

  • As the pods dry they inflate, and the loose seeds inside turn the whole plant into a maraca.
  • A persistent volunteer to the right of the office steps. It owns that spot and keeps coming back.
  • Every part of it is toxic — and one moth has turned that to its own advantage.

How to know it

Shake a pod

Inflated, papery, and full of loose seeds. If it rattles, that is the identification.

Flowers

Yellow and pea-shaped, in upright spikes at the stem tips, often finely streaked with reddish-brown.

Three oval leaflets to a leaf, on erect branching stems three to six feet tall and woody at the base.

How it grows

An erect annual or short-lived perennial. It dies back in frost and returns from seed, and it fixes its own nitrogen — part of why poor sandy ground suits it so well.

Where it comes from

Not from here. Smooth Rattlebox is native to the Old World tropics, across Asia and Africa, and has naturalised through Florida's roadsides, field edges and disturbed sandy ground.

In this park

It has claimed the ground to the right of the office steps and keeps returning. One day something nicer will go there.

What it does here

The moth that eats the poison

The whole plant carries pyrrolizidine alkaloids. Ornate Bella Moth caterpillars feed on it anyway, take up those alkaloids and carry them into adulthood as a defence — so the bright pink and orange moth is advertising chemistry it got from this weed. See the moth

The caterpillars often destroy the developing seeds as they feed, which is a small check on a plant that spreads readily.

Take care

Toxic

Do not eat any part of it. The alkaloids cause liver damage in people and animals, and the same is true for dogs — keep pets away from the pods and seeds.

It spreads

It moves quickly through sandy disturbed ground and is treated as an invasive in Florida. Volunteers are worth pulling while they are small.
