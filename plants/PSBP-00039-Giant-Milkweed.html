Giant Milkweed

Calotropis gigantea

At a glance

  • Monarch and Queen caterpillars can eat these toxic leaves and keep some of the plant's defensive chemicals, which helps make the butterflies themselves distasteful to predators.
  • This really is a milkweed, just on a different scale: a tropical shrub that can grow well over a person's head, with broad gray-green leaves and milky sap.
  • The waxy flowers hold packets of pollen rather than loose grains. A visiting insect can catch a packet on its body and carry the whole bundle to another flower.
  • Giant Milkweed is not native to Florida. UF/IFAS predicts it will become invasive here and no longer recommends planting it. We have yet to find a seedling of it anywhere in the park.

Cultural significance

Fiber strong enough for bowstrings

Fibers from the bark have long been used in parts of Asia for cordage, fishing nets and bowstrings, giving the plant another common name: Bowstring Hemp.

The Crown Flower

The raised center of each flower gives Giant Milkweed its other common name, Crown Flower. The flowers became especially associated with lei-making in Hawaii after the plant was introduced there.

Where to find it here

Two very different spots

One of our largest Giant Milkweeds is hard to miss. It frames the office parking area alongside Firecracker Plant, Amaryllis and Beautyberry. Our other large plant is almost the opposite: tucked behind the Butterfly Garden, where it is easy to walk past without seeing it.

When

Giant Milkweed stays leafy and can flower through much of the year in our climate. Look for clusters of waxy lavender-and-white flowers and, later, inflated pods filled with silky, wind-carried seeds.

Where it comes from

From tropical Asia

Giant Milkweed is native to tropical Asia and has been carried far beyond its original range as an ornamental, a fiber plant and a traditional-use plant. It thrives in warm climates and readily colonizes open and disturbed ground.

What it does

The one everybody sees

The Giant Milkweed by the office is one of the most prominent plants visitors encounter when they arrive. Its large crown-shaped flowers regularly draw attention, and often lead to the question of what such a tropical-looking shrub has to do with butterflies.

The one the caterpillars find

The second large Giant Milkweed is tucked behind the Butterfly Garden and gets far less attention from people. Monarch caterpillars find it just fine. At times many feed there together, chewing enough foliage to leave the plant noticeably stripped.

Borrowing the plant's defense

Giant Milkweed produces cardenolides, chemicals that discourage many animals from eating it. Monarch caterpillars can tolerate these compounds and retain some in their bodies, carrying part of the plant's chemical defense into adulthood.

How to know it

Start with the leaves

Giant Milkweed has enormous opposite leaves, pale gray-green and thick to the touch. From a distance they give the whole shrub a silvery cast.

Crown-shaped flowers

Look closely at a flower. Five waxy petals surround a raised, five-part center resembling a tiny crown, the source of the name Crown Flower.

Milky sap

A damaged leaf or stem releases thick white latex. Don't break the plant to test it: the sap is irritating and can be dangerous in the eyes.

Look for what's missing

Ragged edges, partially eaten leaves and nearly bare stems can mean caterpillars have been feeding. Our Butterfly Garden plant sometimes gets thoroughly munched.

How it grows

Easy to start from a stem

When we cut back the large plant by our office, we have often used the trimmings to make new plants. We trim the stems, remove some of the flowers, use rooting powder and start the cuttings in pots. Giant Milkweed roots readily this way.

Fast in warm weather

Giant Milkweed can quickly become a large shrub in Florida's warmth, roughly 10 to 15 feet tall, though a cold snap can knock it back. It handles sun, dry conditions and well-drained soil well.

Pollen in packages

Like other milkweeds, Giant Milkweed bundles its pollen into structures called pollinia. A visiting insect can snag an entire pollen packet and carry it to another flower.

Seeds made to travel

Mature pods split open and release seeds attached to silky hairs that catch the wind. That is the trait behind Giant Milkweed's current Florida rating: UF/IFAS assessed it in 2024, predicts it will become invasive across the state, and does not recommend planting it. In our park, after years of pods, we have yet to find one seedling that started on its own. Every new plant here has come from a cutting.

Take care

Avoid the sap

Broken leaves and stems release milky latex that can irritate skin and cause serious eye injury. Don't break or handle the plant, and keep the sap away from your eyes.

Do not eat

Giant Milkweed contains toxic cardiac glycosides. The same chemistry the caterpillars have evolved to tolerate makes this a plant people and pets should not eat.
