Sea Ox-eye Daisy

Borrichia frutescens
On this page

At a glance

  • A tiny midge makes a stem tip swell into a round ball, called a gall, where its young feed on a fungus the midge brought along. A stem with a gall will not flower.
  • A whole patch can be a single plant. The stems sucker from shared roots, so many colonies are one genetic individual.
  • After the yellow daisies fade, each flower head dries into a small, hard, spiny ball.
  • It is a plant of salty ground. It grows in salt marshes and at the edge of mangroves, where few other plants can.

Where it comes from

Salt marsh to mangrove edge

It grows along the Atlantic and Gulf coasts from Virginia or Maryland to Texas, south to the Yucatan and in the Keys, in salt marshes, at the edge of mangroves and on salty shores. Pressed specimens from Manatee County come from Longboat Key, Perico Island, near Terra Ceia and Rubonia, and the Madira Bickel Mound.

Where to find it here

You are seeing this plant in action: park volunteers and Bright Futures high school students are actively planting sea ox-eye around the park's eroding pond banks, supported by grants from both the Tampa Bay and Sarasota Bay Estuary Programs.

What it does

A plant for the bank

Its roots run sideways and sucker into a dense patch, which is why it is planted on eroding banks.

How to know it

Silver, fleshy leaves

The leaves are thick and fleshy, in pairs, and silvery gray-green. The silver comes from fine hairs on the leaf. The leaf stalk usually carries a small spine.

Small yellow daisies

The flower heads are bright yellow, with fifteen to thirty short rays around a center of black-tipped stamens. It flowers all year, mostly in spring and summer, and plants stand about two to four feet tall.

How it grows

Salt and sun

It wants full sun and takes salt in the soil and in the air. It spreads by roots and by seed into clumps.
