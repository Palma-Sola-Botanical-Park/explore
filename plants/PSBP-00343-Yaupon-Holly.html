At a glance

  • A Florida native holly whose leaves carry caffeine. Southeastern peoples brewed them for centuries, and the tea is sold again today under the old name, yaupon.
  • The species name, vomitoria, is a slander. Europeans watched ceremonies in which drinkers purged, and blamed the plant. Brewed as a tea, it is simply a mild stimulant.
  • Male and female flowers grow on separate plants. Only females carry the red winter berries, which are for birds, not people.
  • Ours was planted as the weeping form, 'Pendula', and its branches arch and hang rather than standing up.

Cultural significance

The black drink

Across the Southeast, the Timucua, Apalachee, Creek and other peoples roasted yaupon leaves and brewed a strong dark tea for councils and ceremonies. Spanish colonists in Florida called it cacina, from the Timucua; the Creek called it the white drink, for purity and peace. Traces of it have turned up in pottery beakers at Cahokia, near St. Louis, from around the year 1100, hundreds of miles beyond where the plant grows, so the leaves were traded that far.

A native tea again

Yaupon never stopped being a tea plant; it only fell out of fashion. Small Florida and Texas producers roast and sell the leaves again. The tea has no bitterness, because the leaves lack the tannins of ordinary tea, so it cannot be over-steeped.

Where it comes from

The Southeast coast

Yaupon grows wild along the coastal plain from Virginia around to Texas and into Mexico, and through most of the Florida peninsula. It takes dunes, maritime forest, pine flatwoods and swamp edges alike, and salt wind does not trouble it. The name comes from the Catawba word for a small tree.

Where to find it here

Front Pond, nursery side

On the bank of the Front Pond, on the side nearest the nursery. From the drive that leads toward the nursery, go between the Gumbo Limbo and the Peregrina and head for the water; the yaupon is the open, arching shrub at the pond's edge.

What it does here

The native tea plant

This is North America's own tea plant, the one native of the continent that people brew for its caffeine, and it grows wild within a few miles of the park. Ours is young and still finding its shape as a weeping shrub. Whether it is a male or a female plant, the winter berries will tell.

For the elfin and the waxwing

Yaupon is a caterpillar food for Henry's Elfin, a small brown spring butterfly, and its flowers feed bees in March and April when little else in the understory is open. If ours proves female, robins, mockingbirds and cedar waxwings will find the berries.

How to know it

Small scalloped leaves

Small oval leaves, dark and leathery, under an inch long, with a finely scalloped edge. They are much smaller than most people expect of a holly, with no spines.

Arching branches

On 'Pendula' the branches arch up and then hang, so even a young plant looks like a small fountain rather than a bush. The bark is smooth and whitish-grey.

Flowers on both, berries on one

Tiny white four-petalled flowers cluster along last year's stems in spring on both sexes. Only a female sets the round red berries, a quarter inch across, which ripen in fall and hang on through winter.

How it grows

A shrub that suckers

Left alone, yaupon is a many-stemmed shrub or small tree of 15 to 25 feet, and it sends up shoots from its roots, so one plant becomes a thicket. A skirt of young stems around the base is the plant's own doing.

Broad tolerance

Sun or shade, wet or dry, sand or clay, salt spray: it takes them all, which is why it grows in so many Florida habitats. Nurseries raise cuttings from known females so a buyer gets berries.

Take care

Berries are for birds

The red berries are not for eating; they upset the stomach of people and dogs. Leave them to the birds.

The leaves are the tea

The tea is made from properly identified, dried and roasted leaves, not from the berries and not from any other holly.
