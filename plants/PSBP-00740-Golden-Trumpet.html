Golden Trumpet

Allamanda cathartica

At a glance

  • Big yellow trumpets. Each flower opens into five broad lobes, several inches across, against glossy leaves on a plant that can sit like a shrub or scramble like a vine.
  • Count the leaves. Leathery leaves often come in whorls of four around the stem, a good first check before you look at anything else.
  • A name that warns you. Cathartica means purgative, and every part of the plant is poisonous to eat.
  • A prickly fruit, if you ever find one. The capsule is spiny and splits open to release flat, winged seeds.

Cultural significance

A warning carried in the name

The species name, cathartica, refers to a purgative effect. Its leaves and roots have been used in traditional medicine to bring on bowel movements or vomiting. That history explains the name. It is not a reason to taste it.

Where to find it here

Along the fence line by the nursery, between the nursery gate and the back gate, where it climbs the fence. Another stands up as a shrub in the hibiscus garden. Neither is one of those big, sprawling allamandas you see in gardens around town. They are just there, flowering now and then, and most people walk past without noticing. Around here it usually just goes by allamanda.

Where it comes from

Tropical America. People have grown it in warm gardens around the world for so long that it is a familiar sight far from home.

What it does

Nectar made deep inside

The broad yellow mouth narrows into a tube, and the reward is made farther in. An anatomical study found a five-lobed gland wrapped around the base of the flower's ovary, fed by the same tissues that carry water and sugar through the plant. The nectar seeps out through microscopic pores, and it holds both glucose and fructose.

How to know it

Flowers at the tips

Flower clusters form at the ends of the shoots and where leaves meet the stem. Each trumpet opens into five broad, overlapping lobes.

Then check the leaves

The leaves are leathery and glossy, often in whorls of four, and the stems run with milky sap. Yellow Trumpet Flower, which also grows in the park, has yellow trumpets too, but its leaves are divided into toothed leaflets. Golden Trumpet's are simple and smooth-edged.

Shrub or vine? Yes.

It has long woody stems but no tendrils, and nothing that clings. You can see both here: in the hibiscus garden it stands up as a shrub, and on the nursery fence it climbs like a vine. That's why the heading on this page says both.

How it grows

Pollen is not enough

Flowers do not guarantee seed. In one study the pollen landed on the stigma before the flower even opened, yet the pollen tubes failed to grow properly and the flowers withered without setting fruit. Extracts from the stigma also held back pollen-tube growth in a dish. Some cultivated forms need pollen from a different plant as well. So a flower can be pollinated without ever being fertilized.

Another way to a new plant

Growers start new plants from stem cuttings, which keeps a garden form true without depending on seed. It likes warmth, moisture and good drainage, and grows vigorously when it gets them.

Take care

Do not eat any part of the plant: it can cause nausea, diarrhea and swollen lips. The milky sap from broken stems can irritate skin and eyes.
