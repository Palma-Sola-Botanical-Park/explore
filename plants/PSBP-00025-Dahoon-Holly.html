Dahoon Holly

Ilex cassine

At a glance

  • Only the female trees carry berries, and only if there is a male somewhere within about fifty feet to pollinate her. A lone holly can stand for decades and never fruit.
  • The berries that would make you ill are perfectly safe for birds. It is a difference in digestive chemistry - what deters a mammal means nothing to a robin.
  • It fruits in winter, when almost nothing else here is fruiting. That timing is the whole point of the tree as far as the birds are concerned.
  • It will stand in ground that stays wet for weeks, which kills most landscape trees.

Cultural significance

The holly on the Christmas card, except ours

The glossy leaves and red berries people associate with Christmas belong to English holly, which will not grow here. Dahoon is the Florida native that does the same job - same look, native range from the southeastern states down into Mexico and Cuba, and it will do it with its feet in standing water.

Where it comes from

Wet ground in the Southeast

Native to the coastal plain of the southeastern United States and south into Mexico and Cuba. In Florida it turns up in swamps, wet hammocks and pond edges, often alongside Bald Cypress and Red Maple - the company it keeps tells you what it wants.

Where to find it here

By the front pond, near the nursery

It is on the left side of the front pond, to the left of the benches, over toward the nursery. You will not spot it by looking for something striking. Look for a modest evergreen with narrow glossy leaves, standing where the ground stays damp.

What it does here

Nobody stops for it

This is not a plant that turns heads, and as far as we know no visitor has ever remarked on it. It earns its place in a different way. In the coldest, leanest weeks of the year, when the flowering is over and the fruit is gone from nearly everything else, a female Dahoon is still hung with red berries - and the birds know it.

Is ours a male or a female?

Honestly, we do not know. The only way to tell a Dahoon apart is to catch it fruiting, and nobody here has recorded berries on this tree. If you walk past in autumn or winter and find it carrying red fruit, that settles it - please tell us. If it never does, that is an answer too.

How to know it

Narrow glossy leaves

Evergreen, two to three inches long, leathery, dark and shining above and paler underneath, with a few small teeth near the tip and often none at all. Not the spiny holly of the Christmas card.

Red berries, if it is a she

Quarter-inch red drupes in dense clusters along the branches, ripening in autumn and hanging on through winter. Occasionally orange or yellow instead.

Flowers you will walk past

Small and white, four or five petals, in spring. Easy to miss entirely, and worth a look because native bees are all over them.

How it grows

Wet feet, no complaints

It grows naturally in moist to wet ground and will take periodic flooding that would finish most ornamental trees. Twenty to thirty feet in time, on an open irregular crown.

Male and female are separate trees

There is no such thing as a self-sufficient holly. Insects carry the pollen, so the two have to be planted within range of each other or the female simply never sets fruit.

Take care

The berries are for the birds

Bright red fruit at child height is a combination worth knowing about. Eaten, the berries cause nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea in people and the same in dogs. It is a minor upset rather than a danger in small amounts, but the colour is an invitation and children and dogs both accept it. The birds, meanwhile, are fine.
