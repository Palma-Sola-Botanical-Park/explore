Copperleaf

Acalypha wilkesiana
On this page

At a glance

  • Nobody has found a wild copperleaf. It was first collected from island gardens in the South Pacific, already a cultivated plant.
  • Its flowers have no petals. The wind carries the pollen, so they have no need to look like anything.
  • In laboratory tests, extracts of its leaves have slowed MRSA, the antibiotic-resistant staph.
  • Ours is the cream-and-green form called Java White. Copper is only one of the colors this species comes in.

Cultural significance

A name from a voyage

The Swiss botanist Johannes Müller described the species in 1866 from a plant collected in Fiji by the United States Exploring Expedition, and named it for the expedition's commander, Charles Wilkes. Between 1838 and 1842 his ships mapped about 1,500 miles of Antarctic coast, still called Wilkes Land, and brought home some 50,000 pressed plants that became the start of the national herbarium at the Smithsonian.

A coat of many colors

Gardeners have selected dozens of forms: bronze, red, pink, yellow, mottled, ruffled. Its old common names say the same thing: Jacob's coat, fire-dragon, beefsteak plant and match-me-if-you-can.

Folk remedy, lab result

In Nigeria and Gabon, brews of the leaves are a traditional remedy for high blood pressure and fungal skin infections. In one laboratory study, an extract made MRSA vulnerable to ampicillin again. These are test-tube results, not a treatment.

Where it comes from

Known only from gardens

It comes from Melanesia, the islands that run from New Guinea's Bismarck Archipelago to Fiji and Vanuatu. Every early collection came from village gardens, and no one has found a plant that is clearly wild, so its true home is a guess.

Relatives here

It belongs to the spurge family, along with our Garden Croton, Peregrina and Coral Plant. Its closest relative is the chenille plant, so close that the two can cross, which is rare in its genus.

What it does here

Color all year

The color is in the leaves, so the shrub looks much the same in every season. Cream, lime and dark green run through each blade in a different pattern, so the show doesn't wait for flowers.

A cutting of a cutting

Gardeners keep named forms like Java White going from cuttings and air layers, so each shrub is a piece of an older one, passed from garden to garden.

How to know it

Match me if you can

Choose any leaf and try to find another with the same pattern. The cream, lime and green are splashed and speckled differently on every blade, which is how the plant earned the name match-me-if-you-can.

A toothed, nettle-like leaf

Leaves are broad ovals to hearts, four to eight inches long, with small teeth all around the edge. The genus name, Acalypha, comes from an old Greek word for nettle, whose leaves are toothed the same way.

Not a croton

Garden Croton, which colors beds all over the park, is in the same family. Feel the difference: a croton leaf is thick, glossy and smooth-edged; a copperleaf leaf is thinner and softer, with a toothed edge.

Look for the tassels

Male flowers hang in long, thin tassels, up to about ten inches. The female spikes are short and upright, wrapped in toothed green bracts that later cup the seed capsules; the spikes rising between the leaves in our top photo are female. Its cousin the chenille plant flips this: its long red tassels are female, and no male chenille plant has ever been found, so every one is a cutting.

How it grows

Sun brings out the color

Leaf color is strongest in bright light. In shade, new leaves come out greener and the shrub grows looser.

Green shoots

Some shoots on ours carry plain green leaves. Variegated shrubs often throw shoots like that; with more chlorophyll they grow faster and can crowd out the pale growth unless they are cut back. Shade can green up new leaves too, so which is happening on ours isn't settled.

Warmth and water

It grows fast in frost-free weather and looks best with regular water. Frost burns it back, and the damaged stems sprout again when it warms up. The species can reach eight to twelve feet.

Take care

Sap and pets

Don't eat it. Pets that chew the leaves may drool, vomit or get diarrhea, and the sap can irritate skin.
