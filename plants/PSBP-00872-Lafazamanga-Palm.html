Lafazamanga Palm

At a glance

  • Made in a garden. It is a hybrid that arose by accident among palms grown side by side in Hawai'i, and botanists named it only in 2025.
  • A one-plant mother. Its mother, a Madagascar palm now called Chrysalidocarpus andersenii, is known from a single plant in a Hawaiian garden. A storm in 2014 took three of the original four.
  • A familiar father, probably. A hedge of Areca Palms grew nearby, and the hybrid looks more like the Areca than like its mother.
  • The name came first. Growers called it lafazamanga, its mother's Madagascar name, long before botanists described it, and the botanists kept that name on purpose.

Cultural significance

Named in 2025

In the late 1990s a palm collector received seed of an unnamed palm from Madagascar. The seedlings he grew in California all died, but four planted out on the Big Island of Hawai'i survived, and seed from them, pollinated it seems by the Areca hedge nearby, grew into something new. In 2025 the botanists Donald Hodel and Robert Burtscher described both the mother and this hybrid.

Where to find it here

In the Shade Palms

Look for it in the Shade Palms. It is not a big palm, but it is well settled and filling out into one of the more substantial plants in that part of the collection.

Where it comes from

Nowhere in the wild. It began in a Hawaiian garden, from a mother whose seed came from Madagascar. Lafazamanga is the Malagasy name for that mother, probably built from lafaza, a name used there for several of these palms, the Areca among them.

What it does

Visit the family

The Areca Palm, its likely father's species, grows on Welcome Island. Look at both. They share the arching leaves and yellowish stalks, but the Areca makes a dense clump of many stems, while this one keeps to a few.

A small test

In southern California it grows with real hybrid vigor and outpaces the Areca. The botanists who named it did not know how it does in Florida, so ours is a small test of that.

How to know it

Like an Areca, but not quite

Arching leaves, leaflets rising in a V, yellow-green stalks and a pale crownshaft on older stems: it could pass for an Areca. Two things separate them. The bare stalk below its leaflets is longer, roughly one and a half to three feet against the Areca's eight to fifteen inches, and it carries fewer leaflets, 31 to 40 down each side against the Areca's 45 to 59. Count one side of a full leaf and see.

How it grows

A slender, sparsely clustered palm, usually two to five stems, to about 20 feet. When it flowers, the new flower cluster splits the top of its wrapper as cleanly as if it had been cut with shears; the botanists who named it could not say how. In California its fruit clusters grew heavy enough that gardeners propped them up to keep them from tearing off.
