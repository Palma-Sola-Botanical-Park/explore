At a glance

  • The only palm native to mainland Europe, and the only species in its genus. Wild stands run around the western Mediterranean from Spain and Morocco to the Ligurian coast near Genoa, about as far north as any palm grows on its own.
  • Not a column but a clump. It sends up several short stems from the base and slowly builds a dense, shrub-sized palm.
  • Ours grows on the west bank of the Big Pond, on the water side of the walk, between the Queen Palm and the Old Man Palm.
  • In its home range one species of weevil does most of the pollinating and raises its young inside the palm's male flower stalks. That weevil is not in Florida; here the palm gets by with wind and whatever insects call.

Cultural significance

Hair palm

In North Africa the shredded leaves were dried and spun into a coarse fiber sold as vegetable horsehair, stuffed into mattresses and upholstery through the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. That trade is the source of the name Hair Palm.

Palmito

In Spain the young fans are still cut, bleached and woven into hats, baskets and brooms, and the shoots of the wild plants have been eaten as a wild vegetable, which is the meaning of palmito. Wild stands are protected in much of that range now.

Where it comes from

The western Mediterranean

Native to southern Portugal and Spain, the Balearic Islands, Sardinia, Sicily, southern Italy and Malta, and across the water in Morocco, Algeria and Tunisia. It grows on dry hillsides, in coastal scrub and on rocky ground, often as a knee-high shrub where the soil is poor.

A Moroccan cousin

A silvery form from the Atlas Mountains of Morocco grows up to about five thousand feet, where a heavy coat of wax against the mountain sun gives the leaves their blue-grey color. It is the form sold as a blue fan palm, and a little hardier than the green.

Where to find it here

Big Pond, west bank

Walk the west side of the Big Pond. The palm is on the pond side of the walk: a low, many-stemmed clump of small stiff fans, about waist high, with the water right behind it. The Old Man Palm is the next palm along the bank to the north, the Queen Palm the next to the south.

What it does here

A palm from a different climate

Most of the palms around the Big Pond come from the wet tropics. This one comes from dry summers and cool, wet winters, and it takes cold that would kill most of its neighbors. It manages Florida's wet summers anyway.

On the bank

It is one of the row of palms planted along the west side of the pond to frame the walk, a low clump holding the bank between two taller palms.

How to know it

Think clump, not column

Several short trunks rise together, each wrapped in brown fiber, making a broad low mound rather than a tree. A single-trunked one is a gardener's doing.

Small, stiff fans

Each leaf is a fan about two feet across, split nearly to the stalk into narrow, stiff segments that hold their shape in the wind. The color runs from green to grey-green.

Teeth on the stalk

Rows of sharp, forward-pointing teeth run along every leaf stalk. Look; do not run a hand along one.

Two sexes

Male and female flowers usually grow on separate plants, so which this one is stays unknown until it blooms: small yellow clusters low in the crown, in spring.

How it grows

Slow and compact

Eight to fifteen feet tall in a Florida garden, growing four to six inches a year, and often much smaller on poor ground in the wild.

Sun, drought, most soils

Once established it takes full sun, drought and poor soil without complaint. Standing water in the rainy season is the greater risk.

A weevil for a partner

At home, adult weevils shelter and feed in the flower clusters, the females lay their eggs in the male flower stalks, the larvae grow inside them, and the new adults leave dusted in pollen for the next palm. It is the arrangement figs have with their wasps, played out with a beetle.

Suckers

New stems come up from the base year after year. Left alone it becomes a thicket; cut back to one stem it can be grown as a single small trunk.

Take care

The leaf stalks

Rows of stiff, sharp teeth run along every leaf stalk and will puncture skin. Keep hands, and children's hands, off the stalks.

Not for tasting

Around the Mediterranean the young shoots, the bud and the fruit have all been eaten in lean times, but cutting the bud kills a stem and the fruit is bitter. Nothing on this palm is for sampling. It is not poisonous to people or pets.
