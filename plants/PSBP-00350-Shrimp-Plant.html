Shrimp Plant

Justicia brandegeeana
On this page

At a glance

  • The colorful 'shrimp' is a stack of overlapping bracts, which are modified leaves. The real flowers are small white tubes that last only a few days. The bracts around them can stay colorful for months.
  • Hummingbirds and butterflies come for the nectar in the little white flowers.
  • A frost can kill the stems to the ground, yet an established plant usually sends up new growth when warm weather returns.

Cultural significance

A name that changed

Old garden books call this plant Beloperone guttata, the name the botanist Townshend Brandegee gave it in 1912. In 1969 botanists moved it into the genus Justicia and renamed it Justicia brandegeeana, for Brandegee, because the name Justicia guttata was already taken by another plant. Both names mean the same shrub. The genus name Justicia honors James Justice, an eighteenth-century Scottish gardener.

Where it comes from

A shrub from Mexico

Shrimp Plant comes from Mexico and is grown in warm gardens around the world.

What it does here

From a few steps away

The curved spikes are easy to recognize from a few steps away: overlapping salmon scales hanging from slender arching stems, against plain green leaves.

Color most of the year

In a mild climate Shrimp Plant can bloom nearly all year, so a spike is rarely far off.

How to know it

A loose shrub

Slender, branching stems carry spikes four to six inches long. The oval leaves, one to three inches, grow in opposite pairs. Most plants stay two to three feet tall in a garden, and can reach six.

The little white tube

Each flower opens into two lips, with purple or reddish spots on the lower one. Those spots belong to the flower; the leaves around it are plain green.

Not the golden one

Golden Shrimp Plant is a related shrub with yellow bracts. This one's are salmon to red-bronze.

How it grows

Sun and soil

Full sun or part shade, in soil that drains. It does not tolerate waterlogging.

Seeds and cuttings

After flowering, a dry capsule less than half an inch long can form, with a few seeds inside. Gardeners also root stem cuttings.

Also known as

Beloperone guttata (former botanical name)
