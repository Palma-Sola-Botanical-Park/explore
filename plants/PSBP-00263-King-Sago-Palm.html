At a glance

  • Not a palm. It is a cycad, a plant that makes cones and seeds, not flowers and fruit, from a group older than the dinosaurs.
  • The name revoluta, rolled back, is on every leaflet: the edges curl under.
  • Each plant is male or female. A male sends up a tall cone; a female grows a woolly crown that carries big orange-red seeds.
  • Every part is poisonous if eaten, the seeds most of all, and it is deadly to dogs.

Cultural significance

Why it is called sago

The pith and seeds hold starch, and in the Ryukyu Islands people in hard times washed and fermented it for days to get the poison out; the 1920s famine in Okinawa is remembered as the cycad hell. The true sago of the grocery shelf comes from an unrelated palm of Southeast Asia. In Japan this plant has been grown in temple and castle gardens for centuries.

Where it comes from

Southern Japan and beyond

King Sago grows wild on rocky coastal slopes in the Ryukyu Islands and southern Kyushu in Japan, in eastern Taiwan and in Fujian on the Chinese mainland. The Swedish botanist Carl Peter Thunberg described it in 1782 from Japan, where it was already a garden plant, and it has become the most planted cycad in the world.

Where to find it here

South end of the Cacti and Succulent Garden

At the south end of the Cacti and Succulent Garden, where the tiers end, level with Cypress Pond. Walking south on the crushed-shell path with the garden on your right, look for it among the other cycads there: the Giant Dioon and the Cardboard Cycad are a few steps away. Beyond this point the Plumeria and the palm row open out on your left.

What it does here

Cycad or palm

This is a good spot to tell a cycad from a palm. Several cycads grow together here, and the palm row begins just beyond. Both have a crown of fronds on a single stem, but a cycad's fronds are stiff and glossy with a hard point on every leaflet, and instead of flowers it makes a cone. Ours is young, still without a trunk, and has not yet shown whether it is male or female.

The Coontie's foreign cousin

Florida has one native cycad, the Coontie, and the park grows it in rows around the ponds and beside the Butterfly Garden, where it is the only food of the Atala butterfly's caterpillars. King Sago feeds nothing here; the two cycads share a shape and a great age and little else.

How to know it

A rigid starburst

Stiff glossy fronds radiate from one crown in a symmetrical rosette, two to three feet long, each with narrow leaflets in pairs along the stalk.

Check one leaflet

Pick out one leaflet: leathery, sharply pointed, and rolled under along both edges. That curl is what the species name means.

Male or female

In spring a male grows a tall yellow-brown cone, a foot or more high, that warms and gives off a scent when it sheds pollen. A female grows a broad woolly cluster of leaf-like scales at the crown with orange-red seeds an inch across along their edges.

Later, a trunk

An older plant has a trunk wrapped in the armour of old leaf bases, and often pups at its foot.

How it grows

Slow means decades

A trunk grows an inch or two a year, so a King Sago several feet tall has been at it for decades. New fronds come all at once, once or twice a year, unrolling together and hardening over a few weeks.

Wind, beetles and roots

In Japan a study on Yonaguni Island found that a female next to a male gets pollen on the wind, and that small beetles carry it to females farther away. Under the soil, short upturned roots near the surface house bacteria that fix nitrogen, a partnership as old as the cycads.

Sun and drainage

Full sun to light shade and sharp drainage suit it; standing water rots the roots. UF/IFAS does not rate it a problem species in Florida.

Take care

Poison, especially the seeds

All of it is poisonous, and the seeds carry the most. Eating any part can cause severe vomiting and liver damage. For dogs it is often fatal even with treatment, and a single seed is enough; a dog that has chewed it needs a vet at once.

Sharp tips

The leaflet tips are hard and sharp. Look closely, but keep bare skin out of the fronds.
