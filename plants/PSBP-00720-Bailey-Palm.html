Bailey Palm

Copernicia baileyana

At a glance

  • A Cuban fan palm built on a different scale from the rest. A full-grown one carries a smooth, near-white trunk up to two feet across under a great dome of stiff fans.
  • Ours is a seedling: a knee-high spray of fans in a mulch ring, no trunk at all. The column is decades away.
  • Native only to central and eastern Cuba, on open savanna with a long dry season.
  • Named for Liberty Hyde Bailey, the American botanist who studied palms; the genus is named for Copernicus.

Cultural significance

Two names

Liberty Hyde Bailey, the American horticulturist, turned to palms late in a long career and collected in Cuba; the species carries his name. The genus honors Nicolaus Copernicus.

Yarey

In Cuba the fan palms of this genus are the yareyes, and their tough leaves are cut for hats, thatch and rope.

Where it comes from

Cuba

Native to central and eastern Cuba and nowhere else, on open savanna with a long dry season. Cuba has around fifteen species in this genus, all found only on the island; this is among the biggest-trunked of them.

Where to find it here

The palm row south of Cypress Pond

In the row of palms along the Plumeria, south of Cypress Pond, count eleven palms in from the Red Cabbage Palm at the crushed-shell path end. It sits between the High Plateau Coconut Palm and the Buccaneer Palm: a knee-high spray of stiff fans in a mulch ring.

Go ahead and get closer

There is no formal path through this palm collection yet. You are welcome to walk across the open grass to see the palms and read their signs. Watch for low fronds and other plantings as you go.

What it does here

A cousin in the same row

Further along the same row stands the Giant Yarey, the largest of the Cuban fan palms and the one this species is most often confused with. The Giant Yarey is always silver-leaved; Bailey Palm can be green or silver, and ours is green. As the two grow, compare them.

Planted for the long run

Palms like this are planted for the people who will visit in thirty years. Nothing about it looks remarkable yet, which is the reason to know what it is.

How to know it

Right now

A low, stiff spray of fan leaves rising straight from the ground, each fan folded into a shallow V and split into narrow, pointed segments. Nothing about it yet says what it will become.

Teeth

Curved teeth run along the edges of the leaf stalks, even on these young leaves.

One day

A mature palm has a pale grey-white trunk, broad and straight as a column, and a big rounded crown. A strong rib runs out of each stalk into the blade and bends the fan into a shallow cup; the underside is waxy grey.

How it grows

Slow, then steady

Years as a stemless rosette; the trunk comes late. Fed and watered it moves faster than its reputation, and thirty to forty-five feet is the usual size in Florida.

Built for two seasons

The cupped fan sheds the heavy rains of the wet season; the waxy grey underside holds moisture through the dry one.

Flowers and fruit

Big branched clusters of small yellow-white flowers draw bees. At home, birds spread the small black fruit.

Sun and drainage

Full sun and drained ground. Once up, it takes drought.
