Giant Dioon

Dioon spinulosum

At a glance

  • A female's cone can weigh 20 to 30 pounds. It starts upright, then its stalk stretches until the cone hangs below the leaves, often bending the trunk.
  • In the wild it can reach about 50 feet, the second-tallest cycad in the world.
  • In Oaxaca its stony seed coats become buzzing toys, as they already were when a visiting botanist watched children play with them over a century ago.
  • Its wild numbers have fallen by about 70 percent, and it is listed as endangered.

Cultural significance

Found in a flowerpot

The species was named in 1883 from a few small leaves of potted plants, one of them in Yucatán, where it doesn't grow wild. In 1906 the American botanist Charles Chamberlain saw one in a city park in Veracruz and asked the governor to help trace it. Two years later he stepped off the train at Tierra Blanca and found it growing wild, and farther on, beyond Tuxtepec, so much of it that he called the place a Dioon forest.

Tortillas and toys

Chinantec communities in Oaxaca still grind the seeds into flour for tortillas, after a long process that takes the poison out; laboratory rats fed the raw kernel got sick. The hard seed coats, with a hole cut in each end, become zumbadores, buzzing toys.

Two eggs

Dioon means two eggs, for the pair of ovules on each scale of a female cone, the beginnings of two seeds. Spinulosum means with small spines, for the teeth along the leaflets.

Where it comes from

Limestone and rain

It grows wild only in Veracruz and Oaxaca, low down, mostly under a thousand feet, in tropical forest on limestone hills and ravines. Chamberlain photographed plants rooted in bare rock with almost no soil, one exposed root nearly as thick as the trunk. Like other cycads, it keeps nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria in special roots, which helps on soil that thin.

Fewer every year

Farms, ranches and reservoirs have taken much of its forest, and collectors have taken plants. It now survives in only a few places, and international trade in the plants is regulated. In Florida it has been a landscape plant for decades, and it got more popular after an Asian scale insect ravaged the state's sago palms in the late 1990s.

Where to find it here

In the Cycad Area at the south end of the Cacti and Succulent Garden, where the tiers end, level with Cypress Pond. Walking south on the crushed-shell path with the garden on your right, look among the cycads there: the King Sago and the Cardboard Cycad are a few steps away.

What it does here

Three cycads, three leaves

Standing with its neighbors, it is easy to see how different cycads can be. King Sago has narrow, dark, stiff leaflets with the edges rolled under. Cardboard Cycad has thick, blunt, fuzzy ones. Giant Dioon's leaves are the longest of the three, glossy and bright green, with flat leaflets toothed at the edges.

Still close to the ground

Ours hasn't raised a trunk you can see yet; its crown still sits close to the ground. The height, the hanging cones and whether it is male or female are all still to come. Growers call it quick for a cycad, which still means slow, so come back for it over the years.

Its own beetle

In Oaxaca a small beetle lives in the male cones of this one species and no other. It was collected in 1982 and waited forty years for a name, Pharaxonotha woodruffi, after a Florida entomologist. Beetles like it probably carry the pollen to the female cones, as they have been shown to do for other cycads, but for Dioon that is still unproven. It doesn't live here.

How to know it

Go by the arch

Step back and take in the crown: long leaves, five to six and a half feet on a mature plant, rising from the center and arching out, the older ones bending down to the ground. The whole thing is glossy and bright green, lighter than the cycads beside it.

Flat leaflets, small teeth

Each leaf carries dozens of narrow, flat leaflets in rows along a stiff stalk. Each ends in a sharp point, and small teeth run along the edges. Look closely without running a hand along them.

The cones tell male from female

A male makes slender upright cones about a foot and a half long that stay in the crown. A female's cone is one to three feet long and packed with two or three hundred seeds. Without a cone, there is no telling which ours is.

How it grows

A crown at a time

New leaves come all at once, a whole crown together, soft at first and hardening over the following weeks. In the wild a plant may put out one crown a year. Each crown leaves a growth ring in the trunk; cycads were thought to have no growth rings until they were found in dioons.

The scale goes for the cones

The scale insect that ruined so many of Florida's sago palms rarely touches Dioon leaves. When it has been found on this species, it was on the cones.

Take care

Poisonous seeds, sharp leaflets

Every part is poisonous if eaten, the seeds most of all, and cycad seeds damage the liver. A seed or two can kill a dog. The leaflet tips are sharp, so keep bare skin out of the crown.
