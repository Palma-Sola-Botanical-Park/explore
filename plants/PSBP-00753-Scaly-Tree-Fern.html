Scaly Tree Fern

Sphaeropteris cooperi
On this page
At a glanceCultural significanceWhere it comes fromWhere to find it hereWhat it does hereHow to know itHow it growsPhotographs

At a glance

  • There used to be a much bigger tree fern on this spot, outside the office. It was lost. This one is its replacement, and it is still small.
  • Everything new comes from one growing point at the top. Destroy that crown and there is no side shoot waiting to take over.
  • No flowers, no seed. It reproduces by dust-fine spores shed from the undersides of the fronds. In Hawaii, windblown spores have been found more than seven miles from the parent plant.
  • Every frond it drops leaves a pale, coin-shaped scar. The trunk keeps the whole record, in order.

Cultural significance

Named for Sir Daniel Cooper

Published in 1866 and named for a nineteenth-century Australian politician who supported botanical collecting. His surname has followed the fern ever since, through two changes of genus.

Where it comes from

Wet gullies in eastern Australia

Native to the subtropical rainforest of Queensland and northern New South Wales, along stream margins and in sheltered gullies under taller trees.

Here it behaves itself

In Hawaii it does not. Those windblown spores let it spread into native forest there, and it is treated as a serious invasive. Florida does not currently consider it a problem — the same fern, two very different outcomes.

Where to find it here

Outside the office

Still a small plant, so look low rather than up. There was a much larger one on this ground for years, which is the fern most people here remember.

What it does here

A replacement, and a gift

The tree fern that stood outside the office for years was lost. This one went in during August 2026 — a gift from Rhonda Olschefskie, one of the park's photographers, sited by the director. It is young, it is being nursed along by whichever volunteers are here that week, and it is not yet the plant its predecessor was.

Count the coin spots

Every pale oval on the trunk is one frond the fern has finished with and dropped. The scars spiral upward in the order they happened, so the trunk is a running record of the plant's life. On ours there are not many yet, which is the point.

How to know it

Coin spots up the trunk

Clean, pale, oval scars arranged in a spiral — not damage, not disease, just where old fronds let go. Nothing else in the park marks itself like this.

The trunk is not wood

Look closely and it is woolly and russet-brown, packed scales and root fibre rather than bark. What looks like a tree trunk is really an upright fern stem, fibrous all the way through. Best admired with your eyes — the scales and hairs can irritate skin.

Silky fiddleheads

New fronds come up tightly coiled, swathed in straw-coloured silky scales. That is where the scaly in the name comes from, and it is the best thing on the plant to catch at the right moment.

How it grows

Warmth, shade and steady moisture

That is the whole recipe. Given those it can move quickly for a tree fern, adding a foot or more of trunk in a good year.

There is no starting over

It cannot be restarted from a cut section of trunk the way some other tree ferns can. Lose the growing crown and you begin again with another plant — which is exactly what happened here.
