Scaly Tree Fern

Sphaeropteris cooperi
On this page

At a glance

  • The whole plant grows from one point, at the very top. There is no second chance and no side shoot — damage that crown and the fern is finished.
  • It is the fastest-growing tree fern anyone cultivates. In warm, humid weather it can add one to two feet of trunk in a year, which for a tree fern is startling.
  • No flowers, no seed. It reproduces by spores — dust-fine, shed by the million from the undersides of the fronds, and carried by wind for seven miles and more.
  • Ours is young. It replaces a tree fern that stood outside the office for years and was lost.

Cultural significance

A thank-you written in Latin

Ferdinand von Mueller described the species in 1857 and named it for Sir Daniel Cooper, a Sydney politician who had paid for botanical collecting expeditions across Australia. Naming a plant after your funder was how that debt got settled. The genus means globe fern, for the rounded clusters of spores under the fronds.

Where it comes from

Wet gullies in eastern Australia

Native to the subtropical rainforest of Queensland and New South Wales, where it grows along stream margins and in sheltered gullies beneath taller trees. It is common there, and common enough in cultivation that it has become a nuisance in Hawaii and New Zealand — though it has never behaved that way in Florida.

Where to find it here

Outside the office

Still a small plant, so look low rather than up. There was a much larger one on this ground for years, which is the fern most people here remember.

What it does here

A replacement, and a gift

The tree fern that stood outside the office for years was lost. This one went in during August 2026 — a gift from Rhonda Olschefskie, one of the park's photographers, sited by the director. It is young, it is being nursed along by whoever is on that week, and it is not yet the plant its predecessor was.

Count the coin spots

Every pale oval on the trunk is one frond the fern has finished with and dropped. The scars spiral upward in the order they happened, so the trunk is a running record of the plant's life. On ours there are not many yet, which is the point.

How to know it

Coin spots up the trunk

Clean, pale, oval scars arranged in a spiral — not damage, not disease, just where old fronds let go. Nothing else in the park marks itself like this.

The trunk is not wood

Run a hand over it and it is woolly and russet-brown, packed scales and root fibre rather than bark. Underneath, what looks like a trunk is really an upright stem, fibrous all the way through.

Silky fiddleheads

New fronds come up tightly coiled, swathed in straw-coloured silky scales. That is where the scaly in the name comes from, and it is the best thing on the plant to catch at the right moment.

How it grows

Fast, and impossible to replace piecemeal

It wants warmth, humidity and shelter, and given those it climbs quickly. But with one growing point and no woody tissue it cannot be taken from cuttings the way other plants can — there is only ever the one of it, which is why a lost tree fern means starting again from a new plant.
