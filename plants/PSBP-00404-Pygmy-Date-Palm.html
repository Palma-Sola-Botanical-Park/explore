Pygmy Date Palm

Phoenix roebelenii
On this page

At a glance

  • The most planted small palm in Florida, and almost nobody knows it comes from cliffs and riverbanks along the Mekong, where the floods come up over it every year.
  • It is a date palm, but the fruit is nearly all seed with a thin skin of sweet flesh. The grocery-store date is a different and much bigger tree.
  • Ours is three palms, not one. A multi-trunk pygmy date is almost always several seedlings planted together, and the trunks lean apart as they grow.
  • The lowest leaflets on each frond are spines, two or three inches long. Every date palm does this. Look before you reach in.

Where it comes from

The Mekong and its neighbours

Southern China, Laos, Thailand and Vietnam, along rivers, on rocky banks and cliff faces that go under water in the wet season. It was brought to Europe in the 1880s by Carl Roebelen, an orchid hunter working for an English nursery, and the name is his, usually misspelled on the tag.

What it does here

Three at once, and let alone

The familiar entryway palm let grow: three trunks in the open, arching apart, instead of clipped tight beside a front door. Male and female flowers grow on separate palms, so fruit appears only on a female with a male in reach. If ours ever fruits, at least one of the three is a she.

How to know it

Small, slim and knobby

A slender trunk, often a little crooked, knobby with old leaf bases, topped by a soft, fine crown of arching fronds. About twelve feet at most.

Spines at the base of every frond

The first few leaflets at the base of each frond are stiff spines. That is the feature to find before you do anything else close to the crown.

Fruit, and root nubs

Small dark fruit in autumn, on females only. On older palms a mass of stubby aerial root nubs shows at the base of the trunk.

How it grows

Slowly, and it likes water, which is the river in it. Sun or part shade, any soil that drains. Bradenton is near the cold edge of its range and a hard freeze browns the fronds. On our sand it goes hungry for potassium, which shows as orange, dying tips on the oldest fronds; the cure is feeding, not cutting them off, which only speeds the decline.

Take care

The fruit is harmless and not worth the trouble. Mind the spines. And do not confuse this palm with the Sago, a look-alike cycad whose seeds are seriously poisonous.
