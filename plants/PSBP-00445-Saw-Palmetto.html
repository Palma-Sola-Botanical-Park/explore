Saw Palmetto

Serenoa repens

At a glance

  • The saw is on the stalk: sharp teeth line the edges between the trunk and the fan.
  • Its trunk usually creeps along the ground, so a patch of crowns may be one plant.
  • We have two looks: the everyday green one, and a silver-blue form that is common on Florida's east coast.
  • Black bears eat the fruit, and two skipper butterflies raise their caterpillars on the leaves.
  • Fire can burn off every leaf, and the palm grows back from a growing point the flames never reach.

Cultural significance

A honey of its own

In spring, bees work the small, fragrant flowers. The honey they make from them, saw palmetto honey, is a prized Florida honey.

Where it comes from

Saw Palmetto is native to the southeastern United States, with Florida at the center of its range. It grows in places as different as pine flatwoods, dry scrub and the coastal strand, and it shrugs off salt.

Where to find it here

The green one

Our green Saw Palmetto grows in the Natives, a stand of pines, oaks, Green Buttonwoods and other native trees, open enough to let the sun through.

The silver one

The silver form is somewhere else entirely. It grows as underbrush in the shade of the cypress trees between the Plumeria patch and the Serenity Garden.

What it does

Food for many

The berries are an important food for Florida black bears and for many other mammals and birds. Palmetto Skipper and Monk Skipper caterpillars feed on the leaves, and bees and other insects visit the flowers.

How to know it

Palmate, not costapalmate

Saw Palmetto's leaf is palmate: the stalk stops where the fan begins, and every segment spreads from that one point. The Sabal palmettos, such as Florida's state tree, the Cabbage Palm, are costapalmate: the stalk runs on into the fan as a curved midrib. Add the teeth on a Saw Palmetto's stalk, where a Sabal's is smooth, and you can tell them apart in seconds.

Low and creeping

Look below the fans for a trunk that creeps at or near ground level. Some stems turn upward, so a taller plant can still be a Saw Palmetto, but most stay five to ten feet tall.

Green or silver

Most Saw Palmettos are green, but the color varies, and silver to blue-silver forms are common on Florida's east coast. Put our two side by side in your mind: same palm, same teeth, a different coat.

Flowers and fruit

In spring, stalks about three feet long carry clouds of small, yellow-white flowers. The berries start out yellow and ripen black from August through October.

How it grows

Protected at the center

New leaves come from a growing point wrapped in older leaf bases, and those layers insulate it when fire sweeps through. Reserves stored underground then fuel the new growth. Fire can even increase flowering, although the response depends on light, plant size and when the burn happens.

Older than any stem

On Florida's Lake Wales Ridge, DNA tests grouped separate stems into clones, and models of their slow spread put some of those clones at thousands of years old. The age belongs to the clone, not to any one stem: leaves and stems come and go as the plant creeps on.

Take care

Avoid reaching between the saw-toothed leaf stalks, which can cut skin.
