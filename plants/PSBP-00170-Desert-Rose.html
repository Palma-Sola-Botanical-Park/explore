Desert Rose

Adenium obesum
On this page

At a glance

  • Those fat grey branches are a water tank. It is the same solution the Queensland Bottle Tree is working on a few steps away, at a fraction of the size and on a much faster clock.
  • It flowers hard right through the summer, in pink and rose with a pale throat, then drops its leaves and spends the winter looking dead. It is not.
  • Nothing on this plant is safe to eat, and the milky sap will irritate skin. Keep dogs away from it.
  • In Thailand and across Southeast Asia it is a serious collector's plant, bred into hundreds of named forms.

Cultural significance

Poison for big game

The Hadza of Tanzania have long boiled the sap down into arrow poison and used it on large game. The chemistry is the same thing that makes it a plant to keep children and dogs away from in a garden: cardiac glycosides, strong enough to stop a heart.

A collector's plant

In Thailand and neighbouring countries it has become a bonsai obsession. Hundreds of named cultivars have been bred for flower colour and for the shape of the swollen trunk, and a prize plant can change hands for hundreds of dollars.

Named for a city

Adenium comes from Aden, the Yemeni port near where it was first collected. Obesum simply means fat, for the trunk.

Where it comes from

Dry Africa and Arabia

It grows in an arc of dry country from Senegal and Mauritania east across the Sahel to Ethiopia, Somalia, Kenya and Tanzania, and north into Saudi Arabia, Oman and Yemen. Rocky slopes, sandy savanna, poor soil, hard sun and almost no summer rain.

Where to find it here

At the foot of the block wall

In the cactus and succulent garden, low down against the block retaining wall, within a few steps of the Queensland Bottle Tree on the same wall. It sprawls rather than stands, so look down rather than up.

What it does here

The colour in a garden of shapes

The cactus and succulent garden runs mostly on form and texture — grey, green, spines, ribs. This is one of the few things in it that does colour, and it does it for most of the summer. In a bed built around architecture, it is the plant doing the decorating.

How to know it

Trumpet flowers with a pale throat

Five broad petals, deep pink at the rim and fading to near-white down the funnel. They come in clusters at the ends of the branches, several open at once.

Fat branches, few leaves

Smooth grey-green limbs, noticeably swollen and splaying outward, with the leaves bunched only at the tips. For much of the year there is far more branch on show than foliage.

Compare with the Bottle Tree

Both are storing water in the trunk, and they are not related in the slightest — this one sits in the same family as Oleander and Plumeria. Two different corners of the plant world arriving at the same answer to the same problem.

How it grows

Slow, and it sits out the winter

Growth is slow. In cool weather it drops its leaves and stops, which looks alarming and is not. It leafs out again as things warm up and is usually back in flower by late March or April.

At the edge of what it will take

This is a plant for frost-free ground, and Bradenton is half a step colder than the range it is rated for. A hard freeze will strip it, and a bad one can kill it outright.

Take care

All of it, not just the sap

Every part of this plant is toxic — sap, leaves, stems, roots and flowers alike. The milky sap can irritate skin and blister it on people who react badly. It is dangerous to dogs, cats and horses, and licking the sap is enough to cause trouble, so do not let an animal mouth it.
