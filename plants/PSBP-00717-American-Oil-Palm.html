At a glance

  • American Oil Palm is native from southern Mexico through Central America into northern and western South America, plus Trinidad and Tobago.
  • Older literature moved the species through names including Cocos butyracea and Scheelea butyracea before settling on Attalea butyracea.
  • The fruit has edible pulp around a very hard stone containing oil-rich seeds.
  • A mature palm carries a huge crown of long arching feather leaves on one solitary trunk — ours is still young and has a long way to go.

Cultural significance

A many-use palm

Across its native range, people have used the fruit, seed oil, leaves, fibers, hard endocarp and sap. The exact use varies by region, but the palm has served as food, roofing material, fiber source, fuel and a source of oil.

Palm wine can cost the palm

Some traditional sap-harvesting methods involve cutting into or felling the stem. On a solitary palm, removal of the growing point is fatal because there is no replacement trunk.

Where it comes from

Southern Mexico to Bolivia

Its native range runs from southern Mexico to Bolivia and Trinidad and Tobago, including much of Central America and the northern half of South America.

Where to find it here

Right at the front entrance

Immediately on your left as you drive in the front entrance, part of the row of palms planted along the fence on 17th Avenue NW. It's still young — a modest crown of leaves rather than the towering palm this species eventually becomes.

What it does here

Compare two “oil palms”

African Oil Palm and American Oil Palm both produce useful oily fruits, but they belong to different genera and have very different histories in global agriculture. African Oil Palm became the major industrial crop; this species remains primarily a regional multipurpose palm.

How to know it

Start with scale

The crown is broad and built from very long arching pinnate leaves, at full size.

One heavy trunk

Mature plants are solitary, with persistent leaf-base remains often visible higher on the stem.

Fruit in large hanging clusters

The oval fruits ripen yellow-orange to brownish tones around a hard inner stone.

Look for the flower bracts

Thick woody bracts enclose developing inflorescences and may remain visible after splitting.

How it grows

A wet-tropical American palm

It grows primarily in wet tropical vegetation, although it also occurs in disturbed and seasonally flooded landscapes.

A long juvenile stage

This genus can spend years with little visible trunk before the stem rises above the leaf bases. A young plant, like the park's, looks very different from the towering adult form.

One growing point

Because the palm is solitary, destructive harvest of the heart or terminal bud ends the plant rather than leaving a clump to recover.
