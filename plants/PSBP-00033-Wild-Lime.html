Wild Lime

Zanthoxylum fagara

At a glance

  • Wild Lime is a native member of the citrus family. Crush a leaf and the family resemblance is obvious: it smells distinctly of citrus.
  • Giant Swallowtails lay their eggs on Wild Lime and other members of the citrus family. Their caterpillars eat the leaves before becoming one of our largest butterflies, with a wingspan that can reach five and a half inches.
  • Look closely for a caterpillar that resembles a bird dropping. That disguise is part of the Giant Swallowtail's defense against predators.

Where it comes from

A Florida citrus relative

Wild Lime is native to central and southern Florida and extends through the Caribbean, Mexico and parts of tropical America. In Florida it grows naturally in hammocks, scrub and other well-drained wooded habitats.

Where to find it here

Front of the park, and the back

From the entrance, turn left and follow the path along the fence. Wild Lime is part of the shade on your right as you look toward the gazebo by the pavilion. Snake Plants and Crotons grow beneath its canopy. A second Wild Lime grows at the back of the park in the Natives area, near the palms and the Plumeria.

When

Wild Lime stays green through the year. Its tiny yellow-green flowers are easy to overlook; the leaves, hooked thorns and citrus scent are useful identification clues in any season.

What it does here

Making the shade

Our Wild Lime is substantial enough to help form the canopy over this part of the path. Snake Plants, Crotons and other plants grow in the shade beneath it.

A place to start a butterfly

Giant Swallowtails use Wild Lime as a larval host plant. Females lay eggs on the leaves, and the caterpillars that hatch feed on them. The adult butterfly feeds elsewhere; the Wild Lime's particular role is helping raise the next generation.

Look for the bird dropping

Giant Swallowtail caterpillars are mottled brown and cream and resemble bird droppings. If you see something suspicious on our Wild Lime leaves, look twice.

How to know it

Smell a leaf

Gently crush a leaf and smell it. The sharp citrus scent is one of the quickest clues that Wild Lime belongs to the same plant family as oranges, lemons and limes.

Hooked spines

Look along the stems for short, sharp spines, often slightly curved. Don't use your fingers to find them.

Follow the leaf stem

Each leaf is divided into several small leaflets. The central stalk has narrow green wings running between them, a useful identification feature.

Look for something eating it

Eggs or bird-dropping-like Giant Swallowtail caterpillars on the leaves are another clue that you've found one of their citrus-family host plants.

How it grows

Shrub or small tree

Wild Lime can grow as a dense shrub or develop into a substantial small tree, and we have one of each. The plant in the Natives area at the back of the park is still more of a shrub. The one by the pavilion gazebo near the front has grown large enough to help shade the path and the plants beneath it.

At home in drier ground

Unlike the plants along the pond edges, Wild Lime prefers well-drained ground. Once established it handles dry conditions well and grows in sun or partial shade.

Take care

Watch the thorns

Wild Lime branches carry sharp, hooked thorns. Give the plant some room as you pass; our volunteers know from experience how easily they can catch you.

The fruit

The small fruit has been used as food and spice but numbs the mouth. This isn't a plant to sample in the park.
