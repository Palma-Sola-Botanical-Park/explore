Scheffers Palm

At a glance

  • A small, tidy clump of a palm with a very large story: the IUCN lists it as Critically Endangered, and Kew's 2024 account of New Guinea's palms knows it from only two sites, both inside logging concessions.
  • Its wild range is a strip of coastal forest along the north side of New Guinea, from around Jayapura east to Aitape, across the border between Indonesia and Papua New Guinea.
  • Look at the ends of the leaflets. They do not taper to a point; they finish blunt and ragged, as if bitten off.
  • Ours grows in the row south of Cypress Pond, beside the big silver Bismarck Palm.

Where it comes from

Northern New Guinea

Scheffer's Palm is native to central northern New Guinea, in coastal forest near sea level from the neighbourhood of Jayapura, in Indonesian Papua, east to Aitape in Papua New Guinea. It is a wet-forest palm that grows in the shade of taller trees. The botanical name was published in 1935 and is understood to honour Rudolf Scheffer, a director of the botanic garden at Bogor in Java in the 1870s.

Two known sites

The IUCN lists the species as Critically Endangered. Kew's Palms of New Guinea, published in 2024, reports it from only two known sites, both under logging concessions. Nothing about the palm in front of you suggests that; it is one of the plainest-looking palms in the collection.

Where to find it here

Beside the Bismarck Palm

Scheffer's Palm grows in the row of unusual palms just south of our Plumeria collection, on the south side of Cypress Pond, right beside the Bismarck Palm, a young silver fan palm, chest high with no trunk yet. Look for a clump of glossy green feather fronds at knee to head height next to it.

Go ahead and get closer

There is no formal path through this palm collection yet. You are welcome to walk across the open grass to see the palms and read their signs.

What it does here

Easy to walk past

This palm is here because a longtime volunteer collected unusual palms for many years and planted them along this pond, and it is the kind of plant that only makes sense with a sign. We also grow its look-alike, Black-fruit Cluster Palm, in the shaded bed beside the gazebo by the pavilion; the 2024 Kew account says the two are confused with each other, and the surest difference is inside the seed. Walk between the two and see how far you get by eye.

How to know it

Bitten-off leaflets

The leaflets end in a broad, jagged edge instead of a point, as though the tips had been torn or bitten off. Botanists call the shape praemorse. Every palm in this genus has it, and it is the first thing to check.

Thin stems, green sleeves

The stems are slender, no thicker than a wrist, and ringed with old leaf scars, each topped by a smooth green sleeve, the crownshaft, where the leaf bases wrap the stem. Ours has several stems in a tight clump and no bare trunk showing yet.

Flowers and fruit

Small white flowers on a branched stalk below the crownshaft, then fruit that ripen purple-black. Inside each fruit is a seed folded into grooves; the genus name means folded seed. We have not seen flowers or fruit on ours yet.

Compare with Black-fruit Cluster Palm

Black-fruit Cluster Palm, in the shaded bed beside the gazebo by the pavilion, looks very much like this one: same slender clumping stems, same bitten-off leaflets. If you cannot tell them apart from the path, that is a real problem in the botany, not a failure to look.

How it grows

A forest palm in the open

At home it grows in the shade of coastal forest, in ground that is never dry. Ours has the open sky of the palm row, more sun and wind than a wild one would get, and it wants steady water.

One stem or many

The species can grow as one stem or as a clump, so the number of stems does not settle the name. Each stem carries both male and female flowers, so a single plant can set fruit on its own.
