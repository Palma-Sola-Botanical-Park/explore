Madagascar Rubbervine

Cryptostegia madagascariensis

At a glance

  • Shrub or vine. Left alone it mounds into a big, rounded shrub. Give it something to lean on and it climbs much higher.
  • Horns that come in pairs. The seed pods grow in twos, shaped like horns and pointing away from each other. When they split, silky seeds drift off on the wind.
  • Look inside the flower. A small crown of lobes sits at the throat. Here they are whole; on its close cousin, Palay Rubbervine, they are forked.
  • A long way from home. It comes from Madagascar, and in South Florida it has escaped from gardens into the wild.

Cultural significance

The rubber in the name

Break a stem and the sap runs milky. That latex is why it was once planted as a rubber vine. A herbarium note from East Africa records it arriving there as a rubber vine, and only later turning up in coastal gardens as an ornamental shrub.

Where to find it here

You won't have to look hard. Big clumps grow back by the nursery and at the back gate, three or four more stand just outside the nursery, and there is one in the Butterfly Garden.

Where it comes from

Just where the name says: Madagascar. There it grows in dry forest, in savanna and along riverbeds. Its pink-to-purple trumpets carried it into gardens far beyond the island.

What it does

It holds on tight

As a climber it wraps tightly around whatever supports it, and it can hold back the growth of the plants it climbs. In Florida it has escaped gardens into mangrove swamps, hammocks, shell mounds and rockland hammocks, mostly in the southern part of the state, where winters are mild. Cold weather helps keep it in check farther north. Florida frowns on planting it.

Here, it takes work

Left untrimmed, a clump here grows into a huge mound, so the volunteers cut these plants back often; in the Butterfly Garden it is trimmed again and again just to keep room for everything else.

Bees as couriers

In a study of cultivated plants in Brazil, bees were the main insects seen carrying its pollen, packed into small bundles stuck to their mouthparts. In that study the flowers accepted their own pollen but did not pollinate themselves, so the visit matters.

How to know it

Start at the throat

The flower is a five-lobed pink to purple trumpet. Inside, at the throat, a small crown of lobes is the best test: whole in this species, forked in Palay Rubbervine. Color alone will not settle it, because both vary.

Leaves and stems

The leaves sit in opposite pairs, glossy above and duller beneath. Young leaf stalks are greenish, where Palay Rubbervine's can be reddish, and young shoots can be slightly hairy. Older woody stems turn gray-brown and carry pale, warty spots.

How it grows

A shrub until it finds support

On open ground it builds a dense, rounded mound of glossy leaves. Against a fence or a tree it turns climber and keeps going up.

An absolute beast

As a shrub, its roots grip the soil so tightly that pulling one out is extremely hard. As a vine it is worse. Climbing our back gate, it can get out of hand quickly, and then it takes a team with power tools just to make a dent in it.

Male first, then female

In the Brazilian study, each flower worked in two stages: it offered its pollen first and became ready to receive pollen later.

Seeds that fly

The paired pods turn from green to brown before they open. Each seed carries a tuft of silky white hairs that catches the wind, so a new plant can start well away from its parent.

Take care

The plant is poisonous if eaten. Its milky sap can irritate skin, so leave broken stems and leaves alone.
