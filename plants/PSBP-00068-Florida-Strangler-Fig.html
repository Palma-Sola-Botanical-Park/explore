At a glance

  • A Florida Strangler Fig can begin life high in another tree, after a bird or other animal leaves a seed there. Its roots grow downward until they reach the ground.
  • Those roots can thicken and join around the host tree. Eventually the fig may stand on its own, sometimes with an open or hollow-looking center where the host once grew.
  • What looks like a fig fruit is actually a hollow structure with tiny flowers on the inside.
  • Florida Strangler Fig has its own tiny species of fig wasp. The tree and the wasp each depend on the other to reproduce.

Where it comes from

A Florida native

Florida Strangler Fig is native to Florida, the Caribbean and parts of Mexico and Central America. In Florida it grows naturally in hammocks and along the edges of swamps, mostly in the central and southern peninsula. It is one of only two fig species native to the state.

Where to find it here

At the back of the park

Our Florida Strangler Fig grows in the Natives area toward the back of the park. It is a substantial tree, but it is easy to walk past without realizing what it is.

When

The tree keeps its leaves all year. Look for small figs among the leaves; they start golden yellow and turn red to purple as they ripen, and they are most plentiful in spring.

What it does here

A butterfly's tree

In South Florida the caterpillars of the Ruddy Daggerwing, a big orange butterfly with long tails on its hindwings, feed on the leaves of this fig. The butterfly is rare this far north; one was recorded on Anna Maria Island in 2017, and none has yet been seen in the park. If you see one here, tell us.

How to know it

Look at the base

Ours grew up in the open, with no host tree, so it looks like a many-trunked tree rather than a strangler: a dozen stems rising from one spreading base, with roots running over the ground around it. That many-stemmed habit is the same growth that, started high in another tree, would have wrapped and fused around it.

Small figs, not showy flowers

Look for small round figs among the leaves. You won't see ordinary flowers; the flowers are hidden inside the figs themselves.

Thick green leaves

The leaves are simple, leathery and smooth-edged, usually glossy green.

Not the banyan

The spreading form and aerial roots of large figs get them confused with banyans. This is Florida Strangler Fig, Ficus aurea. The classic Banyan, Ficus benghalensis, is a different fig native to the Indian subcontinent.

How it grows

It can start without the ground

A seed carried into the crotch or crevice of another tree can germinate above ground. The young fig lives at first as an epiphyte, sending aerial roots downward. Once those roots reach the soil, the fig has a much larger supply of water and nutrients and can grow quickly.

How a tree becomes a strangler

As the fig grows, its descending roots thicken, branch and can fuse together around the host. The expanding fig competes with the host for light, water and space, and the host may eventually die. The fig can remain standing with an open center where the other tree once was.

A garden inside a fig

Slice open a wild fig and its strange construction makes sense: the flowers face inward, lining a hollow chamber. A female fig wasp, smaller than a grain of rice, enters through a tiny opening, carrying pollen from another fig. She pollinates the flowers and lays her eggs inside, and the next generation carries pollen onward. Florida Strangler Fig has its own species of fig wasp, and neither can reproduce without the other.

Take care

Watch the milky sap

Broken leaves and stems release a white latex that can irritate skin and eyes. Keep the sap out of your eyes and wash it from your skin after contact.
