At a glance

  • Fishtail palms are the only palms whose leaves divide twice. Every small leaflet ends in a ragged, fishtail edge.
  • Each stem flowers just once, starting at the top and working down, and then it dies.
  • In Singapore, civets eat so much of the fruit that their droppings fill up with fishtail seeds.
  • It has gone wild in Manatee County. In 2015 botanists found it growing in the hammock at Emerson Point.

Cultural significance

A working palm

Across Southeast Asia the trunk yields sago starch, the leaves are used for thatch and weaving, and the fluffy fibers on the young leaves make tinder.

Mild by name

Mitis is Latin for mild or gentle, usually read as unarmed: unlike many palms, it has no spines. The Portuguese missionary and botanist João de Loureiro named it in 1790 from plants in Vietnam. The mildness stops at the fruit.

Where it comes from

Southeast Asia, mostly

It grows wild from southern China through Myanmar and Indochina to the Philippines, Borneo, Sumatra and Java, and on the Andaman and Nicobar Islands. Burma is one country of about fifteen in its range; the name just stuck.

Wild in Manatee County

Birds eat the fruit and the seeds pass through unharmed, so it can spread from gardens. In 2015 botanists found it growing wild in the coastal hammock at Emerson Point Preserve, on the Manatee River. The state keeps it on its invasive-plant watch list, and in South Florida growers are asked to keep it from spreading.

What it does here

Civet jam

In its home range, civets and hornbills eat the fruit and carry the seeds away. Common palm civets in Singapore eat so much of it that researchers there nicknamed their droppings blueberry-jam scats. On Narcondam, a small island in the Andaman Sea, a hornbill found nowhere else is its only seed carrier.

Three fishtails

We grow three fishtail palms. This species makes a clump of slender stems, none much thicker than six inches. Giant Fishtail and Mountain Fishtail each raise a single massive trunk, several times as thick.

How to know it

Follow one leaf

Trace a leaf from the stem. The main stalk carries side stalks, and the side stalks carry the leaflets. Young plants start with once-divided leaves and switch to the twice-divided kind as they grow.

Torn at the tip

Each leaflet is a flat wedge, narrow where it joins and ragged across the end, as if it had been torn. That is its normal shape, not insect damage.

A clump, not a column

Several gray, ringed stems rise together, with brown fibrous netting around the leaf bases. Fishtail sheath fiber like this is twisted into rope and bound into brooms.

Flowers in threes

When a stem flowers, long strands hang from the leaf bases, with the flowers in threes: one female between two males. The fruit that follows is round, about half an inch across, ripening red to purplish black.

How it grows

From the top down

A stem grows for years without flowering. Then it opens a flower cluster at the top and works downward, one cluster after another, until the lowest has flowered. Then the stem dies, so a fruiting stem is near its end.

Does the clump live on?

Here the experts disagree. Most references say new suckers from the base keep the clump going after each stem dies. Florida palm specialists say the opposite: every stem in a clump dies within a year or two of flowering, and any young growth is seedlings. Ours has young fishtail leaves coming up between the old stems. When our stems flower, we'll find out.

Sun or shade

It grows in full sun or deep shade, 15 to 25 feet tall here and taller in the wild, and does well indoors in a bright room. It is frost-tender and susceptible to lethal yellowing, the disease that killed so many of Florida's coconut palms.

Take care

Leave the fruit alone

The fruit pulp is full of needle-like calcium oxalate crystals that burn skin, mouth and eyes. Don't handle or eat the fruit, and keep pets away from fallen fruit.
