At a glance

  • The palm of the Victorian parlour. By 1900 there was a Kentia in every grand hotel lobby in Europe, and potted Kentias lined the first-class Palm Court on the Titanic.
  • In the wild it grows on one island in the Tasman Sea, Lord Howe, and nowhere else. Every seed sold in the world comes from there, under a quota the island sets each year.
  • Ours is a youngster, a fountain of fronds at knee height with no trunk yet. Come back in a decade.
  • It became the houseplant it is because it puts up with low light, dry air and neglect better than almost any other palm.

Cultural significance

From an unknown island to every parlour

Lord Howe Island was unknown to Europeans until 1788 and did not export a seed until the 1870s. Within thirty years the Kentia was the palm of every drawing room and conservatory in Europe and North America, and the island has lived on the trade ever since, permit by permit.

Where it comes from

One island, two palms

Lord Howe Island, about four hundred miles east of Australia, in lowland forest and on sandy flats. It shares the island with one close relative, the Belmore Sentry Palm, and the two split into separate species on a patch of land smaller than ten square miles without ever being apart. What keeps them apart is timing: the Kentia flowers some weeks before its cousin, so the pollen never meets.

Where to find it here

In the shady row of collection palms at the north-east tip of the park, beyond the Royal Palms and not far from the front fence. It is the small one between the Dwarf Lady Palm and the Cat Palm, a spray of fronds in a mulch ring.

What it does

The parlour palm let out

A houseplant, outdoors, in Florida. It is not thrilled about our summers, and in full sun and heavy rain it sulks; in a shady, sheltered spot it manages. A Kentia in the ground is a chance to watch the parlour palm become the forty-foot tree it always wanted to be. Ours is at the very beginning of that.

How to know it

Drooping leaflets

Long, arching feather fronds with narrow leaflets that hang rather than stand, so the crown looks relaxed and open.

Smooth stalks

The leaf stalks carry no spines or teeth at all.

A ringed trunk, later

On a grown palm, a slim green trunk marked with neat rings where each old frond fell. On ours, that is still a few years off.

How it grows

Slowly. It wants shade from the afternoon sun, steady moisture and shelter from a hard frost, and it will be fifteen years old before it fruits. When it does, the flowers come in November and December below the fronds, and the egg-shaped fruit takes years more to ripen to a dull red.
