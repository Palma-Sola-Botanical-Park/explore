At a glance

  • On Round Island it is also a nursery: a 2021 study found most of the nests of Günther's gecko, one of the island's rarest reptiles, tucked into this palm.
  • The wild palms hang on the small islands off Mauritius, chiefly Round Island. Goats and rabbits ate them nearly to nothing; since the animals were taken off, they have been coming back.
  • The blue is a mature palm's colour, and a subtle one. Young plants flush red at the leaf stalks, just like their red cousin seven palms down the row.
  • It is Endangered. Male and female flowers grow on separate palms; ours has flowered.

Cultural significance

A nursery's name and an island's word

The species name loddigesii honours the Loddiges family, whose nursery in Hackney, east of London, was one of the great importers of tropical plants in the early 1800s; much of what European glasshouses grew then came through their catalogues. Latania is older than that: it is latanier, the islanders' own word for these palms, carried into botanical Latin.

Where it comes from

Mauritius, and its islets

Blue Latan is native to Mauritius, in the Indian Ocean east of Madagascar. Its wild stands are now confined to small islands off the north coast. Round Island, about fourteen miles offshore and less than a square mile of volcanic rock, holds the most. The IUCN listed the species as Endangered in 1998.

Round Island, recovering

Goats and rabbits released on Round Island stripped it for more than a century. The goats were gone by 1979 and the rabbits by 1986, and the palms and the rest of the island's plants have been regrowing since; the Mauritian Wildlife Foundation reported in 2025 that the latans had regenerated strongly. The palm is habitat as well: a 2021 study of Günther's gecko found 78.8 percent of its nest sites in Blue Latan palms.

Three islands, three latans

The genus has three species, one for each of the Mascarene Islands: Blue Latan on Mauritius, Red Latan on Réunion, Yellow Latan on Rodrigues. All three are on the IUCN's threatened lists, and we grow two of them.

Where to find it here

Along Cypress Pond

Blue Latan Palm grows in the row of unusual palms just south of our Plumeria collection, on the south side of Cypress Pond. Start from the Red Cabbage Palm at the crushed-shell path end: it is a bit short of halfway along, between the Buccaneer Palm and the Licury Palm.

Go ahead and get closer

There is no formal path through this palm collection yet. You are welcome to walk across the open grass to see the palms and read their signs.

What it does

Half of a pair

This palm is the other half of a pair. Its red cousin stands seven palms toward the Red Cabbage Palm end of the row, at about the same age, and the two are all but identical: the same silver fans, the same beaded flower stalks. They are part of a collection of unusual palms that a longtime volunteer planted here over many years, and together they make a point no single palm could: the colours in the names belong to seedlings, and grown palms keep the difference to themselves.

How to know it

Silver fans, no trunk yet

Big, stiff fans with a silver-grey cast, on long stalks, with a rib that runs on into the blade and bows it. Ours has no trunk yet; the crown rises straight from the ground. The trunk, when it comes, will be pale grey and swollen at the base.

Teeth on the stalks

Small teeth run along the edges of the leaf stalks near the base, and they scratch; keep your hands off the stalks. The underside of a mature leaf carries a thin pale wool that adds to the silver.

Compare with the Red Latan Palm

If you find a latan with red on its youngest leaf stalk, you may have the red one. If you find plum-sized brown fruit, you have a female. Neither has turned up on ours yet.

How it grows

Sun and drainage

Round Island is dry, windy and rocky, and this palm wants what it had there: full sun, ground that drains, and no standing water. Bradenton is at the cool edge of its range and a hard winter can mark the fronds.

Separate sexes

A single trunk, no suckers; new palms come only from seed. Each palm is male or female, and only a female with a male within reach of the pollinators sets fruit. Ours has flowered; we have seen no fruit and do not yet know which it is.
