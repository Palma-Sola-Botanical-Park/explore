Date Palm

Phoenix dactylifera

At a glance

  • Look for the silver. The fronds are long, sturdy and arching, in blue-green to gray-green with a silvery cast. That sets this palm apart from many bright-green tropical palms.
  • Male and female flowers grow on separate trees. In date-growing regions, wind and insects carry the pollen between them, and a female needs it to make seeded dates.
  • The male has a say in the harvest. Pollen from different males can change the size of the dates and when they ripen.
  • Only this palm is the date palm. Many palms carry a date-palm name, such as the Canary Island, Senegal and Pygmy date palms, but the species grown for the dates we eat is this one.

Cultural significance

A food plant with a long journey

Date Palms have been grown for food in the Middle East for centuries, and people carried them far beyond that region. Growers choose which male's pollen to use, partly to bring a crop forward or to hold it back until after damaging rain. The effect depends on the female variety too.

Where it comes from

No one knows exactly where wild date palms first grew, because people have planted and moved them for so long. North Africa and the Middle East are central to the story. It is a palm of deserts, yet it grows where its roots can reach water.

Where to find it here

Look for it between the restrooms and the Big Pond, near the entrance to the Rare Fruit Garden. The path there is small and many people miss it, and this palm is worth the detour. Its long fronds are sturdy and impressive, arching outward in silvery gray-green.

How to know it

A feather with a defended base

Narrow leaflets line a long central stalk, like a feather. Near the trunk, some leaflets are modified into hard spines. Older trunks keep the marks of former leaf bases in a rough spiral pattern.

How it grows

The difficulty with humidity

This palm likes dry air and strong sun. Florida’s humidity makes fruit harder to produce here than in the dry regions where dates are a major crop.

Take care

Feel free to walk up and take a closer look, even though there is no path to it. Just watch out for the lower leaf bases, where rigid spines can puncture skin.
