At a glance

  • The leading pomegranate variety grown for sale in the United States, one called 'Wonderful', began in Florida. California's orchards grow it from cuttings that came from Florida, starting in 1896.
  • A wet Florida summer is hard on pomegranates. Fungal rot starts on young fruit in May, and University of Florida researchers say it can ruin a whole crop before the fruit ripens. A few varieties resist it.
  • A pomegranate wears a little crown, and the crown is what is left of the flower. After the petals fall, the tough cup that held them stays on the fruit as it grows.

Cultural significance

Seeds and the underworld

In the Greek hymn to Demeter, Hades gives Persephone one pomegranate seed to eat, and she must spend a third of every year below. In Ovid's version it was seven seeds, and the time was half. A clay pomegranate made in Greece roughly 2,400 years ago is in the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York, modeled with its crown.

A word from the fruit

The English word grenade comes from the French word for pomegranate. The explosive was named for the fruit, probably for its shape and for being stuffed so full of seeds. Grenadine, the red syrup, is made from the juice.

Where it comes from

Asia, then everywhere

It comes from southwestern and central Asia, roughly from Turkey and Iran east to Pakistan. People are reported to have grown it in Persia about 5,000 years ago, and the Romans knew it as the Punic apple, after Carthage. It reached Florida and the Southeast in the 1700s, mostly as a backyard shrub.

How to know it

A twiggy shrub

Look for a bushy shrub or small tree with several stems and narrow, glossy leaves set in pairs, bronze when young. Some twigs end in sharp points.

Crinkled flowers

The flowers are orange-red, with crinkled petals around a ring of many stamens.

Fruit that changes

Young fruit can be pale, tan, bronze or reddish, so color alone does not tell you much.

How it grows

Two kinds of flowers

One shrub carries two kinds of flowers. Some have a swollen base and a long central style and can turn into fruit. Others are functionally male: their female parts are stunted and they never make fruit. A shrub can set fruit with its own pollen.

Rain and splitting

Fruit also splits when watering changes suddenly, especially in the weeks before harvest, which is a risk in a rainy summer.

Leaves

Pomegranates drop their leaves where winters are cool and may keep them in milder climates. Fresh bronze growth fills the fine branches again in spring.

Take care

Thorns

Some twigs end in sharp points. Watch your eyes and hands when you lean in to look at flowers or fruit.
