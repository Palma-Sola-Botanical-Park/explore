Sweet Viburnum

Viburnum odoratissimum
On this page

At a glance

  • In spring the white flower clusters smell strongly sweet. Red berries follow and ripen to black, and birds eat them.
  • Left alone it can reach thirty feet. It also sends up new shoots from its roots, called suckers, that gardeners cut out every year.
  • Next spring's flowers are already on the plant in winter: the buds form in fall and wait out the cold.

Where to find it here

At Palma Sola, Sweet Viburnum lines the office and galleria, growing against a raised deck that sits six feet off the ground with a four-foot rail on top.

Where it comes from

Asia, by way of the nursery

True sweet viburnum grows wild from Assam and the Himalaya across southern China to Taiwan and Southeast Asia. The hedge in many Florida yards may instead be its Japanese cousin, which botanists now list as a separate species, awabuki, wild in Japan, Korea and Taiwan.

What it does

A hard cut every year

The plants push through the deck rails. Once a year they get a hard cut, far more severe than most people expect, and they come back fast. Bright Futures high school volunteers do the cutback to keep the walkway clear.

How to know it

Glossy and opposite

The leaves are large, glossy dark green and leathery, in pairs on opposite sides of the stem, and the plant stays green all year.

Flowers, then berries

The flowers are small and white, in clusters. The berries are small and oval, red at first and black when ripe.

How it grows

Sun, and room

It is fast-growing, and it likes full sun, though it will take part shade. Once it is established it handles dry spells, but it does not do well in salty ground. It needs regular pruning to stay in bounds, but a cut after about October takes off the flower buds, so a hedge trimmed late may skip its bloom.
