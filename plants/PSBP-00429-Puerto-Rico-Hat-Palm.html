Puerto Rico Hat Palm

Sabal causiarum

At a glance

  • A hat palm. Young leaves were picked while still folded in the bud, scalded in boiling water, dried in the sun and split into strips for weaving.
  • A massive trunk, in time. A grown palm's single gray trunk runs roughly 14 to 30 inches thick; the measurements vary from source to source.
  • Twisted fans. Each fan twists into several planes around a strongly curved midrib.
  • Endangered in Puerto Rico and Vulnerable worldwide, in a home range from southern Hispaniola through Puerto Rico to the Virgin Islands.

Cultural significance

Hats from Joyuda

The hat trade centered on Joyuda, on Puerto Rico's west coast near Cabo Rojo, where families wove at home. The width of the strips set the quality: the narrower the strip, the finer the hat, and fine hats were woven in the morning, the evening or in rainy weather. Older leaves thatched roofs, and the fiber went into baskets and mats.

A trade that faded

By 1936 the local leaf industry was bringing in tens of thousands of dollars a year. By 1943, cheap machine-made straw and paper hats were already eating into its market.

Where to find it here

Outside the front gate

Leave by the front gate and turn right along 17th Avenue. It stands in a row with two other Sabals, the Trinidad Palm and the Bermuda Palmetto.

Where it comes from

The northeastern Caribbean: southern Hispaniola, Puerto Rico and the Virgin Islands.

What it does

Three Sabals in a row

With the Trinidad Palm and the Bermuda Palmetto beside it, this is a good place to compare three Caribbean Sabals with the Cabbage Palms, Florida's state tree, that grow all over the park.

How to know it

Fans and leaf bases

Look for fans that twist into several planes and light brown, papery bits near the leaf bases. When it flowers, the flower branches arch out beyond the leaves.

Palm people say: costapalmate
Pinnate
Leaflets line a long central stalk, as on the Cat Palm.
Palmate
Stalk stops at the edge of the blade, as on the Saw Palmetto. No rib.
Costapalmate (this one)
Stalk keeps going into the blade as a rib, and the segments spread from both sides of it.
Fruit helps

Its fruit is small, black and mostly round, from about a quarter to just under half an inch across. The Hispaniolan Palmetto, a close look-alike, has pear-shaped fruit about half an inch long, and the Bermuda Palmetto's is larger still.

How it grows

Ours is still a young plant, with fans rising straight from the ground. The famous trunk comes later: single, gray, and ringed with close leaf scars once the old leaf bases fall away.
