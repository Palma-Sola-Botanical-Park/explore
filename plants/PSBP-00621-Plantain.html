Plantain

Musa × paradisiaca

At a glance

  • The apparent trunk is tightly wrapped leaf bases, not wood.
  • Each leafy stem produces one bunch; new shoots keep the clump going afterward.
  • Green plantains cook like a starch, while ripe ones become sweet enough for a very different dish.

Cultural significance

Plantains are everyday food across much of tropical Africa, the Caribbean and Latin America. Green fruit can be boiled, mashed or fried; as it ripens, starch changes to sugar and the same fruit takes on another role in the kitchen. Tostones start with green plantains, while sweet fried maduros use ripe ones.

The broad leaves have a use of their own. Wrapped around food before steaming or cooking, they hold a parcel together without becoming part of the meal.

Where it comes from

Cultivated plantains descend from wild bananas of Southeast Asia. People selected seedless forms and carried planting shoots from place to place. The result is a collection of cultivated varieties, not one identical plantain grown everywhere.

Where to find it here

Look near the entrance to the Rare Fruit Garden.

What it does here

A hanging bunch lets you see several stages at once. The developing fruits sit above the large purplish bud; as its colored bracts lift, they expose more rows of flowers. Each fruit began as the ovary of a female flower.

How to know it

Find the replacements

Start at ground level and look for smaller shoots beside the taller stems. They are the next generation of this clump. Above them, the long leaves unfurl from the center as tightly rolled spears.

Older leaves often split into strips along their side veins. Wind can make a leaf look ragged while much of its green surface still works.

How it grows

After fruiting, that stem dies back. The underground stem remains alive and feeds new shoots, so losing a fruiting stem does not mean losing the plant. Growers manage those shoots to give the next crop room and light.

A plant with this much leaf area needs a steady water supply. Warmth supports growth; dry weather, wind and cold can interrupt it.
