Stumpy Palm

Dypsis carlsmithii

At a glance

  • Critically Endangered in the wild. When the species was assessed in 2012, fewer than fifteen mature palms were known, at two sites in northeastern Madagascar.
  • It reached palm collectors before science had a name for it. Growers called it "Stumpy", and the nickname stuck after the species was formally described in 2002.
  • The scientific name has since changed. Kew now accepts Chrysalidocarpus carlsmithii; Dypsis carlsmithii is still the name most growers use, and the one on our sign.

Cultural significance

A collector's palm before it had a name

This palm was circulating among growers for at least ten years before anyone had described it. Palm grower Jeff Marcus found an unidentified specimen in the Hilo, Hawaii garden of Donn Carlsmith, and collectors passed it around as Dypsis 'stumpy'. In 2002 Kew botanist John Dransfield and Marcus described it formally and named it for Carlsmith. Only afterward were wild plants located in Madagascar.

Where it comes from

Northeastern Madagascar

Stumpy Palm is native to lowland rainforest in northeastern Madagascar, where it is known from just two places: Tampolo, on the west coast of the Masoala Peninsula, and Mahavelona, north of the port of Toamasina. That tiny wild range is why a palm that could pass for an anonymous young feather palm deserves a second look.

Where to find it here

Along Cypress Pond

Stumpy Palm grows in the row of unusual palms just south of our Plumeria collection, on the south side of Cypress Pond. Starting from the Red Cabbage Palm end of the row, it is the fourth palm in: Red Cabbage Palm, Red Latan Palm, Lala Palm, then Stumpy Palm.

Go ahead and get closer

There is no formal path through this palm collection yet. You are welcome to walk across the open grass to see the palms and read their signs. Watch for low fronds and other plantings as you go.

What it does here

Part of a collection we are still uncovering

This palm belongs to a group of unusual palms that a longtime volunteer bought and planted around the park over many years. Most were never labelled for visitors, and some are only now being identified. Stumpy Palm was standing here before most of us understood how uncommon it is.

How to know it

The crown

Long feather-shaped fronds arch out from the top, and on our young plant the crown is already wider than the palm is tall. On older plants the leaflets fan out at different angles, so each frond looks full rather than flat.

The crownshaft

Just below the leaves, the leaf bases wrap the stem into a smooth green column, the crownshaft. On mature palms it can turn a warm orange with red flecking where new growth is exposed. Ours has not yet.

The trunk

As the palm matures, the old leaves leave closely spaced rings on a single olive-green trunk. For now the base is still wrapped in old leaf bases.

Step back

Our plant does not announce its rarity. At this stage it looks much like several other feather palms; the sign, the green crownshaft and the broad crown are what to go by.

How it grows

A wet-forest palm

Its home is lowland rainforest, and it prefers steady moisture to drought. It grows slowly into a single-trunked palm with a broad crown.

A name change worth knowing

In 2022 a study of Madagascar's palms moved this species from Dypsis to Chrysalidocarpus. You will meet both names in palm books, nurseries and online.
