Pemba Palm

Dypsis pembana
On this page

At a glance

  • Pemba Palm grows wild only on Pemba Island, off the coast of Tanzania, in two small patches of coastal forest.
  • It grows as a clump of slender, strongly ringed stems rather than one massive trunk, and its fruit ripens bright red.
  • A botanist once reported its trunks used as the goal posts on a village football field on Pemba.
  • It is rated Vulnerable, meaning at risk in the wild, because it grows in so few places.

Cultural significance

Goal posts

A 1991 report in the journal Palms, from a botanist's field notes, describes trunks of this palm used as the goal posts of a football field near the Ngezi forest on Pemba. It is a single report, but a vivid one.

Where it comes from

Pemba Island, Tanzania

This species is endemic to Pemba Island, Tanzania. It grows in low, wet coastal forest from sea level to about thirty feet up, and its whole natural range is two small areas on that one island. Nearly all of its relatives grow on Madagascar or the Comoros, hundreds of miles across the sea, which makes this palm the outlier. The name pembana simply means “of Pemba,” and local Swahili names include Mpapindi.

Where to find it here

Big Pond walkway

On the south walkway along Big Pond, it should be about the third palm from the Rare Fruit bridge, after the Satake and the Açaí.

Look for the clump

Several slim green stems rising together are the quickest clue along this stretch of the walkway.

What it does here

One island in one clump

The park's palm collection includes species with enormous continental ranges and others confined naturally to tiny areas. Pemba Palm represents the latter: its entire native distribution is Pemba Island.

How to know it

Several slender stems

The palm suckers from the base and builds a clump of narrow trunks.

Strong rings

The green to pale stems show conspicuous horizontal leaf-scar rings.

A crownshaft

Each stem ends in a crownshaft, the smooth, tight tube of leaf bases from which the leaves spread.

Feather-shaped leaves

The leaves arch outward and can be about three feet long, with forty to fifty narrow leaflets down each side.

Red fruit

Mature fruit is bright red and becomes a strong identification clue when present.

How it grows

A lowland forest palm

Its native habitat is warm, humid and close to sea level, so this is not a cool high-elevation species despite its relatively tidy landscape form.

Frost

A tropical palm like this one is marked by frost, and a hard freeze can do it serious harm.

Clumps renew themselves

Basal shoots add new stems as the plant expands, allowing an established clump to contain stems of different ages.

Few places, so Vulnerable

The world conservation list rates it Vulnerable. The reason is its small range, two forest areas on one island, rather than a measured fall in numbers, and tourism development is named among the threats. That rating dates from 2006 and is flagged as due for an update.

A name that changed

H. E. Moore described this palm in 1962 under the genus Chrysalidocarpus, and in 1995 it was moved to Dypsis. You may still see the older name, Chrysalidocarpus pembanus, in books.
