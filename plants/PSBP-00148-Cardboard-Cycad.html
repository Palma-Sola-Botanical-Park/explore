Cardboard Cycad

Zamia furfuracea

At a glance

  • Pinch a leaflet: it is thick and stiff enough to feel like cardboard.
  • It isn't a palm or a fern. It's a cycad, from a line of seed plants older than the dinosaurs.
  • Every part is poisonous, and the bright red seeds can kill a dog.

Where it comes from

It comes from a small area of eastern Mexico, in the state of Veracruz, where it grows on coastal dunes and scrub. That start explains how well it takes sun, sand and salt spray here.

Dug from the wild

Decades of digging for the plant trade have left its wild populations small and scattered. Most plants sold today are grown in nurseries.

Where to find it here

The park's main plant is on Welcome Island, just inside the gates as you drive in.

A corner full of cycads

Several more grow on the stone tiers at the south end of the cactus garden, across the path from the mangroves at Cypress Pond, alongside the King Sago and the Giant Dioon. A larger, untidier one sits further south and up the tiers.

What it does here

Pollinated by a weevil

Cycads have no flowers. In Mexico this one is pollinated by a tiny snout weevil that breeds inside the male cones, feeding on pollen, then carries that pollen to the female cones on other plants. Ours do make cones: the male ones stand up from the center of the plant.

How to know it

Stiff, blunt leaflets

Each leaf arches out from a low stump of a trunk, with thick, blunt-tipped leaflets set in pairs along it.

Rusty fuzz

New leaves come up coated in a rusty, mealy fuzz, which is what furfuracea means. A finer fuzz stays on the older leaflets.

Cones, never flowers

Male and female cones grow on separate plants. Male cones are slender and upright; female cones are stout and barrel-shaped, and split open to show bright red seeds.

How it grows

It grows slowly into a low mound, three or four feet tall and wider than it is high, and asks for little once it is settled.

It makes its own fertilizer

Special roots near the surface host cyanobacteria that pull nitrogen from the air, so it gets by in poor, sandy soil.

Take care

Poisonous, and deadly to dogs

Every part of the plant is poisonous to people and pets. The bright red seeds are the worst: one or two can kill a dog. Keep dogs away from it, and don't let children pick up the seeds.
