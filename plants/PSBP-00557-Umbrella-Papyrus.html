Umbrella Papyrus

Cyperus alternifolius
On this page

At a glance

  • Cut a stem, turn it upside down and stand the umbrella end in a glass of water. In a couple of weeks a new plant sprouts from it. In the wild the stems bend until the head touches wet mud and root there on their own.
  • The umbrella is not a crown of leaves. It is a ring of bracts around the tiny flowers, held up on a bare three-sided stem. Roll one between your fingers: sedges have edges.
  • It has escaped into wet places across the middle and south of Florida, and the state lists it as a plant to watch. Ours stay where they are put.
  • Its roots pull copper and manganese out of dirty water, and engineers use it in built wetlands for exactly that.

Where to find it here

Two plantings. One is on the bank of the Big Pond, a round green dome standing in the water with rocks at its feet, near the African Oil Palm. The other is in the Galleria Field, a few steps south of the Kukui tree.

Where it comes from

Africa’s wetlands

Swamps, stream sides and seasonally flooded grassland across tropical and southern Africa, Madagascar and the Arabian Peninsula, where it is cut for weaving, building and fodder. It is not the papyrus of the ancient Egyptians, which is a much bigger cousin; it is the one that took over water gardens everywhere.

What it does

Feet in the pond

The pond clump does the job this plant is built for. Its stem bases and roots stand in the water and give small fish and insects somewhere to hide, wading birds hunt its edges, and it is a green dome on the bank in every season.

How to know it

Straight three-sided stems

Smooth, bare, triangular in cross-section, three to five feet tall, in a dense clump.

The umbrella

A flat spray of long narrow bracts at the top of every stem, radiating like spokes.

Tufts in the centre

Small brown-green tufts sitting in the middle of the umbrella in summer and autumn. Not showy, but that is the flower.

How it grows

Wet feet, always. It thrives at a pond margin or in shallow water and dies back fast if it dries out. It spreads by rhizome into a widening clump and by seed on the water, which is how it gets away in Florida. Sun or part shade; fast in warm weather.
