At a glance

  • When this palm was shipped to Southeast Asia, its pollinator stayed behind. For decades plantation workers pollinated every flower by hand. Somebody finally brought the weevil over in 1981 and the hand-work stopped almost overnight.
  • One fruit yields two different oils — one pressed from the orange pulp, another from the hard kernel inside it. Different chemistry, different industries, same piece of fruit.
  • In West Africa, chimpanzees crack the nuts open with stones. It is one of the clearest records of tool use by any animal that is not us.
  • Ours is at the cold edge of where this will grow, and it shows. It is a specimen here, not a crop.

Cultural significance

Five thousand years of it

Palm oil shows up in West African diets in the archaeological record at least five thousand years back, which makes this one of the longest-used crop plants anywhere. Portuguese ships met it in the 1400s. By the 1800s it was an export — soap and candles first, then industrial lubricant for British factories.

And now, roughly half the supermarket

Carried to Southeast Asia in 1848, it became the most productive oil crop on earth. Malaysia and Indonesia now grow about eighty-five percent of the world's supply across more than nineteen million hectares. That expansion has cost an enormous amount of forest, and the habitat of orangutans and a great deal else with it. Both halves of that are true of the tree in front of you.

Where to find it here

The southwest corner of Big Pond

It stands prominently at the southwest corner of Big Pond, near the shade of the elm, right down on the limestone rock of the bank with its fronds leaning out over the water. Coming the usual way, it is the first palm on your left.

Where it comes from

The wet lowlands of West Africa

Native from Senegal south to Angola and east toward Lake Victoria, in country where water is dependable — riverbanks, freshwater swamp, the open edges of forest where light reaches the ground.

What it does

Holding its own at the cold edge

This is a hot, wet, equatorial palm, and Bradenton is about as far north as one can be kept going. Growth that would be quick on a plantation is slow here, and the oldest fronds brown off at their tips. It carries the corner anyway. The trunk is only now starting to put on real volume, and it has gone sturdy and woody with it — the look of a palm that has decided to stay.

How to know it

A trunk of stubs

Old leaf bases stay on rather than falling clean away, so the trunk is armoured in short woody stubs arranged in diamonds. It is a stout, rough column, quite unlike the smooth grey trunks further along the walk.

Big arching fronds

Long feather fronds with many narrow leaflets, arching well out and down. On ours they reach over the pond itself.

Do not grab a frond

The leaf stalks carry stiff sharp spines along their edges. This is not a palm to push past or take hold of.

How it grows

Slow here, tall at home

Up to about sixty-five feet in the wild and a good deal less in Florida, on a single trunk with a crown fifteen to twenty-five feet across. Growth that would be moderate in the tropics is slow at this latitude.

The bird that lives on it

In Africa the palm-nut vulture takes more than sixty percent of its adult diet from this one fruit — an unusually tight bond between a large bird of prey and a single plant. Grey parrots and fruit bats work the same crop.
