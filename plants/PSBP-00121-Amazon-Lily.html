At a glance

  • It is a garden hybrid that almost never sets seed, so gardeners can share it only by dividing the bulbs: every plant is a piece of an older one.
  • This one has come down through the Baden family since the 1880s. A plaque on Bird of Paradise Island, the bed behind the office, tells the story.
  • The name misleads: its two parent species grow wild in the Pacific-side rain forest of Colombia and Ecuador, not in the Amazon.
  • After hurricane saltwater flooded its bed, it was cut back to bare stubs. It is growing again from the bulb.

Cultural significance

A living family heirloom

Divisions of this plant have been handed down through the Baden family since the late 1800s, and the park's Amazon Lily carries that heritage forward. A plaque on Bird of Paradise Island, facing the office, commemorates it.

Why a family line matters

A plant that makes almost no seed can travel only in pieces, a bulb divided and handed on. A Philadelphia nursery catalog was already offering Amazon lilies in 1879, so a bulb in an American family by the 1880s fits the history.

Where it comes from

Colombia and Ecuador, not the Amazon

It is native from western Colombia to western Ecuador, in rain-forest understory on the Pacific side of the Andes, and is thought to be a natural cross between two species there, Urceolina moorei and U. sanderi. Planchon and Linden named it in the 1850s from a Colombian plant. Most plants anywhere grow in gardens, and some wild colonies may be leftovers from cultivation.

A name that moved

Botanists now file it under Urceolina, a genus that took in Eucharis in 2018, so older labels say Eucharis × grandiflora. For about a century the hybrid and its look-alike, Urceolina amazonica, were mixed up in gardens, and both are still sold as the Amazon lily.

Where to find it here

Bird of Paradise Island, facing the office

Look low beneath the much taller Bird of Paradise plants; the Amazon Lily is easy to overlook until it flowers. A commemorative plaque marks the spot.

What it does

It refuses to give up

Saltwater flooding in recent years cut the old plant back to bare stubs, but in 2026 new growth is returning from the bulb, the latest chapter in a heritage that has already lasted well over a century.

How to know it

Glossy broad leaves

The leaves are dark green, glossy and broad, eight to thirteen inches long, with deep pleats running along them.

White flowers on a stalk

Two to six white, sweetly scented flowers rise together on a bare stalk sixteen to twenty inches tall. Each has a short, toothed cup in the middle, green below and white at the tips, about half as long as the cup on its look-alike, which is the quickest way to tell them apart.

How it grows

Shade plant

This is a bulbous understory plant that prefers bright shade, moisture and protection from intense sun.

Flowering and weak spots

It can flower at any time of year, and it blooms best slightly crowded. A dry spell can set off a new flush. Frost and salt both hurt it.
