Heavenly Bamboo

Nandina domestica
On this page

At a glance

  • It isn't a bamboo. The resemblance is in its upright, cane-like stems.
  • What look like many small leaves are one big divided leaf, split into pointed leaflets.
  • Ours flowers and sets berries; in late May it carried white flowers and green berries at the same time.
  • Its berries have killed flocks of Cedar Waxwings, and in Florida it is listed as an invasive plant.

Cultural significance

Southern heavenly bamboo

The name comes from the Japanese nanten, itself from the Chinese for southern heavenly bamboo, and domestica means of the household. In Japan it was planted beside washbasins as a guard against evil, and its wood was carved into charms for children against whooping cough.

Where it comes from

It is native to eastern Asia, from the Himalayas through China to Japan, and came to the United States as a garden plant in the early 1800s.

Where to find it here

On Welcome Island, tucked in among the Tree Crinum.

What it does here

Hemmed in

Ours is a single plant, crowded in by Tree Crinum, and in about ten years here we have seen no seedlings or spread from it. What one hemmed-in plant does in a bed and what the species does in a north Florida woodland are different questions.

Berries birds can't handle

Birds eat the red berries, and that is how it travels. But the berries contain compounds that release cyanide, and a bird that gorges on them can die. A flock of Cedar Waxwings in Georgia was found dead with their crops packed with these berries. Waxwings feed in flocks and fill up fast, which is thought to be why they, of all birds, were the ones hit.

How to know it

Cane-like stems

Upright, mostly unbranched stems, like thin canes.

One leaf, many leaflets

Each leaf is divided two or three times into small, pointed leaflets, so a single leaf looks like a whole spray. Find where one leaf joins the stem.

Flowers, then berries

Small white flowers in upright clusters, then round berries that ripen bright red and hang on through winter.

Color through the year

New leaves come in red or bronze, turn green for summer, and redden again in cool weather.

How it grows

Birds eat the berries and pass the seeds, which is how it travels beyond gardens. It also spreads underground by suckers, so one plant slowly becomes a thicket. Across north Florida and into the central peninsula it has moved into woodlands, where its dense growth shades out the plants beneath, and the state lists it among its most damaging invasive plants.

Take care

Poisonous

All parts contain compounds that release cyanide, the berries especially. Don't eat any part of it, and keep pets away.
