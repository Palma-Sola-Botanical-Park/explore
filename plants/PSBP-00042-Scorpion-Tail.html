Scorpion Tail

Heliotropium angiospermum
On this page

At a glance

  • The flower stalk curls like a scorpion's tail and uncurls as it grows. The flowers open one at a time, starting at the base, so one stalk shows fruit at one end, open flowers in the middle and buds at the tip.
  • The tiny white flowers feed butterflies as different as the small gray hairstreak and the big orange gulf fritillary. At Florida's southern tip they also feed two of the state's rarest butterflies, both endangered: the Miami blue and Schaus' swallowtail.
  • A single plant lasts only a year or a few, but it reseeds so freely that a patch can outlast the plants that started it.

Cultural significance

The sun in an older name

Heliotropium comes from Greek words for sun and turn, from the old belief that these plants swung their flowers to follow the sun. The name keeps the belief, not a promise that this patch will turn as you watch.

Where it comes from

Coast and tropics

A Florida native, found along the coast and south down the peninsula. Beyond Florida it grows in Texas and through the tropics of the Americas. Coastal hammocks and open, disturbed ground suit it.

Where to find it here

Around the ponds

Look for it around the park's ponds, along the top of the banks.

What it does here

Part of the pond restoration

Scorpion Tail was planted here as part of the grant-funded restoration of the pond edges: a low native for the top of the bank.

More than one season

In Florida it can flower nearly all year. It has been photographed in flower here in March, June, July and September 2026, so there is more than one season in which to watch for butterflies.

How to know it

Small flowers, strong veins

The white flowers have five spreading lobes around a yellow center. The leaves are green and softly hairy, with deep, plain veins. A whole plant is usually one to three feet tall.

Follow the edge

Seen from the side, the flowers line up along one edge of the stalk rather than bunching into a rounded head.

How it grows

Dry ground, salty air

Sun or part shade and sandy, well-drained soil suit it. Salt carried on the wind is fine; standing salt or brackish water around the roots is not, which is why it goes at the top of a bank rather than at the waterline.

Fruit behind the flowers

When the flowers finish, the stalk fills with small green fruits that split into seed-bearing nutlets.

Take care

Not a plant to eat

Don't eat any part of it. Heliotropium plants contain alkaloids that can damage the liver if eaten.
