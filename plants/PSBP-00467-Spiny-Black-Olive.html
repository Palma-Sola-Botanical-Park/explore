At a glance

  • The branches grow out flat and zigzag, tier above tier, so the whole tree reads like a drawing. That is where the nickname Geometry Tree comes from.
  • The leaves are thumbnail-sized and come in little rosettes of five or six on short spur shoots along the twigs, like beads on a string.
  • Stiff spines up to an inch long hide at the base of the leaf clusters. Look before you touch.
  • It is the small, spiny cousin of the Black Olive, the big shade tree of South Florida streets, and the two can cross.
  • Grown in a pot it is a bonsai classic, sold in America as the Ming Tree.

Cultural significance

A bonsai that grows itself

Slow growth, tiny leaves and flat tiers of branches are what bonsai growers spend years coaxing out of other trees. This one does it on its own, which is why it is a staple of the bonsai bench and goes by Ming Tree in the trade.

The cousin it crosses with

Black Olive, Terminalia buceras, is the big relative, a street shade tree across South Florida. Crosses between the two are grown as ornamentals, and in the Sian Ka'an country of Mexico's Yucatán, wild trees with leaves and flower clusters halfway between the two have been read as natural hybrids.

Where it comes from

Its home is the Caribbean, from the islands down to southern Mexico and Belize, and the far southern tip of Florida, where wild trees are few. It grows in hardwood hammocks, along the edges of pinelands and on the coast, where salt wind keeps most trees small.

Where to find it here

In the far southeastern corner of the park, on the grass beside the bridge into the Rare Fruit area. Most people cross that bridge without a second look at it.

What it does

The tree that gets photographed

Plain at a glance, and yet the volunteers and photographers who stop for it keep coming away with something: the cream flowers, the fruit turning brown, the zigzag tiers against the sky. It rewards the second look most visitors never give it.

How to know it

Stand back first

The shape gives it away before anything else does: branches held out flat in layers, each one jogging left and right as it goes. A young tree is all open tiers. With age the crown fills in and rounds off, but the layers are still there if you look.

Then look close

The leaves are glossy dark green on top, paler underneath, and new growth comes in reddish. Each is about the length of a thumbnail, widest near a rounded or slightly notched tip. They sit in rosettes on short spur shoots, and that is the test against the look-alike: Walter's Viburnum, another small-leaved Florida native, carries its leaves in opposite pairs along the twig and has no spines.

Flowers and fruit

The flowers are tiny, cream-colored brushes in short clusters near the branch tips. The fruits that follow are peppercorn-sized, oval and faintly angled, and they turn from green through tan to brown over a couple of months. The tree can carry both at once.

How it grows

Slow, tough, and not quite evergreen

It is a shrub or small tree, ten to twenty feet at most, and it takes its time getting there. It wants sun, shrugs off drought and salt wind, and can drop part of its leaves in a dry spell rather than hold every one all year.

Where the spines are

The spines sit just below each leaf cluster, so they are easy to miss until you reach in. Keep your hands on the outside of the tree and let your eyes do the work.

Take care

Stiff spines up to an inch long hide at the base of the leaf clusters and can puncture skin. Keep hands clear of the branches.
