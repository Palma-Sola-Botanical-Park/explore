Bermuda Palmetto

At a glance

  • Bermuda's own palm. It is the only palm native to the island.
  • Endangered at home. Building and invasive plants have pushed it back into a few protected forests.
  • Ancient forests in stone. Bermuda's coastal rock holds molds of palm trunks and fronds from about 100,000 years ago, most likely this same palm.
  • A cousin of the state tree. It is a Sabal, like the Cabbage Palms all over the park, and you can tell them apart by the flowers and fruit.

Cultural significance

Hats, rope and roofs

Bermudians made hats, baskets, fans and rope from its leaves and thatched their roofs with them. By the late 1600s, plaiting its leaves had become an export trade, run largely by women, that reached North America and Britain. One braid used for hats and handbags is called fishpot, after the pattern of Bermuda's fish traps.

Food, drink and shelter

When castaways were stranded on Bermuda in 1593, they ate the palm, drank its juice and sheltered under its leaves. Bermudians also made a drink called bibby from it: most accounts say from sap tapped just below the growing bud, one from the fruit.

Standing when buried

Some of the fossil fronds cut across the layers of the old dunes. That means the palms were still standing when blowing sand buried them.

Where to find it here

Outside the front gate

Leave by the front gate and turn right along 17th Avenue. It stands in a row with two other Sabals, the Puerto Rico Hat Palm and the Trinidad Palm.

Where it comes from

Bermuda, in upland forest, coastal forest and freshwater marsh. Where it grows on other islands, people brought it.

What it does

A home in the fiber

In Bermuda the fiber around the crown gives insects a place to live and birds a source of nesting material. Ours is still too young to have built up much of it.

How to know it

Stiff fans

The fan is stiff and strongly folded, which makes the crown look coarse. The leaf stalk is smooth, with a yellow patch where it meets the blade.

Against the Cabbage Palm

When it flowers, its flower clusters stay within the length of the leaf stalks; a Cabbage Palm's reach out past the leaves. Its black fruit also runs larger, about half to three-quarters of an inch across, though a big Cabbage Palm fruit can come close.

How it grows

Ours has no trunk yet. For now the leaf stalks rise straight out of the ground, wrapped at the base in old leaf sheaths.
