At a glance

  • Two chemotherapy drugs, vinblastine and vincristine, come from this common garden flower. Both are still used against leukemia and lymphoma.
  • The discovery began with a diabetes tea. In 1952 a doctor mailed 25 leaves from a patient in Jamaica to his brother, a researcher in Canada. The leaves barely touched blood sugar but sharply cut white blood cells, a clue for leukemia, a cancer of those cells.
  • Both drugs work by stopping cells from dividing, which hits fast-growing cancer cells hardest. Healthy cells that divide quickly are hit too, which is why chemotherapy has side effects.
  • Making a paper clip's weight of vinblastine takes more than a thousand pounds of dried leaves, by one common estimate.

Cultural significance

Two labs, one hunch

A second research team, at a drug company in the United States, was testing the plant at the same time, also because of its diabetes reputation. The Canadian team isolated vinblastine by 1958, and the company's lab isolated vincristine in 1961. Within a few years both were in hospitals.

Was the tea right?

The first test found that leaf extracts barely moved blood sugar. Later studies in diabetic rats report lower glucose, so the folk reputation isn't settled either way.

What the drugs jam

Before a cell divides it builds a scaffold of fibers that pulls its copied chromosomes into two sets. Vinblastine and vincristine stop that scaffold from forming, so the cell stalls.

Where it comes from

Madagascar, then everywhere warm

It is native to Madagascar, where eight of the genus's nine species live. Gardeners carried it around the tropics, and in Florida it now seeds itself into beds and edges.

Vinca, by habit

Botanists called it Vinca rosea until 1837, when it got a genus of its own, Catharanthus, from the Greek for pure flower. Nurseries kept the old name, so it is still sold as vinca.

A dogbane cousin

It belongs to the dogbane family, like the park's Mexican Plumeria. Both build their flowers on a narrow tube that opens into a flat, five-lobed face.

What it does here

Color through the heat

Many bedding flowers quit in a Florida summer. This one keeps going, one flat face after another, in white and shades of pink and rose, usually with a darker eye.

Spread by hand

Periwinkle does so well here that volunteers have been spreading it slowly around the park entrance and the cactus and succulent garden, for a dash of permanent color.

A deeper flower than it looks

The flat face is only the entrance. The nectar sits down a narrow tube about an inch long, so mostly long-tongued butterflies and moths can reach it. Some modern garden forms offer less nectar than the wild kind.

How to know it

A loose mound

A low, branching mound, usually one to two feet tall, with stems that spill over the edge of a bed.

Follow the pale line

The glossy oval leaves sit in opposite pairs, and each has a pale line down the middle.

How it grows

Sun and a dry spell

Full sun and sandy, well-drained soil suit it, and once it is established it shrugs off a dry spell. Wet feet and long rainy spells are what trouble it: in a soggy summer a blight can blacken the young leaves.

Spilling past the bed

It sets seed freely, so seedlings turn up beyond the bed. Where frost never comes it can live for years as a small shrub; elsewhere it is grown as an annual.

Take care

Do not eat any part

Do not eat any part of it or brew it into tea. Every part holds the same family of alkaloids that makes the medicines, and in quantity they cause vomiting, diarrhea and low blood pressure, with tremors and seizures in severe cases. It is toxic to dogs, cats and horses too.
