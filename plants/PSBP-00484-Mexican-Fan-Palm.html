Mexican Fan Palm

Washingtonia robusta
On this page

At a glance

  • Look up. This is a tall, slim palm, usually fifty to a hundred feet, with a straight trunk under a crown of green fans.
  • Watch the tips. The fan leaves droop at the ends, and dead ones can hang on as a shaggy skirt below the crown.
  • It is a desert palm that drinks. In Mexico it grows in desert washes where water runs underground all year.
  • Birds carry it around. They eat the black fruit and spread the seeds, and the palm has naturalized in Florida.
  • Small cream flowers hang in drooping clusters, in spring.

Cultural significance

A president in the name

The whole genus, Washingtonia, is named for George Washington.

A street palm

People plant it along streets and roads around the world, which is just how the park uses it, in a long row at the Rare Fruit entrance.

Where to find it here

The Rare Fruit entrance

A long row of these stands at the entrance to the Rare Fruit area. Look up between the restrooms and the Rare Fruit trail, and you will see seven tall ones. Count them. One more stands in the Pavilion field, opposite the long row of Royal Palms.

Where it comes from

Baja California Sur and Sonora, in Mexico, in desert washes where underground water keeps the ground damp. Botanists disagree about its rank. Some call it its own species, and others treat it as a form of the California Fan Palm.

How to know it

Tall, slim and shaggy

Look for a straight, slim trunk topped with green fan leaves whose tips droop. On many palms a skirt of dead leaves hangs below the crown, though the tallest ones shed theirs.

Species or form?

Experts do not agree on whether this is a separate species or a variety of the California Fan Palm. We call it Mexican Fan, as most gardeners do.

How it grows

A palm that gets around

Birds eat the black fruit and drop the seeds far from the parent. That is how the palm has naturalized in Florida and southern California, and growers in south Florida are asked to keep it from escaping.
