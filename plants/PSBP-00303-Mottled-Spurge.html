Mottled Spurge

Euphorbia lactea
On this page

At a glance

  • It is not a cactus. Cacti are American; this is from India and Sri Lanka, and the two families arrived at the same spiny, water-storing body on opposite sides of the world without ever meeting.
  • Break a stem and it bleeds thick white latex that seals the wound in seconds. That sap blisters skin and can do real harm to an eye, which is why this is a plant for looking at.
  • The wavy, ruffled one in the succulent bed is two plants in one: a crested mutation grafted onto the roots of a different spurge. Look for the seam near the base of the fan.
  • In parts of India it is a hedge. Cattle do not push through it twice.

Where it comes from

India and Sri Lanka

Dry scrub and rocky ground in India and Sri Lanka, from where it has been carried around the warm world as a living fence and a curiosity. It reached Florida gardens in the mid-twentieth century along with the taste for dramatic plants that needed no water.

Where to find it here

In the Cacti and Succulents beds. There are two to look for: the crested, ruffled form sitting in the ice plant like a grey-green coral fan, and a taller candelabra with three- or four-sided green stems marbled in white.

What it does here

The impostor in the cactus bed

Stand it next to a real cactus and play spot the difference. The cactus carries its spines in tufts from little cushions; the spurge carries them in pairs along the ridges, and the spurge bleeds white when it is cut. Two unrelated families, one answer to a dry country. That is the whole reason it is in this bed.

How to know it

Ribbed stems doing the work of leaves

Upright, succulent, three- or four-angled, with a wavy ridge down each angle and pale marbling on the green. No leaves to speak of.

Paired spines

Short black spines set two at a time along the ridges, not in tufts.

White latex

Any cut or break weeps milky sap. A cactus does not.

The crest

On the grafted plant, a fan of frilled, ruffled growth on top of a plain straight stem that belongs to another species. The calloused join is visible near the base of the fan.

How it grows

Slow, and eventually a small tree of fifteen feet where it never freezes. Bradenton is at the cold edge of that; a hard frost turns the stem tips soft and dark. Full sun and sharp drainage keep it happy, and too much water is the usual way to lose one. It rarely flowers in cultivation and is grown from cuttings, which are left to dry and seal for a few days before planting.

Take care

The sap is the hazard. It blisters skin and can injure an eye badly, so no snapping stems, and gloves and glasses for anyone who has to prune it. Not for eating, by people or dogs.
