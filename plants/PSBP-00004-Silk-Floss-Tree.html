At a glance

  • The trunk is armed with large conical spines, dramatic on young trees and worn flatter on old ones.
  • It drops its leaves in autumn and then covers the bare canopy in large pink flowers.
  • Football-shaped pods split open to release black seeds wrapped in masses of white silky floss.
  • The green bark of a young tree contains chlorophyll and can photosynthesise on its own.

Cultural significance

Toborochi

In Bolivia the tree is called toborochi, Guaraní for the sheltering tree, and it carries one of the country's best-known legends: a pregnant princess named Araverá, pursued by spirits, is said to have hidden inside the swollen trunk to give birth. The bottle-shaped form is held to shelter her still. The toborochi is the symbol of Santa Cruz and appears on the city's coat of arms.

Floss and timber

The silky fibres around the seeds have been used as stuffing for pillows and upholstery. They closely resemble true kapok, which comes from a close relative — Ceiba pentandra — and it was that species, not this one, whose floss filled life jackets for the US Navy. The wood is light enough for canoes, and the seed oil is used in South America.

Where it comes from

Native to the subtropical and tropical forests of South America — Brazil, Argentina, Paraguay, Uruguay and Bolivia.

Where to find it here

By the Office.

An open question on our tree

Ours is well past the green-bark stage and its trunk has long since turned gray — yet green still runs down the grooves. We are not certain why. The best guess is a film of algae settling into the damp channels where rainwater funnels down, rather than the tree's own chlorophyll, but we are still looking into it.

What it does here

A tree in bloom is a serious nectar source, and it flowers in autumn when much else has finished — useful timing for late bees and migrating hummingbirds. Birds also raid the burst pods for floss to line nests.

What one tree can hold

In a single study of one suburban tree in Mexico, researchers recorded 79 insect species, eight bird species and a nectar-feeding bat visiting its flowers. That is one tree in one place, not a promise about ours — but it shows what a flowering Silk Floss can become.

Three trunks worth comparing

Its closest relatives include the Kapok and the African Baobab, and both are growing here too — three of the strangest trunk architectures in the plant kingdom, in one park.

How to know it

Trunk

Bottle-shaped and swollen, studded with stout conical spines. Green-barked when young, gray at maturity.

Flowers

Large hibiscus-like blooms, pink to pink-purple with pale spotted centres, four to six inches across, October through December.

Pods and floss

Green capsules up to eight inches long that split to release dozens of black seeds in white silk.

Nothing else here looks like it. The spiny bottle trunk identifies it in any season, with or without leaves.

How it grows

Green bark

A young Silk Floss has chlorophyll in its bark, so the trunk and branches keep photosynthesising even when every leaf has gone. The colour fades as the bark matures and greys.

Flowers before foliage

It is deciduous, and it drops its leaves in autumn before flowering rather than after. The display lands on bare branches, which is why a tree in full bloom can look like it is made of flowers.

Seeds on the wind

Mature pods split and expose seeds wrapped in light silky fibres, which carry them away from the parent tree.

Why the spines?

One hypothesis is that the trunk spines protected these trees from large browsing mammals, including the South American megafauna — giant ground sloths and gomphotheres — that died out around ten thousand years ago. It is plausible and often repeated, but it is not settled.

Full sun, moist well-drained soil, fast growth where there is water. Plant it at least fifteen feet from a path: it forms large surface roots at the base.

Take care

The spines

The large conical trunk spines are the hazard here. Do not lean on the trunk or let children climb it.
