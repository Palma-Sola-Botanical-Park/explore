At a glance

  • Firebush can carry red-orange flowers and ripening berries at the same time, offering nectar and fruit from one shrub.
  • Butterflies regularly work the flowers outside our office. The first Giant Swallowtail in the park's own photo collection was feeding on this Firebush.
  • This Florida native can grow into a large shrub here, which is exactly how we use it: as part of the living wall that frames our Shade Garden.
  • In warm parts of Florida the flowers keep coming for much of the year. UF/IFAS puts it simply: it blooms all year.

Where it comes from

A Florida native with a huge range

Firebush is native to Florida, but Florida is near the northern end of a much larger natural range. It grows through the Caribbean, Mexico, Central America and much of South America, reaching as far south as Argentina.

Where to find it here

At the Shade Garden

Look just outside the office. Our Firebush grows tall alongside Turk's Turban, forming a leafy wall along one side of the Shade Garden. The paver path leads through this taller planting into the lower, shadier garden beyond.

When

Firebush has an unusually long flowering season in Florida. Around Bradenton it can remain in flower for much of the year, although cold weather can slow or damage it. Flowers and berries may be present on the same plant.

What it does here

It helps make the Shade Garden

Firebush isn't just planted beside our Shade Garden for its flowers. Its height and dense growth, together with Turk's Turban, create an enclosing wall on one side. Walk along the pavers and the planting changes from the sunny area outside the office to a more enclosed space filled with plants that thrive in shade.

Watch for big butterflies

Butterflies frequently dart among these flowers. The first Giant Swallowtail in the park's own photo collection was photographed feeding on this Firebush in July 2026. Giant Swallowtails are among Florida's largest butterflies, and adults visit flowers for nectar.

Two plants, two life stages

There's another Giant Swallowtail connection elsewhere in the park. Adult butterflies feed at Firebush flowers, while their caterpillars eat plants in the citrus family, including our native Wild Lime. One plant feeds the butterfly; another feeds its young.

How to know it

Look for the tubes

The flowers are slender red-orange tubes held in clusters at the ends of branches, built for hummingbirds and butterflies.

Follow a berry as it ripens

Firebush fruit changes color as it matures, eventually becoming dark purple to nearly black. Different stages can occur together while flowers are still opening.

Step back

In our climate, Firebush isn't necessarily a small flowering bush. Left to grow, it can become a substantial shrub or small tree. The Florida Native Plant Society gives a potential height of about twenty feet.

How it grows

Sun makes more flowers

Firebush tolerates some shade, but flowering decreases as shade increases. In sun it produces the dense flowering growth that makes it valuable to nectar-feeding wildlife.

Warmth lets it get big

Firebush behaves differently across Florida. Freezing temperatures can kill the stems back farther north, after which the plant may resprout from the base. In warmer areas it keeps growing as a woody shrub and can reach fifteen feet or more.
