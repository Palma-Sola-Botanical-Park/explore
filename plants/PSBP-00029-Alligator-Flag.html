Alligator Flag

Thalia geniculata

At a glance

  • Alligator Flag is native to Florida and has a remarkably broad native range that also includes tropical America and tropical Africa.
  • It spreads by rhizomes and can form large colonies in marshes, swamps and pond edges.
  • It is a host plant for the Brazilian Skipper. Caterpillars feed on the leaves and often hide in rolled leaf shelters.
  • The flowers are small compared with the plant, carried on tall branching stalks that bend at distinct joints.

Cultural significance

A Seminole food and cooking plant

Ethnobotanical records describe Seminole use of the roots, rhizomes, flowers and stem bases as food, while the large leaves were used to wrap food for cooking.

Where it comes from

Kew accepts Thalia geniculata and gives a native range spanning tropical Africa and the southeastern United States through tropical America. In Florida it is a wetland plant of marsh edges, swamps, cypress sloughs and wet ditches.

Where to find it here

The Galleria-pavilion bridge

Planted in 2024 by Christine and Randy, knee-high in the muck at the time. By 2026 it had grown over 8 feet tall — tall enough to touch from the bridge — part of the park's project of planting along the pond edges to help preserve them.

The northwest corner of Front Pond

Molly and Miles planted three more here. They're holding on, though not thriving yet.

When

Summer through fall is the best time to find the small purplish flowers above the leaves. Rolled leaves can reveal Brazilian Skipper caterpillars even when the plant is not flowering.

What it does here

A wetland plant that also feeds a butterfly

Along the pond edge, the plant does two visible jobs at once: its rhizomes occupy wet soil and its leaves provide larval food and shelter for Brazilian Skippers.

Easy come, easy go

The bridge planting grew fast enough to reach over 8 feet — then critters started eating it back just as quickly. A reminder that a thriving wetland planting is still being eaten by something, and that's normal, not a failure.

How to know it

Huge leaves first

Broad, upright, paddle-like leaves rise well above the water or wet soil.

Flowers above the foliage

Slender branching flower stalks extend above the leaves and carry small purple to whitish flowers.

Look for the knees

The flowering branches bend at distinct joints; geniculata refers to that knee-like form.

Rolled leaves tell another story

A leaf folded or rolled into a tube may be a Brazilian Skipper shelter rather than simple damage.

How it grows

A rhizome-built colony

Underground rhizomes send up new shoots, allowing one planting to widen steadily in wet ground.

Water is the requirement

Florida sources place Alligator Flag in very wet soils and shallow standing water. It is not a dry-site plant.

Fresh water suits it better than salt

Florida Native Plant Society guidance rates it poorly for saltwater inundation and salt spray.

Take care

Alligator Flag has a documented history as a food plant, but that is not an invitation to eat from the park. Leave roots, shoots, flowers and leaves in place.
