At a glance

  • The Aztecs called it the sleepy sapote. A Spanish doctor wrote in the 1570s that eating the fruit made people drowsy, and the leaves and seeds are still used in Mexico as a folk sedative.
  • It is in the citrus family, and the Giant Swallowtail butterfly knows it: it lays its eggs on the leaves, and its caterpillar is disguised as a bird dropping.
  • Casimiroa honors Casimiro Gómez, said to have been an Otomí man from Hidalgo who fought and died in Mexico's war of independence. The two Mexican botanists who named it in 1825 were handing plant names to the war's heroes.
  • The seeds are poisonous. The flesh is sweet and custard-like, and the fruit bruises so easily that it rarely reaches a supermarket.

Cultural significance

Sleepy sapote

Cochiztzapotl, the Aztec name, means sleepy sapote. Francisco Hernández, the physician Philip II sent to Mexico in the 1570s, wrote that eating the fruit brought on drowsiness and called the seeds a deadly poison. In Mexico the seeds, leaves and bark are still used as a sedative in folk medicine.

A name from the war

Pablo de La Llave and Juan Lexarza, the botanists who named the genus, also named plants for Hidalgo, Allende, Abasolo and Morelos, the leaders of the war. What is known of Casimiro Gómez comes from a few nineteenth-century lines; the botanists' own dedication has not been read in modern times.

Where it comes from

The Mexican highlands

White sapote comes from the highlands of central Mexico and Central America, from fairly dry country a few thousand feet up. Franciscan monks brought it to California about 1810, and it reached Florida later. It is grown commercially only on a small scale, because the fruit is picked firm and bruises at a touch.

What it does here

A citrus cousin in disguise

Nothing about it looks like an orange tree, but the family shows in the leaf: a hand of usually five leaflets on one stalk, dotted with tiny oil glands. The Giant Swallowtail, whose caterpillar is a familiar sight on backyard citrus, lays on it too. Turn a leaf over and you may find a caterpillar that looks exactly like a bird dropping.

A bloomer that may not fruit

Some varieties make flowers that work only as female, so a lone tree can bloom every year and never set fruit without a pollen tree nearby.

How to know it

Five fingers

Each leaf is a stalk with three to seven glossy leaflets fanning from its tip, usually five. The flowers are small and greenish-yellow, and the fruit is a smooth, thin-skinned ball two and a half to four inches across that ripens from green to yellow.

How it grows

Upright, then spreading

A young tree grows upright and then spreads, with branches that arch and droop. It can reach fifteen to sixty feet. A seedling takes seven or eight years to bear, so growers graft the varieties they want. A young tree is hurt by a frost in the mid-twenties.

Take care

The seeds

The seeds are poisonous and should never be eaten. The flesh of the ripe fruit is fine.
