Little Lady Palm

Rhapis excelsa
On this page

At a glance

  • For about 200 years in Japan, only the upper classes grew this palm. By one account, a lord gave one to the shogun, who passed offshoots on to other lords as marks of honor.
  • Some lady palms have striped leaves; most are plain green. Nobody has found a way to make a plant grow stripes on purpose, even with X-rays or chemicals, and in 1973 the rarest striped plants in Japan were priced at $20,000 to $80,000 each.
  • In the wild, lady palm stems can reach about 13 feet, yet Japanese collectors keep their finest plants in pots only 8 to 20 inches high. Its scientific name, Rhapis excelsa, even means 'tall'.

Cultural significance

A name from the islands

By 1709 a book printed in Japan called it Kannon-chiku, which means a bamboolike plant from Kannon-zan, a temple or mountain in the Ryukyu Islands. A book of 1818 says it came from there.

Stripes only by division

A striped plant stays striped only when you divide it. Seedlings can't be counted on to carry the pattern, and in Japan the palm rarely sets seed anyway, so each named plant was a piece of an older one. Collectors registered a new variety only after the stripes held through three divisions in a row.

Where it comes from

Rain forest, then gardens

Lady palm grows wild in lowland rain forest in southern China, including Hainan, and in northern and central Vietnam. It spreads by underground stems and forms colonies beneath the trees. People have grown it for so long that pressed specimens from Japan are all thought to come from gardens.

A name from one leaf

A Swedish botanist named it in 1784 from a plant in Japan. The specimen that carries the name is a single pressed leaf in Uppsala, Sweden, and since plants in Japan were cultivated, it probably came from a garden plant.

In Manatee County by the 1890s

A Manatee County nursery offered it in an 1890s catalog as a hardy little cane palm, three or four feet tall, for two or three dollars. That is the earliest Florida record we have found.

What it does here

Not an areca

The park's Areca Palm also grows as a clump of slender stems, so the two are easy to mix up from a distance. Areca has feather-shaped fronds, with leaflets down both sides of a central rib. Lady palm leaves open from a single point, like a hand with the fingers spread.

A palm for dim places

In the wild it grows under rain-forest trees, which is why it keeps its color in shade where most palms sulk. In full sun the tips of the oldest leaves tend to brown, and striped forms scorch in bright light.

How to know it

A thicket, not a trunk

Look for many upright canes crowded into one clump, each under an inch thick and wrapped in coarse, dark fiber. Older canes stand taller and younger ones fill in below, so the clump is full from the ground up.

Squared-off fingertips

Each leaf is a fan of four to ten broad, pleated segments joined near the base. The tips are blunt, as if cut straight across, with a few teeth.

How it grows

Wider, not taller

Underground stems send up new canes around the old ones, so a clump slowly widens. Most garden clumps top out somewhere between 7 and 12 feet after many years.

Flowers among the fans

Male and female flowers grow on separate plants, in small branched sprays hidden among the leaves. Female plants carry small white berries.
