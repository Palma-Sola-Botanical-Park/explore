Gap Palm

Cyrtostachys loriae

At a glance

  • The leaf stalk is almost missing. On most palms there is an obvious length of bare stem between the trunk and the leafy part of the frond. Here it can be an inch or two, sometimes barely there at all, so an enormous leaf appears to start right where it joins the plant.
  • In its home forests it can reach about a hundred feet, with single fronds running to fifteen feet or more. Ours has a very long way to go.
  • Five palms once given their own scientific names - collected in different corners of New Guinea, by different botanists - turned out to be this same variable species.
  • It is rarely seen outside specialist palm collections. This is not a palm you will run into in a Florida front garden.

Cultural significance

Five names became one

Botanists working in different parts of New Guinea kept finding what looked like distinct palms and naming them accordingly. When the specimens were finally laid side by side and compared properly, the differences turned out to be ordinary variation within a single wide-ranging species. All five names were folded into this one.

A palm with many names

Its range crosses a great many languages and communities, and it has picked up local names to match. Gap, the name we use here, is one of them - recorded in New Guinea, and now the name on a label in Bradenton.

Where it comes from

Across Papuasia

It grows naturally across New Guinea and east through the Bismarck Archipelago to the Solomon Islands, in wet tropical forest from the lowlands up into the hills.

A palm people built with

This is the part worth knowing. Across New Guinea and the Solomons the stems and leaves have been used for house posts, flooring, water pipes, roofing and even mattresses, and the growing heart has been eaten. What reads here as a rare collection palm is, where it comes from, closer to a hardware store.

Where to find it here

What it does here

Small now, and that is the point

There is no trunk yet, no pale collar, none of the heavy hanging crown this palm is known for - just a handful of very large leaves coming straight out of the ground. Almost everything that makes the species remarkable is still ahead of it.

Go looking for the leaf stalk

One thing you can check today, and it is the best thing about the plant. Follow a frond back from its leafy part toward the centre and try to find the bare stalk that on any ordinary palm holds the leaf out away from the stem. You will struggle. On this one it barely exists.

How to know it

Leaflets that hang

The leaflets droop from the frond rather than standing stiffly out from it, which gives even a young plant a soft, slightly weeping look.

A pale collar, once it has a trunk

On a grown plant there is a long crownshaft below the leaves, pale yellow to light green. That is what separates it from its famous relative the Sealing Wax Palm, whose crownshaft is brilliant scarlet. Ours has no trunk yet, so it has no collar yet either.

One trunk, no clump

It will grow as a single heavy stem with no suckers or offsets around the base. That is worth knowing now, because it rules out the clustering ornamental palms this one could otherwise be mistaken for as it fills out.

How it grows

Built for wet tropical forest

It comes from warm, humid forest and does best with reliable moisture.

Solitary, so every plant starts from seed

One trunk, never any suckers or offsets. There is nothing to divide, so a new plant only ever begins as a seed.

Bradenton is near its cool limit

This is a genuinely tropical palm, and here it is close to the cold end of what it will tolerate outdoors. A hard freeze is the weather that would test it.
