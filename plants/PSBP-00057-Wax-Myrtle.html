Wax Myrtle

Morella cerifera

At a glance

  • A bird is named after eating these. Yellow-rumped Warblers live on the berries through migration, and older field guides call them Myrtle Warblers for it. They can digest the wax coating; most birds cannot.
  • The roots pull nitrogen out of the air and put it into the soil. It is doing the same job as a bean plant, for the benefit of everything growing near it.
  • Caterpillars of the Red-banded Hairstreak and the Polyphemus Moth feed on it.
  • Crush a leaf and it smells like a spice shelf. The leaves have been used the way you would use a bay leaf.

Cultural significance

Bayberry candles

The grey berries are coated in a hard aromatic wax. Colonists boiled it off — a great many berries for a very small candle — and the result burned cleaner than tallow and smelled far better. The botanical name says it outright: cerifera means wax-bearing. Bayberry candles are still on shelves at Christmas.

Where it comes from

All of Florida, and most of the coast

Native through every part of Florida including the Keys, and north and west along the coasts from New Jersey to Texas. It grows in wetland edges, river margins, pine flatwoods, sand dunes and dry uplands — a range of conditions that would finish off most shrubs.

Where to find it here

Around Cypress Pond

They line the pond and run along the back of the park. There is no single specimen to go and look at; they read as a thicket, a green wall at the edge of the water, and that is the point of them.

What it does here

The plant nobody comes to see

Nobody stops for a wax myrtle. They screen the far side of Cypress Pond, they hold the back of the park in, and they get cut back when they lean over the path. That is the whole of the relationship, and it is worth saying plainly rather than pretending otherwise.

Quietly the hardest worker here

Now the other side of it. This is one of the most useful plants on the property: winter food for migrating birds, a host plant for two butterflies and moths, cover and nesting for birds and turtles along the water, roots that hold the pond bank together and feed nitrogen to everything around them. Almost nothing else here does that many jobs at once, and nothing else does it with less notice taken.

How to know it

Crush a leaf

This is the quickest check and it is the pleasant one. The leaves are narrow, olive-green, and toothed toward the tip, and when you rub one between your fingers it releases a sharp resinous smell. Nothing else along that pond edge does it.

Berries along the stem

Not in bunches at the end of a branch — packed directly along the woody stems in tight knobbly lines, pale grey-blue with a waxy bloom you can scrape with a fingernail.

It grows as a crowd

Rarely a single tidy shrub. It suckers and leans and grows into its neighbours until a stand of them reads as one continuous thicket.

How it grows

Fast, tough, and sociable

A big multi-stemmed shrub or small tree, quick to establish and untroubled by wet feet, salt or poor soil. Left alone it spreads into a thicket, which is exactly why it gets planted as screening and exactly why it needs cutting back off a path.

Take care

Not a problem plant

Not toxic to people, dogs or cats. The leaves have a history as a bay leaf substitute, though the berries are not something people eat — those are for the birds, quite literally.
