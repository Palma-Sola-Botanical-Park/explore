Traveller's Palm

Ravenala madagascariensis

At a glance

  • Not a palm. It is a cousin of the Bird of Paradise, from Madagascar, with its huge leaves set in one flat fan like an open hand.
  • The leaf bases overlap into cups that hold rainwater, which is where the traveller's name comes from.
  • In Madagascar, lemurs pry open the flower bracts for nectar and carry the pollen on their faces.
  • The seeds are wrapped in bright blue, one of the rarest colours in the plant world.

Cultural significance

The traveller's compass

The story goes that the fan lines up east to west and a lost traveller can steer by it. The leaves do turn toward the light, but not reliably enough to trust, and the name more likely comes from the water in the leaf bases. In Madagascar the tree is a national emblem: it appears on the country's seal and was for years the logo of the national airline.

Where it comes from

Eastern Madagascar

Traveller's Palm grows wild in the rainforest belt of eastern Madagascar, where it takes over clearings and roadsides and can cover whole hillsides. It was long thought to be the only species in its genus; a 2021 study split Ravenala into six, and this, the one everyone grows, is the common lowland one.

A family of three

Ravenala is the Malagasy name. Its family, the bird-of-paradise family, has only three genera: this one in Madagascar, the Bird of Paradise plants of South Africa, and one tree in the Amazon.

Where to find it here

Big Pond, west bank

On the west bank of Big Pond, right at the water. Follow the walkway along the south side of the pond to the corner where it turns north; the fan stands on the pond side a few steps past the turn, among the rows of Coontie planted along the bank.

What it does here

The fan at the water

This is the plant people photograph from across the pond. Stand on the far bank and the whole fan shows in one plane; stand under it and the leaves close over you like shutters. It flowers here: stiff green bracts, like lobster claws, appear among the leaf bases in summer with the white flowers inside them. What visits those flowers in Bradenton nobody has recorded; the lemurs stayed in Madagascar.

Its cousin across the park

Its nearest relative in the park is the White Bird of Paradise, on Bird of Paradise Island and by the office. Put the two side by side in your mind and the family shows: the same paddle leaves on long stalks, the same boat-shaped bracts, only here the leaves are twice the size and set in a single fan.

How to know it

One flat fan

Every leaf grows in the same plane, in two ranks, so from the side the crown is a flat fan and from the end it is a narrow slab. No palm does this.

Cups at the base

Each leaf stalk wraps the next at the base and forms a deep cup. After rain there is water in it.

Bracts and blue seeds

The flowers come from stiff, boat-shaped green bracts stacked in a row among the leaf bases, the same design as a Bird of Paradise flower with the orange left out. The fruit is a woody capsule that splits to show black seeds in a bright blue coat.

Torn by design

Wind tears the big leaves along their veins into strips. A ragged fan is the usual state of the plant, not a sign of trouble.

How it grows

Built large

It grows fast in warmth and steady water, on a ringed trunk like a palm's, and in Florida can reach 30 feet or more with a fan 15 feet across. Give it room.

New stems at the base

Young stems come up around the base of an older trunk, so a single plant becomes a cluster of fans. Nurseries divide those offshoots or raise it from seed. Frost marks the leaves, and a hard freeze kills it.

Take care

Water in the cups

The water in the leaf bases is rainwater with whatever fell in after it. It is not drinking water.

The seeds

The blue-coated seeds are not food here. Enjoy the colour and leave them.
