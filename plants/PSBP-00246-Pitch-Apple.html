At a glance

  • The park grows it in three places, and in all of them for the same reason: a big, dependable block of green that asks for nothing.
  • Ours have never been seen to fruit. They stay green and leafy, and that is the whole show here.
  • The leaves are thick enough that a fingernail leaves a mark that never fades.
  • Almost every one of these in Florida is female, and sets seed without ever being pollinated. The seedlings are clones of the parent.

Cultural significance

What the resin is for

People boiled the sticky black resin out of the ripe capsules and packed it into the seams of wooden hulls, which is where the name comes from. The tree is not making it for us, though. Its flowers produce no nectar at all — they ooze resin instead, and bees come to collect it as waterproofing and cement for their nests. We found a use for a building material that was already being made for somebody else.

Where it comes from

Florida, and a long argument

It grows through the Caribbean, Central America and down into Colombia and Venezuela, and reaches the United States only in the Keys. Whether those Keys trees are genuinely wild or old escapes from gardens has been argued over since the 1960s and still isn't settled.

Where to find it here

Just outside the main gate

A tall, wide mass of dark green right by the gate, with the live oaks behind it. It has been left to grow big on purpose, and most people read it as part of the hedge line rather than as a plant.

Green at the Serenity Garden

A mainstay there, and a big part of why that space reads as lush year-round.

Nursery parking

Planted to block the view of the water treatment plant.

What it does here

Green, and reliable about it

Every Pitch Apple here was put in to do the same job: hold a large, reliable mass of green. No fuss, no season when it looks bad, no particular demands. It endures. What it does is fill the spaces that would otherwise look like they needed some substance in them.

A few of them are also covering something

The one outside the main gate has been grown tall and wide over the large water manifold, and the nursery planting blocks the view of the water treatment plant. The volunteers know the site water cutoff is in among the gate planting — after the storms somebody had to go in and find it.

How to know it

Leaves in fours

The leaves come in opposite pairs that stack into a cross at each branch tip, each one paddle-shaped with a pale midrib.

Marks that stay

Press a fingernail into one and the scar sets and stays, which is why the tree is also called the Autograph Tree. Nobody here has ever found a signature on ours.

How it grows

Big, dense and multi-stemmed

Multi-trunked, dense, and inclined to spread as wide as it goes high. Roots can drop from the branches and root where they land. Down in the Keys it would make a tree of twenty-five feet or more; here it is grown as a tall shrub mass instead.

At its northern edge

This is a South Florida plant and Bradenton is a half-step colder than the range it prefers. A hard winter can strip it.

Take care

Not food

The fruit is not edible and the sap can irritate skin, though you are unlikely to meet either one here — these plants have not been seen to fruit. If you do spot fruit on one, tell us. We would far rather hear about something we have missed than not hear about it.
