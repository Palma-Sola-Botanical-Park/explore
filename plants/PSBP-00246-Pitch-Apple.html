Pitch Apple

Clusia rosea

At a glance

  • Thick, paddle-shaped leaves, stiff enough that a fingernail leaves a mark that never fades. People have been signing them for generations.
  • The name has nothing to do with apples. Sailors boiled the sticky resin out of the fruit and used it to seal the seams of wooden boats.
  • The park grows it in three places, and in every one of them it is hiding something or filling space.
  • Ours have never been seen to fruit. They stay green and leafy, and that is the whole show here.

Cultural significance

Boat caulk, not fruit

The resin that oozes from a ripening capsule dries black and tacky, and it was once boiled down and packed into the seams of wooden hulls. The fruit was hardware, not food.

Where it comes from

Florida, and a long argument

It grows through the Caribbean, Central America and down into Colombia and Venezuela, and reaches the United States only in the Keys. Whether those Keys trees are genuinely wild or old escapes from gardens has been argued over since the 1960s and still isn't settled.

Where to find it here

Just inside the main gate

Low and wide beside the brick walk, with the live oaks behind it.

Green at the Serenity Garden

A mainstay there, and a big part of why that space reads as lush year-round.

Nursery parking

Planted to block the view of the water treatment plant.

What it does here

The park's workhorse screen

Every Pitch Apple here was put in to do a job. The one at the gate hides the water shutoff valve, which works right up until there is an irrigation leak to isolate and somebody has to get in behind it. The nursery planting blocks the water treatment plant. Only at the Serenity Garden is it simply there to be green, and even then it is filling a space that would otherwise look thin.

How to know it

Leaves in fours

The leaves come in opposite pairs that stack into a cross at each branch tip, each one paddle-shaped with a pale midrib.

Marks that stay

Press a fingernail into one and the scar sets and stays. That is where the other name comes from — people have been signing these leaves in gardens for generations.

How it grows

Wider than tall

Multi-trunked, dense, and inclined to spread. Roots can drop from the branches and root where they land. Left alone in the Keys it would make a tree of twenty-five feet or more; here it is kept as a shrub.

At its northern edge

This is a South Florida plant and Bradenton is a half-step colder than the range it prefers. A hard winter can strip it.

Take care

Not food

The fruit is not edible and the sap can irritate skin. You are unlikely to meet either one here — these plants have not been seen to fruit.
