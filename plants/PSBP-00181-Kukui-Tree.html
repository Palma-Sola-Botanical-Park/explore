Kukui Tree

Aleurites moluccanus

At a glance

  • Look up in summer and the tree is hung with green nuts about the size of a plum. The kernel inside each one is roughly four-fifths oil.
  • That oil is why the tree travelled. It has been used for light, food, medicine, dye, tattoo ink and canoe varnish for a very long time.
  • Hawaii made it the state tree in 1959, the same year it became a state.
  • Do not eat the nuts off the ground. Raw, they will make you seriously ill.

Cultural significance

A lamp and a clock

The kernels were threaded onto a coconut-leaf spine and lit one at a time. Each nut burned for a fairly predictable stretch, so a strung wick did two jobs at once: it lit the room and it measured the evening.

Too useful to leave behind

Polynesian voyagers packed it into the canoes and carried it thousands of miles across open ocean, one of a short list of plants considered essential enough to take. It naturalised in Hawaii so completely that most people assume it was always there.

You have probably already met it

The oil is still pressed for soap and skin care. Anyone who has picked up a bottle in a Hawaiian gift shop has met this tree without knowing it.

Where it comes from

Not from Hawaii

It is native to island Southeast Asia, most likely the Moluccas. People moved it so early and so thoroughly that its original wild range is now largely guesswork.

Where to find it here

In the open field by the Galleria

It stands out in the field rather than in a planted bed, so you get the whole crown at once. In summer the easiest way to confirm you have the right tree is to look up into the branches for the nuts.

What it does here

It actually fruits

This tree carries a real crop here, which is not a given for a tropical Pacific species this far north. In a good year the canopy is thick with nuts and the ground underneath collects what falls.

Worth knowing if you have a child along

Those fallen nuts look like something you could crack and eat. Raw, they are not.

How to know it

Leaves that change their mind

Young leaves are broad and lobed, a bit like a maple or an ivy. Older ones on the same tree often settle into a plain oval. Both shapes hang there at once.

Paler than its neighbours

Fine star-shaped hairs coat the new growth and the leaf undersides, giving the crown a dusty, silvery cast. In Hawaii a grove on a hillside is visible from a long way off, and travellers steered by them.

How it grows

Fast and broad

It gets up quickly into a wide, rounded crown, often about as wide as it is tall.

Take care

Never eat a raw one

Raw kernels cause severe vomiting and cramps. Cooking breaks the compounds down, which is exactly why roasting turned this into food in Hawaii and Indonesia, but that is not something to experiment with. The sap can also irritate skin.
