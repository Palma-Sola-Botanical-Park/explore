At a glance

  • It was patented in 1989 before anyone knew where it came from. An Australian couple raised it from a seed they took for a sport of a common philodendron; botanists described it as a new species in 2002 from a plant in a West Palm Beach garden, origin unknown. It is now placed in southern Brazil and Paraguay.
  • It does flower, low in the clump where nobody looks: a dull dark red sheath about five inches long, pale pink and cream inside, several times a year.
  • Count the fingers on a leaf. An old leaf has fifteen to twenty; a young plant's only ten to sixteen. Find the oldest leaf and the newest and compare.
  • It goes by three scientific names at once. One authority calls it Thaumatophyllum, Greek for wonder-leaf; another keeps it in Philodendron; a third treats it as a garden hybrid.

Cultural significance

A trade name from a poem

The patent called it 'Winterbourn', after the couple who raised it. The Australian sellers who took it on renamed it Xanadu, the pleasure-dome of Coleridge's poem. They never said why.

Where it comes from

Southern Brazil, found late

Its home is the south of Brazil and Paraguay. The plant reached gardens first and botany second: by the time it was named as a wild species, it had already been in nurseries for more than a decade.

What it does here

A clump, not a climber

Unlike the climbing philodendrons, this one stays on the ground as a slowly widening clump. Its short, thick stems are patterned with the scars of old leaves and send out thick roots.

Wonder-leaf

The name Thaumatophyllum means wonder-leaf, from the Greek. It was coined in 1859 for a relative, long before this plant was known, and applied to it in 2018 when the group was split off from Philodendron.

How to know it

Deep green cut leaves

The leaves are glossy, deep green, up to a foot and a half long, and cut almost to the midrib into many lobes. They stand on long stalks that meet at the center of the clump. A young plant's leaves are far less divided.

How it grows

Shade and warmth

It grows fast in light shade or filtered sun and reaches two to four feet high and wider than that. Cold in the mid-thirties damages it. New plants come from dividing the clump.

Take care

Burns the mouth

Chewing any part burns the mouth, for people and pets alike, and the sap can irritate skin.
