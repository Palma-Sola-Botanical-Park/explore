Pindo Palm

Butia odorata

At a glance

  • The ripe fruit is fragrant enough to have given the species its name: odorata means perfumed. When Barbosa Rodrigues formally described it in 1891, he called the fruit pulp highly aromatic.
  • Those blue-gray fronds bend outward and then sweep back toward the trunk, giving the whole palm a dense fountain shape you can read from across the garden.
  • This tropical-looking feather palm can handle brief temperatures around ten degrees Fahrenheit — far colder than most palms with this look.
  • Cross a Pindo Palm with a Queen Palm and you get a Mule Palm — a sterile hybrid that mixes traits from both parents and is grown for its size, feather leaves, and cold tolerance.

Cultural significance

Jelly Palm really means jelly

The fruit is eaten fresh and turned into jelly, juice, and liquor. In Uruguay, people also roast and grind the kernels for a drink known as coconut coffee.

More than a garden palm

In eastern Uruguay, Butia odorata grows across enormous natural palm groves called palmares. Around Rocha, those groves cover tens of thousands of acres and are woven into the local landscape, food, and culture.

Where it comes from

Pindo Palm is native to the far south of Brazil and Uruguay, where it grows in subtropical grasslands and palm-grove landscapes that can look nothing like the tropical forest people picture when they hear the word palm.

The palm groves have a problem

Some old Butia groves in Uruguay are full of mature palms but very few young ones. Grazing can keep seedlings from establishing, so a landscape can still look full of palms while slowly losing the next generation.

The name that fooled nurseries

For decades this palm was commonly sold as Butia capitata. That name belongs to a different palm from central Brazil. The familiar Pindo or Jelly Palm of cultivation is Butia odorata, and the name was formally validated in 2011.

Where to find it here

We have two of very different ages, which makes them worth comparing.

The young one

It grows in the sunny open ground south of the Plumeria field. It is still small, a low clump of leaves with no trunk yet, so don't look for the big blue-gray fountain. Look low.

The mature one

It stands outside the fence. Leave through the back gate by the nursery, turn left, and you'll see it.

What it does

This palm looks far more tropical than its climate history suggests. The blue-gray feather leaves belong to a species from the cooler end of South America, which helps explain why Pindo Palm handles Florida cold better than most palms with this look.

How to know it

Go by the whole shape

Step back first. The blue-gray fronds arch out and then curl back toward the trunk, giving the crown a dense fountain shape. That silhouette is usually the quickest clue.

A deep V in every frond

The narrow leaflets sit at an angle along the leaf stem rather than lying flat, so each frond has a pronounced V-shaped profile when you look along it.

The trunk stays knobby

Old leaf bases hang on for years, building the rough, chunky pattern that covers much of the trunk on younger palms.

Orange fruit in heavy clusters

When it fruits, look for large hanging clusters of yellow to orange fruit about an inch across. The fruit is edible and can become one of the easiest clues on a mature palm.

How it grows

Pindo is slow. A mature landscape palm is usually around fifteen to twenty feet tall, so the thick trunk and full crown take years to build.

Light changes the look

In stronger sun the crown tends to stay tighter and grayer. With more shade, the leaves can stretch longer and look greener, so two Pindo Palms may not read exactly alike.

Florida is almost too tropical

It survives perfectly well in south Florida, but Pindo Palm actually grows better in the cooler, less humid parts of the state. That makes sense once you know its home is southern Brazil and Uruguay, not the equatorial tropics.

Take care

Watch the leaf stalks

The leaf stalks are armed with stiff spines near the trunk. They are easy to miss under the feather leaves, so look before reaching into the crown or pruning old fronds.
