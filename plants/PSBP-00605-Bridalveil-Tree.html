Bridalveil Tree

Libidibia punctata
On this page

At a glance

  • The name is about the leaves, not the flowers. Thousands of tiny paired leaflets on slender stems make a fine green haze that moves in a breeze, and from a distance the whole crown reads as a veil.
  • The bark, when the tree is older, is the show: it peels in thin papery strips to bare mottled patches of tan, grey and green. The green is living tissue that makes food from light, briefly unmasked. Ours is too young to show it yet.
  • At home in Colombia and Venezuela it is granadillo, a cabinet timber, dense and dark. Here it will never be big enough to cut, and nobody would want to.
  • Yellow flowers in upright clusters from late spring to autumn, with bees and butterflies at them.

Where to find it here

In the Galleria Field, a young tree about twelve feet tall, still held up by guy lines, with a sign at its foot.

Where it comes from

Northern South America

Seasonally dry forest in Colombia and Venezuela. It is a legume, a cousin of the Royal Poinciana and the tamarind, and it still goes by its old name Caesalpinia granadillo in most Florida nurseries.

What it does

Young, staked, and worth the wait

A tree still finding its feet. It went in as a sapling and the stakes are there because a young Bridalveil has a habit of forking low, and those forks need shaping while the wood is soft. In summer the yellow flowers bring bees. Bradenton is at the cold edge of where this tree is happy, so a hard freeze is the thing to watch for. If it comes through a few winters it will be one of the finest-textured shade trees in the park.

How to know it

Leaflets in pairs

Each leaf is divided and divided again into small oval leaflets in neat pairs, so a branch looks feathered.

Bark, later

Smooth and grey on ours. The peeling, mottled bark comes with age. Come back for it.

Yellow flowers, flat pods

Upright clusters of small yellow five-petalled flowers with long stamens, then flat pods two or three inches long.

How it grows

Full sun and drainage; it does not mind sand. Moderate speed to thirty feet or so, in a vase shape that often forks low. Early pruning every few years sets it up to hold together in a storm.
