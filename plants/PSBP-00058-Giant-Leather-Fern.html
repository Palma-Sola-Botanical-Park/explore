Giant Leather Fern

Acrostichum danaeifolium
On this page

At a glance

  • Florida's biggest fern. Fronds reach six to twelve feet, and among the fifty states the genus grows wild only in Florida.
  • The brown fuzz under some fronds is not rot. It is a solid carpet of spore cases; those are the fertile fronds, and they are meant to look that way.
  • Three grow on our pond banks, two at Front Pond and one at Cypress Pond, leaning out over the water and holding the edge together.
  • It stands in water, takes salt and shrugs off flooding. Drought is the one thing it cannot take.

Where it comes from

Florida and the tropical Americas

Native to Florida and south through the Caribbean, Central America and much of South America to Brazil. In Florida it grows throughout the peninsula and the Keys, in freshwater and brackish marshes, along mangrove edges, in cypress swamps and wet hammocks. Cold keeps it out of the rest of the country: no other state has a wild leather fern.

Two Florida leather ferns

Florida has a second species, Golden Leather Fern, a plant of the coast and the mangroves. On that one only the upper leaflets of a frond are fertile. On ours a fertile frond is brown from top to bottom.

Where to find it here

Front Pond

Two grow at Front Pond. The easier one to see is on the west side, the side nearest the nursery, leaning out over the water; the other is across the pond, near the entrance loop of the drive.

Cypress Pond

A third stands at Cypress Pond. In spring 2026 a young fern was found beside that pond as well, possibly the first new one to come up here on its own.

Look from across the water

They lean out over the pond, so the whole fountain of fronds is easier to take in from the far bank than from beside the base.

What it does

Holding the bank

These ferns were planted for their roots as much as their size. The rhizome mat under each clump knits the pond edge together where the bank would otherwise wash away. They ask nothing in return: no watering, no feeding, and a flooded bank does not bother them.

Please don't tidy them

Every so often someone wants to trim the brown fronds off. Those are the plant's reproductive fronds, and cutting them takes away the spores. The brown is the point.

Cover at the water line

The arching fronds make a shaded pocket at the water's edge that small fish, frogs and turtles use.

How to know it

Start with the size

A fountain of coarse, leathery fronds, waist high on a young plant and well over head height on an old one, usually with its feet in the water.

Two kinds of frond

Turn one over. A sterile frond is green underneath. A fertile frond is covered edge to edge in brown, velvety spore cases: no spots, no clusters, one even layer. Both kinds grow on the same plant, and both are healthy.

Big plain leaflets

Each frond is divided once into long, smooth-edged leaflets a foot or more long, set in two rows along the stem.

Wet feet

It grows where the ground is wet or flooded: pond margins, marshes, ditches, mangrove edges. A fern this size on dry ground is something else.

How it grows

Water first

It likes standing water and takes weeks of flooding. Full sun is fine at the water's edge; away from water it does better with some shade. Drought is what it cannot take.

Salt is fine

Unlike most ferns it tolerates brackish water and salty ground, which is why it grows along mangrove edges.

From a rhizome

No trunk. New fronds rise from a thick rhizome at ground level, and the clump widens slowly as the rhizome spreads.

Spores, not seeds

No flowers, no seeds. Spores shed from the fertile fronds, and for years none of them took on our banks. The young fern at Cypress Pond may be the first.
