At a glance

  • In summer a mature Royal Poinciana can cover its broad crown with red-orange flowers. Ours bloom heavily enough that visitors comment every year.
  • Look closely at a flower: four petals are red-orange, while the upright fifth petal is marked with white or yellow and red.
  • After the flowers come woody seed pods one to two feet long. Our trees produce them heavily, and by late summer the pods can be more conspicuous than the remaining flowers.
  • Two of our Royal Poincianas frame the Serenity Garden wedding site, with our Baobab between them.

Cultural significance

Flamboyant

Around the world this tree is the Flamboyant, from the French. The English name honors de Poincy, a 17th-century French governor of St. Kitts, where the tree is still the national flower.

A homeland nobody knew

It was planted across the tropics for more than a century before wild trees were found in Madagascar in 1932. The name and the tree travelled the world long before anyone knew where it came from.

Where it comes from

From Madagascar

Royal Poinciana is native to the seasonally dry forests of northern and western Madagascar. It has since been planted throughout the tropics and subtropics, Florida included.

Where to find it here

One greets you near the front pond

As you enter by car and round the first curve, a Royal Poinciana is likely to be the first large specimen tree you notice. Its broad, low branching gives you a good view of the tree's spreading form. It is also the tree people photograph. Nearly every Royal Poinciana picture taken in the park is of this one; the two bigger trees at the Serenity Garden hardly get noticed as trees.

Two frame the Serenity Garden

Behind the Galleria, two larger Royal Poincianas stand at the Serenity Garden, our most commonly used wedding ceremony setting. The ceremony is framed by the two trees, with our Baobab between them and Big Pond to the side.

The biggest one is the one nobody sees

The park has five in all. A small one grows in the nursery parking area, and the biggest of them shades the nursery potting table, where no visitor goes. That one is for the volunteers who work under it.

When

Summer is for flowers. By September the bloom is passing and the pods take over. Our trees flower heavily and pod even more heavily.

What it does here

Part of the wedding setting

The two mature trees at the Serenity Garden are not simply background plantings. Their spreading crowns frame the space where many Palma Sola wedding ceremonies take place, with the Baobab centered between them.

Winter Nights under the Lights

All three Royal Poincianas become part of the park's holiday display. The smaller tree near the entrance has low, reachable branches that a high-school team decorates with colored lights. The two Serenity Garden trees are too big for that; those are lit by people with the equipment to reach them.

How to know it

Look at the whole tree

Mature Royal Poincianas spread outward as much as upward, developing a broad, umbrella-like crown with long, often nearly horizontal branches.

Find the different petal

Each large flower has five petals. Four are red to orange-red; the upper petal stands apart, marked with white or creamy yellow and red.

Ferny from a distance

Each apparent spray of foliage is actually a large leaf divided once into branches and then again into dozens of tiny leaflets. That double division gives the crown its fine, feathery texture.

When the flowers are gone

Look for the pods. They are flat, dark brown and woody, often one to two feet long, and can remain hanging from the branches long after flowering.

How it grows

Built outward

Royal Poinciana grows quickly into a broad, spreading tree, often becoming wider than it is tall. That architecture explains both the wedding canopy made by our mature trees and the reachable branches on our younger entrance specimen.

Flowers give way to pods

Royal Poinciana belongs to the bean family. After flowering, successful flowers develop into flattened pods that lengthen and eventually become dark, hard and woody. Each contains numerous hard seeds.

Sun matters

The tree grows and flowers best in full sun and well-drained ground. Its wide-spreading roots and broad, sometimes brittle branching also mean it needs room, something our open lawn and Serenity Garden specimens have.
