Staghorn Fern

Platycerium
On this page

At a glance

  • It makes two kinds of frond: flat shields that clasp its support in papery layers, and green antlers that arch out from them.
  • It never needs soil. In the wild it lives on tree trunks, catching rain and fallen leaves in its own fronds.
  • No flowers and no seeds: it spreads by spores, shed from brown patches under the antler tips.
  • Some of ours arrived as donations so big they had to be moved by tractor.

Where to find it here

Look in the Pavilion Field, at the gazebo by the pavilion, and in the Shade Garden. Some hang in the forks of trees; others sit at ground level, wrapped around the base of a palm.

Where it comes from

Staghorn ferns grow wild in the tropics of Africa, Madagascar, Southeast Asia, New Guinea and Australia, with one species in South America. In forest they perch on trunks and branches rather than growing in soil.

A Florida porch favorite

In South Florida they are a fixture of gardens and porches, hung on boards and tree trunks. One common kind has escaped onto trees in wild hammocks there.

What it does

Donations too big to carry

Many of our staghorns were given to the park, and some came so large they needed a tractor. Every so often staff and student volunteers divide a big colony into smaller plants and mount them on boards for our plant sales.

From colony to board

A young plant is cut away from the back of the colony with its roots, set on a pad of moist moss on a board, and tied in place until its new shields grip on their own.

How to know it

The whole outline

A rounded mass of overlapping shields, with forked green fronds hanging or arching out from it like antlers.

Brown is normal

The shields start green and turn brown and papery as they age, and the fuzzy brown patches under the antler tips are spores, not rot.

How it grows

Catching its own compost

New shields grow in layers over the old ones, and their upper edges stand up like a basket. They catch rain, fallen leaves and debris, and as that rots it feeds the roots tucked behind them. A colony at the foot of a palm is living on the trunk the way it would on a forest tree.

Spores, not seeds

The brown felt under the antler tips is made of countless spores. A spore first grows into a tiny, green, heart-shaped plant, and only that can produce a new fern.

Small new plants also form at the edges of the shields. That is how a colony fills out, and how it can be divided.
