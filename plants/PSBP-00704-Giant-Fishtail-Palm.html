At a glance

  • Caryota is the only palm genus with fully bipinnate, or twice-divided, leaves.
  • Giant Fishtail Palm is native from the eastern Himalaya and northeastern India through mainland Southeast Asia to southern Yunnan.
  • The accepted name is Caryota obtusa. Caryota gigas, still seen in horticulture, is a synonym.
  • The palm flowers down the trunk in a terminal sequence. When the stem finishes flowering and fruiting, it dies.

Where it comes from

Eastern Himalaya to southern Yunnan

Its native range runs from Arunachal Pradesh and the eastern Himalaya through Bangladesh, Myanmar, Laos, Thailand and Vietnam to southern Yunnan in China.

Where to find it here

The palm row south of the park

In the row of unusual palms in the southern part of the park — from the Galleria, head south past the Serenity Garden.

What it does here

A palm that breaks two palm rules at once

Most palms have fan leaves or once-divided feather leaves, and most familiar landscape palms keep flowering year after year. Giant Fishtail Palm instead has twice-divided leaves and a life-ending flowering sequence.

How to know it

Find the fish tails

Individual leaflets are wedge-shaped with jagged, blunt outer edges — visible even on the park's young plant.

Then look at the whole frond

The leaf is divided into branches that are themselves divided into leaflets: a bipinnate structure unique to Caryota among palms.

One massive stem

This species is solitary rather than clumping — ours is still a young plant with no trunk yet. Some “giant” fishtail, huh? Not so much yet.

Flowering moves downward

On a reproductive stem, older inflorescences are higher and progressively younger ones occur lower down.

How it grows

A wet-tropical Asian giant

It grows primarily in wet tropical vegetation.

The flowering sequence is terminal

Stems die after flowering. In this solitary species, that means the visible palm's single trunk eventually dies.

Take care

Do not handle the fruit with bare hands

UF/IFAS palm-seed guidance specifically recommends gloves when handling Caryota fruit. The pulp contains irritating calcium-oxalate crystals that can burn skin and mouth tissues. Leave fallen or hanging fruit alone.
