Lala Palm

Hyphaene coriacea

At a glance

  • Lala Palm can fork its trunk, sometimes more than once. Branching trunks are unusual enough in palms to make this a strong field clue.
  • It also suckers from the base, so one plant can develop a broad multi-stemmed form.
  • The leaf stalks carry dark triangular spines.
  • It is native across parts of eastern and southern Africa, including western Madagascar and several western Indian Ocean islands.

Cultural significance

A working palm in southern Africa

In Mozambique and neighboring regions, people harvest Lala Palm leaves for baskets, mats, roofing and other woven goods. Sap is also tapped locally for palm wine. The species is part of everyday material culture as well as natural vegetation.

Where to find it here

The palm field south of Cypress Pond

In the row of palms next to the Plumeria planting, just south of Cypress Pond. Head south through the park and you'll find a whole field of unusual palms — Lala Palm is in the southwest corner of it. (That is a different field from the Royal Palms in the northeast.)

Look for forks

If the plant has begun branching, that is a more useful clue than leaf color.

Where it comes from

Eastern and southern Africa

Its native range spans from eastern Africa southward into Mozambique and South Africa, with populations on Mayotte, Juan de Nova and western Madagascar.

What it does

A palm that breaks the one-trunk rule

Visitors often learn that palms grow from a single terminal bud and do not branch like ordinary trees. Lala Palm is a useful exception: its stems can divide into forks while the plant also sends up new shoots from below.

How to know it

Look for a fork

Older stems can divide into two branches, sometimes repeating the pattern.

A clump as well as branches

New shoots also emerge from the base, so branching above ground and suckering below can occur on the same plant.

Stiff fan leaves

The crown carries broad fan-shaped leaves on long petioles.

Dark spines on the stalk

Triangular blackish spines line the petiole margins.

How it grows

Built for seasonally dry landscapes

This species is widespread in warm African savannas and coastal regions rather than confined to rainforest.

Harvest can be repeated, within limits

Leaf cutting for weaving and sap tapping are long-standing uses, but studies in Mozambique examine how harvest intensity affects growth and regeneration. Traditional use does not mean unlimited removal is harmless.

Broad range, lower global concern

It carries a Least Concern conservation assessment, which fits its much wider distribution than the island-endemic palms elsewhere in this collection.
