At a glance

  • Our Royal Palms were county nursery stock, left behind when Manatee County stopped growing its own street trees. They rooted through their pots where they stood.
  • The smooth green column below the leaves isn't trunk. It's a sleeve of wrapped leaf bases called the crownshaft.
  • It drops its own old leaves, base and all, one about every month, which is why the trunk stays so clean.
  • The genus is named for an American army engineer, General Roy Stone.

Cultural significance

Cuba's tree

In Cuba the royal palm is the national tree and stands on the coat of arms. Its leaves thatched country houses, and the tough leaf sheaths, called yaguas, were used to wrap bales of tobacco.

A palm for boulevards

In the 1800s royal palms were planted to line Havana's grand avenues, and the idea crossed the water. Thomas Edison brought royal palms from Cuba to line the road past his winter home in Fort Myers, now McGregor Boulevard, which is how Fort Myers became the City of Palms.

Named for a road builder

The botanist O. F. Cook named the genus Roystonea in 1900 for General Roy Stone, an army engineer whose road building in Puerto Rico had won the people's admiration.

Where it comes from

It is native to South Florida, the Caribbean, Mexico and Central America. In Florida it grows wild only in the far south, in wet hammocks and swamps like the Fakahatchee Strand, where it rises well above the forest canopy. Ours stand well north of that wild range.

Where to find it here

Follow the driveway from the Galleria to the white trellis with the direction signpost, and go through it. The field straight ahead is the Royal Palms.

What it does

Left in their pots

These palms were grown here for Manatee County's streets, back when the park was a county nursery. When the county stopped growing its own trees, they were simply left where they stood, outgrew their pots and rooted. That's why the rows wander: mostly two rows, with others scattered among them, the way a nursery sets out stock rather than the way anyone plants an avenue.

Shade and a skipper

Today the field gives the Royal Palms their name on our map and a tall, airy shade over the lawn. Monk Skipper caterpillars feed on palm leaves, and royal palms are among their hosts, and birds come for the small dark fruits.

How to know it

Read the outline

One tall, pale gray trunk, smooth as poured concrete and often swollen near the base or partway up, with a tuft of arching feather leaves on top.

The green sleeve

Just below the leaves the trunk changes to a glossy green column. That is the crownshaft: the wrapped bases of the living leaves. The line where gray turns to green is the giveaway.

Rings and flowers

Faint rings around the gray trunk mark where old leaves were attached. Flower stalks burst from just below the crownshaft in long hanging sprays of tiny white flowers, followed by small dark fruits.

How it grows

One leaf at a time

A new leaf rises from the center of the crownshaft while the oldest one at the bottom dies. When it falls, its sheath peels away with it, so no dead leaves hang on the trunk.

Fast and tall

It grows fast and commonly reaches fifty to seventy feet. The bulges in the trunk are normal: they mark years when growing conditions were better or worse than usual.

Take care

Falling leaves

Old leaves come down without warning, and one can be ten to fifteen feet long and heavy. Don't linger right under the crowns on a windy day.
