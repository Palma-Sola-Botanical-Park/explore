Jungle Flame

Ixora coccinea

At a glance

  • The name Ixora probably comes, by way of Portuguese, from Ishvara, "lord", a Sanskrit name for Shiva. In southern India the flowers are temple offerings.
  • Each flower is a four-pointed star at the end of a slender tube an inch or more long. The nectar sits at the bottom, where a butterfly's tongue can reach it.
  • In Thailand, students bring ixora to their teachers each year. The needle-like buds stand for a sharp mind.
  • Yellow leaves with green veins are a common sight on Florida ixoras. Our alkaline soils keep the roots from taking up enough iron and manganese.

Cultural significance

Flowers for the temple

In Kerala the shrub is chethi, and its flowers have long been offered in Hindu temples. The Dutch recorded the custom on the Malabar coast in the 1670s. The botanical name grew out of the same worship.

Named from a drawing

The species officially rests on a drawing of chethi published in 1679 in Hortus Malabaricus, the great Dutch flora of the Kerala coast. Linnaeus gave it its name in 1753.

Needles for the teacher

In Thailand ixora is dok khem, the needle flower. At the Wai Khru ceremony each year, students carry flower trays to their teachers, and the long, pointed buds are there to stand for a sharp mind.

Where it comes from

India to Indochina

It grows wild in western and southern India and Sri Lanka, and from Bangladesh east through Myanmar and Thailand to Cambodia and Vietnam. Gardeners have carried it throughout the tropics and selected forms in yellow, pink, orange and white, some dwarf, some tall.

In Florida

It is a standard landscape shrub in South Florida and now and then escapes from gardens. A state risk assessment rates it as a moderate risk, to be watched.

What it does here

Tube and tongue

The long tube keeps the nectar out of easy reach, so the flower suits butterflies and moths best. In a study in Kerala, butterflies made more than two-thirds of the visits, and their tongue lengths tracked the tube length almost exactly. Bees and sunbirds visit too, and in Florida it draws butterflies and hummingbirds.

Coffee cousins

It is in the coffee family, along with our Firebush, Pentas and Noni. Firebush and Pentas also make tubular flowers for long-tongued visitors, so the three make a good side-by-side: red tubes on a native shrub, star clusters on a bedding plant, and this one from South Asia.

How to know it

A ball of stars

Flowers come packed into rounded heads four to six inches across, at the very ends of the branches. Each flower is a long tube that opens into four pointed lobes, spread in a cross. Ours are red to red-orange.

Count the points

Count the points on one flower: four, every time. Pentas, its cousin, has five.

Paired, glossy, nearly stalkless

Leaves come in opposite pairs, leathery and glossy, one and a half to three inches long, sitting almost directly on the stem. New leaves come out bronze and darken to deep green. Between the bases of each pair sits a small stiff point, the stipule, a coffee-family trait.

How it grows

Flowers at the tips

Flower heads form at the tips of the branches, so every shearing cuts off the next round of flowers. A tightly clipped hedge flowers less than a loosely pruned one.

Yellow between the veins

Ixora wants acid soil. In Florida's alkaline soils, and especially next to concrete, new leaves turn yellow between green veins because the roots can't take up enough iron and manganese. Micronutrient sprays and chelates help, but a yellow leaf is a clue, not a diagnosis.

Warm and frost-tender

It grows best in sun with regular water and flowers through the warm months. Frost and freezes injure it, so it does best in the southern half of Florida. Left unpruned, some forms reach 10 to 15 feet.
