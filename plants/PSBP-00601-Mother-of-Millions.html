Mother of Millions

Kalanchoe delagoensis
On this page

At a glance

  • The tiny plants clustered at the tips of the leaves are not seeds. Each one is a baby copy of the parent, and it roots wherever it falls. That is how it earned the name Mother of Millions.
  • Every part is poisonous if eaten, to people, pets and livestock. In Australia, cattle have died from eating it, and the flowers are the most poisonous part.
  • It does not stay put. In the cactus and succulent garden here, its plantlets root beyond its bed and we have trouble keeping it confined.

Cultural significance

A name that traveled

Delagoensis points to Delagoa Bay, now Maputo Bay in Mozambique, where the plant described in 1837 was collected. The plant itself comes from Madagascar, across the channel. The name records where it was collected, not where it comes from.

An argument that lasted 190 years

That 1837 description was so short that botanists argued for about 190 years over whether the name counted. In the meantime many books called the plant Kalanchoe tubiflora, and some still do. A botanical committee finally ruled that the first name was valid.

Where it comes from

Bare granite in Madagascar

At home it grows mostly on open granite outcrops and rocky slopes in the drier south, in full sun.

Out of the garden and down the gully

Gardeners carried it to warm countries around the world. In 1940 it was reported escaping around a railway settlement in Queensland, Australia, where workers grew it because it needed so little water. Visitors took plants home from a tennis tournament, and plants thrown on rubbish dumps washed down the gullies. In 1988 the district declared it a noxious weed. It has escaped from gardens in Florida too.

What it does here

Bells in March

Open orange-red flowers hung from tall stalks here in March 2026, with brown, spent ones among them. Each bell is a tube about an inch long that opens at the bottom.

Visitors to the bells

Sunbirds have been reported at the flowers in Madagascar and honey bees in Australia.

How to know it

Follow a leaf to its tip

The leaves are narrow, grooved and nearly round in cross-section, gray-green with violet-brown spots, 1 to 6 inches long and only about one-eighth to one-quarter inch wide. They often grow in pairs or three to a level around the stem. The plantlets sit at the tip, among 3 to 9 small teeth, and nowhere else on the leaf.

Not the broad-leaved ones

A relative called Mother of Thousands has flat, wide leaves with plantlets all along the edges. A hybrid of the two, first made by hand in California in 1935, is in between and is also called Mother of Millions.

A tall, spare silhouette

A single unbranched stem, usually between 1½ and 3 feet tall, stands up with the leaves in tiers like spokes.

How it grows

Buds waiting in the notches

Each plantlet starts as a tiny bud waiting in a notch at the leaf tip. While the days are long the buds wake and the plantlets drop in a steady rain, and the shedding stops when the plant is about to flower.

Flowers take two seasons

In greenhouse tests, plants needed two rounds of long days followed by short days before they flowered. Here, buds were showing on Christmas Day 2025 and open flowers by March 20, 2026.

Seed as well as plantlets

The flowers set seed, and a lot of it: an Australian study estimated up to 20,000 seeds from one plant. Which does more to spread a colony, plantlets or seed, has not been measured. A plant tends to die back after it flowers, and the plantlets and seedlings around it take over.

Take care

Poisonous if eaten

The poison acts on the heart. In people and pets, eating it can cause vomiting and diarrhea and can upset the heartbeat. If anyone eats any, call a doctor, a vet or poison control right away.
