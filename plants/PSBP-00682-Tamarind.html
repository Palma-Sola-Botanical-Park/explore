At a glance

  • It has no close relatives anywhere. Tamarindus is a genus containing exactly one species, and this is it.
  • You have almost certainly eaten it already. Tamarind pulp is one of the things in Worcestershire sauce.
  • The pod refuses to open. The rest of the pea family splits neatly along a seam to drop its seeds; this one locks them inside a brittle shell and waits for something to break it.
  • People eat the pulp freely. Dogs must not — it carries the same acid that makes grapes dangerous to them, at many times the strength.

Cultural significance

A name that hid where it came from

It is an African tree, from the dry savannas south of the Sahara. It reached India so early that by the time anyone wrote it down it had been part of the cooking and the medicine there for thousands of years. Arab traders meeting it in India called it tamar hindi — Indian date — and when Linnaeus described it in 1753 he was working from Indian material and named it indica. The name has been pointing at the wrong continent ever since.

The sour note

Tamarind is what a great deal of the world reaches for when food needs to be sour. It runs through Indian cooking, through drinks across Latin America and the Caribbean, and through the bottle of Worcestershire in the fridge door.

Where it comes from

The dry belt of tropical Africa

Native to the seasonally dry savanna running from Sudan and Ethiopia west across sub-Saharan Africa. It is built for a long dry season and a short wet one.

Where to find it here

Near the bridge, south end of Rare Fruit

Walk the Rare Fruit garden toward its south end and the bridge. The tamarind is on your left as you get closer. Ours is still young, so look for a small tree rather than the broad domed thing it becomes.

What it does here

It manages on its own

A good many of the fruit trees here need company. The papaya up the path needs a male plant nearby; the lychee cannot pollinate itself at all. Tamarind is self-fertile, so one tree standing by itself is enough. In a collection where a lot of things are waiting on a partner, this one is not.

How to know it

A lacy canopy

The leaves are divided into many small paired leaflets set in neat rows, which gives the whole tree a fine, feathery look — far more delicate than you would expect from something that grows this big.

The leaflets fold up

Like many in the pea family, the leaflets close against each other at night and reopen in the morning.

Pods that stay shut

The fruit is a fat, cinnamon-brown pod with a brittle shell. It will not split on its own. Inside, the seeds sit in a dense sticky acidic pulp — the part people want.

How it grows

Slow, and eventually very wide

Growth is unhurried. In time it makes forty to sixty feet with a broad dome of a crown and branch tips that arch over and hang down. It needs real room, which is worth knowing while it is still small.

Take care

Safe for you, serious for your dog

The pulp is food and has been for thousands of years, with no concern for people at all. Dogs are a different matter. Tamarind is among the richest natural sources of tartaric acid — the compound behind grape and raisin poisoning in dogs — at many times the level found in grapes. A moderate number of pods can cause acute kidney injury, and vets treat it as an emergency. Keep dogs away from fallen pods.
