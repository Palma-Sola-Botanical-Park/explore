Golden Pothos

Epipremnum aureum
On this page

At a glance

  • It almost never flowers. The gene for the last step in making its flowering hormone is switched off, and one spray of that hormone brings flowers in about two months.
  • The big split leaves on our vine and the little hearts in a windowsill pot are the same plant. Climbing is what turns one into the other.
  • With no flowers to go on, botanists filed it in the wrong genus for more than 80 years.
  • Escaped vines climb hammock trees and carpet the ground across central and south Florida, where it is listed as invasive.

Cultural significance

Named from a cutting

It was first named in 1880, as a Pothos, from young plants in a Belgian nursery that were said to come from the Solomon Islands, a claim nobody has been able to back up. In 1908 it was moved to Scindapsus, still without a flower. It finally bloomed in Puerto Rico and in Miami in the early 1960s, and those flowers showed it belonged in Epipremnum, where it has been since 1964. The old name pothos simply stuck.

Named twice

Meanwhile, in 1899, a French naval doctor had described a wild climber from the forests of Mo'orea as a new species. It took more than a century to settle that his wild vine and the nursery plant were the same thing.

A cutting of a cutting

Almost every pothos ever sold started as a piece of another one. The familiar varieties are sports of the same few plants: Jade, the all-green one, is a reversion of Marble Queen. The first pothos grown from seed for breeding came from the University of Florida, which sprayed plants with the flowering hormone, pollinated the flowers by hand and patented a seedling in 2025.

Clean air?

A 1989 NASA study put golden pothos in a sealed chamber and found it pulled pollutants from the air. A 2019 review of thirty years of such tests worked out what that means for a room: somewhere between ten and a hundred pots on every square foot of floor to match an ordinary air exchange.

Where it comes from

One island

Its wild home is Mo'orea, a small volcanic island in French Polynesia, where it climbs the trees of the lowland forest. The houseplant trade has carried it to every warm country since, and it has gone wild from Hawaii and Sri Lanka to South Africa, where one survey counted about 187,000 plants on less ground than these ten acres.

Loose in Florida

It has been on Florida's invasive plant list since at least 1997. Escaped vines climb hammock oaks and run across the ground until they blanket the ferns and orchids underneath. Cut one at the base and the vine up in the tree sends down new roots within the season, so it has to be killed one node at a time.

What it does here

A houseplant at full size

Ours has done outdoors what the pot on the shelf never gets the chance to do. It has climbed a tall trunk and hidden it in a column of adult leaves, cut in from the edge. Stand at the base and look straight up it.

How it gets out

Pothos escapes in Florida the easy way: cuttings dumped in the woods behind a yard take root. On the Pacific island of Niue, whole infestations are thought to have started that way.

How to know it

Follow the vine up

It is a vine, so look for a stem pressed against a trunk rather than standing on its own. It makes two kinds of roots: short clasping ones that flatten against the bark, and longer feeding roots that hang free, some reaching all the way to the ground.

Golden streaks

The green leaves are streaked and splashed with yellow, which is what aureum, golden, refers to. The pattern differs from leaf to leaf. All-green pothos exist too, as a separate form, so color alone won't settle it.

Splits, not holes

Adult leaves can reach three feet, with a few irregular cuts in from the edge. Near the midrib there may be tiny see-through dots, but they rarely open into real holes. A Swiss cheese plant, by contrast, is full of them.

How it grows

Up makes it big

In a University of Florida trial, identical plants trained up a pole made leaves nine and a half times larger than the same plants trained sideways. Climbing toward decent light flips the switch to adult leaves; a vine trailing over a pot stays young indefinitely.

Roots at every joint

Each node, the joint where a leaf meets the stem, can put out roots and a new shoot. A piece an inch long with one node will root in about a month.

Why it so rarely flowers

A 2016 study found almost none of the active form of gibberellin, the hormone that sets off flowering, in its shoot tips; the gene for the last step in making it was silent. Sprayed once with the hormone, plants budded in seven or eight weeks. Its closest relative flowers freely everywhere it grows. When pothos does bloom, it makes a whitish, canoe-shaped hood about six inches long around a thick whitish spike.

Take care

Keep it out of mouths

The leaves and stems are full of needle-like calcium oxalate crystals. Chewing them burns the mouth and throat and causes drooling and vomiting, and that goes for dogs, cats and horses too. The sap can irritate skin.
