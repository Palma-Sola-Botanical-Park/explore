Showy Chalice Vine

Solandra grandiflora
On this page

At a glance

  • Its flowers are huge: cups up to about ten inches long that open pale cream and deepen toward gold over their short life.
  • The scent is strongest after dark, a sweet smell often compared to coconut.
  • It is a cousin of the tomato and the potato, in the nightshade family, and like many of its relatives every part of it is poisonous.
  • The genus is named for Daniel Solander, the Swedish naturalist who sailed around the world with Captain Cook.

Cultural significance

Named for a voyager

Solandra honors Daniel Solander, a student of Linnaeus who sailed on Captain Cook's first voyage, from 1768 to 1771, and helped gather thousands of plant specimens from places Europeans had never botanized. The genus was named for him in 1787.

Where to find it here

By the office, near the Silk Floss Tree.

Where it comes from

It is native to the American tropics, from Mexico and the Caribbean south into Brazil, where it climbs high into forest trees and along forest edges.

Nightshade relatives

It belongs to the nightshade family, alongside tomatoes, potatoes, peppers and petunias, and alongside poisonous kin like jimsonweed and deadly nightshade. The family is built on alkaloids, and this vine carries plenty of them.

What it does

A cup worth looking into

The flowers are wide enough to look straight into: a long throat, a frilled rim, and the stamens standing up from the middle. Stand close on a warm evening and the scent is at its strongest.

A flower on a short clock

Each flower lasts only a few days. It opens pale and fragrant and is already turning gold as it ages, so a vine in bloom shows you the whole sequence at once: slim buds, fresh cream cups and older gold ones. Ours has been photographed in full flower in February.

How to know it

Glossy leaves

Thick, shiny, oval leaves, dark green, crowded along heavy woody stems.

Long buds

Before they open, the flowers are long, slim yellow buds that point out from among the leaves.

Stripes inside

Look into an open cup: dark purple-brown stripes run down from the frilled rim toward the center.

Compare with Cup of Gold

Its better-known cousin, Solandra maxima, often sold as Cup of Gold, has a wider, deeper-gold bowl. Ours has longer, slimmer, paler flowers.

How it grows

Pale to gold

Each flower opens pale cream and deepens toward gold as it ages, then drops.

A woody climber

In the wild it scrambles up into trees on thick, rope-like stems. In gardens it climbs fences, walls and arbors, or it can be pruned into a big mounded shrub, and it can reach thirty feet or more if you let it.

Take care

Poisonous

Every part is poisonous if eaten: it carries the same kind of alkaloids as jimsonweed and deadly nightshade. Don't eat or chew any part, wash your hands after handling it, and keep pets away. The sap can irritate skin, so gardeners wear gloves to prune it.
