Christmas Palm

Adonidia merrillii
On this page

At a glance

  • Named for its fruit. Clusters of bright red fruit, each about an inch and a half long, ripen in late fall and winter, and the palm looks decorated.
  • From the Philippines, mainly Palawan, and the north of Borneo. Planted all over South Florida.
  • Fast to about six feet, then slow, and it tops out around 20 to 25 feet, which is why it fits small yards.
  • We have two plantings: one on the Big Pond walkway and one by the Joannis Palm near the Baobab.

Where it comes from

Palawan and Borneo

Christmas Palm grows wild in the Philippines, above all on Palawan, on limestone near the coast, and across the water in Sabah at the north end of Borneo. It is also one of the most planted palms in the tropics, so it turns up far outside that range.

Merrill's palm

The Italian botanist Odoardo Beccari described it in 1909 and named it for Elmer Drew Merrill, the American botanist who ran the botany of the Philippines from Manila for twenty years and built its herbarium. In 1919 Beccari moved it into a genus of its own, Adonidia, little Adonis, for the red fruit; Adonis, in the myth, is the youth whose blood became red flowers.

Where to find it here

Big Pond, south walkway

Take the walkway along the south side of Big Pond. Counting from the bridge into the Rare Fruit Garden at the southeast corner, the Christmas Palm is the eleventh palm, just past the Montgomery Palm, on the pond side. Two trunks grow together there, two palms planted in one hole, as nurseries often sell them.

By the Baobab

A second Christmas Palm stands near the Joannis Palm, by the Baobab in the Serenity Garden.

What it does here

The common cousin

Botanists filed this palm under Veitchia for forty years, alongside Montgomery Palm next door and Joannis Palm by the Baobab, until a 1999 study gave it back its own genus. The three grow within a short walk of each other, and they are built the same way: one ringed trunk, a smooth green crownshaft, red fruit. This is the common one, the palm at the supermarket door; the other two you will rarely see outside a collection.

How to know it

Green crownshaft

A smooth bright green sleeve, the crownshaft, sits between the grey ringed trunk and the fronds. It is the same green as the leaves, not purple or white.

Leaflets in a V

The fronds arch and the leaflets stand up in a V from the stalk, so the frond looks pleated when you sight along it.

Red fruit

The oval fruit hang in clusters below the crownshaft, green through summer and red from late fall through winter. Some are usually on the tree in any month.

Count the trunks

Two or three trunks together are two or three palms. This species grows one trunk and never suckers; nurseries plant several in one pot for a fuller look.

How it grows

Fast, then slow

It grows quickly to about six feet, then slows, and it rarely passes 25 feet. It takes full sun, tolerates some salt air, and wants water while young.

Flowers and rings

Male and female flowers grow on the same spray, so a lone palm sets fruit, and it flowers most of the year. Old fronds fall away cleanly and leave the rings on the trunk.

In Florida

This palm is one of the species that lethal yellowing, a disease spread by a small planthopper, can kill in South Florida. It is not on Florida's invasive plant lists.
