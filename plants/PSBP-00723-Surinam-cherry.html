Surinam cherry

Eugenia uniflora

At a glance

  • The fruit looks like a tiny pumpkin, with eight deep ribs. Most ripen bright red; a rarer kind ripens nearly black and is sweeter.
  • The fruit is ripe about three weeks after the flower opens, which is fast for a shrub.
  • Bees visit the white flowers for pollen. The flowers make little or no nectar, so the bees come for the powder, not a sweet drink.
  • The leaves hold a sharp-smelling oil that repels flies, released when the leaves are walked on. In Brazil they were spread over the floors of houses.

Cultural significance

Pitanga

In Brazil it is called pitanga, and the fruit goes into juice, jam and jelly. The flesh is sweet and sour with a resinous edge, and the big central seed is too resinous to eat. Most fruits hold one seed; a few hold two or three small ones.

Where it comes from

A hedge that got out

It comes from Brazil and neighboring parts of South America, and it was growing in south Florida gardens before 1931. It takes shears so well that it was clipped into the green walls once common here. Birds carry the seeds into natural woodland, where it forms dense thickets, and cutting one down only makes the stump sprout again. Florida now frowns on planting it for that reason.

Where to find it here

By the gazebo

In the rare fruit garden by the pond, Surinam cherry is on the left side of the path as you are about to enter the gazebo.

What it does here

Bronze tips

New leaves come out bronze or reddish and turn glossy green as they age, so a growing branch can wear two colors at once, with the newest at the tip.

Clipped or free

A clipped hedge still fruits heavily, but the shrub fruits best left alone, growing into a loose, many-branched bush instead of a wall.

How to know it

Leaves in pairs

Small, glossy, smooth-edged leaves sit opposite each other along the twig, oval and narrowing to a point.

A white brush

Each flower has four white petals curled back around a brush of fine stamens with pale yellow tips.

How it grows

Slow to start

A seedling may fruit at two years or hold off for five or six. The seed stays alive only about a month, so it sprouts quickly or not at all. Full sun gives the most fruit, though it takes part shade.
