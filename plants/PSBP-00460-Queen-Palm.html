At a glance

  • Queen Palm is native to South America, from Brazil through Paraguay and Uruguay to northeastern Argentina.
  • Mature trees carry long arching feather leaves on a smooth gray trunk and can reach about 50 feet in Florida landscapes.
  • Fruit clusters can weigh more than 100 pounds and contain more than a thousand seeds.
  • The UF/IFAS Assessment updated in August 2026 rates Queen Palm Caution in North, Central and South Florida: manage it to prevent escape.

Where to find it here

Big Pond, west bank

Walk from the Galleria with Big Pond on your left, toward the back of the park. About two-thirds of the way down the pond, on the bank, the walkway turns north — the Queen Palm stands right there, between the Caranday and European Fan palms.

Where it comes from

Brazil to northeastern Argentina

Its native range runs from Brazil to northeastern Argentina, including Paraguay and Uruguay.

What it does

A Florida landscape lesson

Queen Palm shows how a plant can move from ordinary ornamental to management concern. Its appearance in yards does not make it native, and its abundant fruit gives it a route from cultivation into natural areas.

How to know it

A tall smooth trunk

The trunk is gray, solitary and relatively slender for the height of the palm.

Long feathery leaves

The crown is loose and arching rather than stiff.

Orange fruit

Large hanging clusters ripen bright orange and can become one of the tree's most conspicuous features.

No crownshaft

The leaves attach directly around the top of the trunk rather than above a smooth green crownshaft.

How it grows

Fast enough to become ubiquitous

Queen Palm became common in Florida because it grows relatively quickly and tolerates a range of warm landscape settings.

Fruit is the escape route

Fallen fruit produces unwanted seedlings, and Queen Palm has been documented invading natural hammocks.

Current Florida status matters

The 2026 UF/IFAS Assessment rates it Caution statewide, not merely in South Florida. Manage fruit and seedlings to prevent spread.

A Florida disease target

A lethal Fusarium wilt, caused by a fungus specific to palms, has no cure — and contaminated pruning tools are a possible local route of spread.

Take care

Leave the fruit for management, not snacking

The fruit is not considered toxic, but it is fibrous and dominated by a hard seed. Fallen seeds can also be a choking or obstruction hazard for pets.
