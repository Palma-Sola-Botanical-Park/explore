Air Potato

Dioscorea bulbifera
On this page

At a glance

  • In the heat of summer it can grow eight inches in a day. A single vine can reach sixty feet or more, enough to smother trees.
  • Look at how it twines. Air potato winds to the left, counterclockwise as it climbs. Its lookalike, the winged yam (another vine), winds the other way and has square, winged stems.
  • The hanging potatoes are clones. Only female plants are known in Florida, so it spreads by bulbils and tubers instead of seed. Each lump that drops, a bulbil, is a copy of its parent and can sprout, even when it is small.
  • Two beetles were brought in to fight it. The first, released in Florida in 2012, shreds the leaves, but the vine keeps making its potatoes, so a second beetle that eats inside the bulbils was approved in 2021.
  • If you find bulbils on the ground, pick them up at any size and freeze them in a bag until they squish, so they cannot sprout.

Cultural significance

Food elsewhere

In parts of Africa and Asia, cultivated forms of this plant are grown for food, and the lumps are eaten after careful preparation. The wild ones here are bitter and can be poisonous. In Chinese herbal medicine the tuber has also caused liver injury.

Where it comes from

Asia and Africa, then a garden

It grows wild across tropical Asia and Africa. The earliest Florida record is 1905, in the garden of the horticulturist Henry Nehrling, who grew it and sent bulbils to federal plant officials who were testing it as medicine. Accounts differ on the details. A DNA study of Florida's vines found they match Chinese plants more closely than African ones.

Where to find it here

In the Royal Palms, near the gazebo. It is relentless there in the rainy season.

What it does here

Smothered, with one exception

A mat of air potato leaves shades out whatever it climbs, so it does little for wildlife. The one animal tied to it is the leaf beetle sent to control it. That beetle cannot finish its life on any other plant found in Florida.

How to know it

Heart-shaped leaves, hanging potatoes

The leaves are broadly heart-shaped and alternate along the stem, with the veins curving out from the base. The potatoes, the bulbils, hang from the leaf joints, smooth and roundish, up to five inches by four.

Not the winged yam

The winged yam, a close lookalike, has square stems with ridges, leaves that sit opposite each other, and twines to the right. Air potato's stems are round or slightly angled.

How it grows

Dies back, comes back

The stems brown and die back in winter. Underground tubers and the bulbils that fell on the ground start it over in spring.
