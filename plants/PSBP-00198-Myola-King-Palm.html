At a glance

  • An ordinary-looking king palm from an extraordinarily small piece of Australia: it grows wild only around Myola, on the Atherton Tableland behind Cairns in Queensland.
  • Botanists collected it in 1976 and did not name it as a species until 1994. The IUCN lists it as Vulnerable; Queensland and the Australian government list it as Endangered.
  • Wait for a breeze. The leaflets are glossy green on top and silver-grey underneath, and the crown flashes pale when the wind turns them.
  • Ours is one of three king palms in a row along the 17th Avenue path, and the only way to tell them apart is the signs.

Where it comes from

A very small piece of Australia

Myola King Palm is native to the Atherton Tableland of northeastern Queensland, around Myola near Kuranda, at about 1,100 to 1,300 feet. It grows along creeks and riverbanks in rainforest, on volcanic soil, in country with heavy rain and cooler nights than the coast below.

Named in 1994

The palm was collected beside Warrill Creek at Myola in 1976 and described as a species by the Australian botanist John Dowe in 1994, in a revision of the king palms. The name myolensis just says where it lives. The IUCN assessed it as Vulnerable in 1998; Queensland's and Australia's own threatened-species lists rate it Endangered, and its creekside habitat sits in a district being cleared and built on.

Where to find it here

Along the 17th Avenue path

Myola King Palm grows along the path that runs inside the 17th Avenue fence, toward the Tides End end of the park. Three king palms stand in a row there between the path and the lawn, with the tall Royal Palms behind them: Purple King Palm, then Myola King Palm, then Peach River King Palm.

What it does

Three kings, one row

Three king palms in a row, from three separate patches of Queensland rainforest, and to most eyes they are the same palm. That is the point of standing them together. They are part of a collection of unusual palms that a longtime volunteer planted here over many years. Read the sign, then look for what the sign says.

How to know it

Silver underneath

The leaflets are glossy dark green above and silver-grey beneath, from a coat of fine scales. Turn a low frond over, or wait for wind; the crown goes pale for a moment as the undersides show.

Crownshaft and rings

The leaf bases wrap the top of the stem into a smooth, slightly blue-green cylinder, the crownshaft, and the fronds spring from the top of it. Below, the trunk is ringed with the scars of dropped fronds. Ours is young, with only a few feet of trunk.

Flowers and fruit

Flowers come in big cream-white sprays below the crownshaft, and the fruit ripen bright waxy red, each about half an inch long. Nothing has flowered on ours yet.

Compare with the king palms beside it

Purple King Palm on one side, Peach River King Palm on the other. The three differ in details a botanist measures, the colour of the crownshaft, the shape of the fruit, the scales on the leaflets, and young palms are hard even for specialists. Use the signs, and enjoy that three species this alike each have their own small patch of rainforest.

How it grows

A creekside palm

At home it stands beside creeks in rainforest, in rich volcanic soil that never dries out. Here it has the part shade of the Royal Palms behind it, closer to home than open lawn would be. It wants steady water, shelter from wind, and no long dry spells.

Built to cross-pollinate

Male and female flowers grow on the same spray, but not at the same time: the males shed their pollen before the females on that spray are ready, so pollen tends to come from another palm. New plants come from seed.
