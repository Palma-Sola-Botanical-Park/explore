At a glance

  • Prunes come from plums selected for drying, especially varieties of European Plum.
  • The hard stone inside a plum encloses its seed; it is not the seed itself.
  • Winter cold helps prepare plum buds for spring—a complication in a place with mild winters.

Cultural significance

European Plum has supplied fruit for preserves, baking and drying for centuries. Different varieties offer different colors, flavors and uses. A good prune variety needs more than a purple skin: its sugar content and the way its flesh dries matter.

Growers usually graft a chosen variety onto a rootstock. That preserves the fruiting variety, while a seed from the same fruit may grow into a tree with different qualities.

Where it comes from

Its origins lie around the Caucasus and northern Iran. Long cultivation carried it far beyond that region and produced the many forms now grown in temperate orchards.

What it does here

This tree puts a familiar fruit in an unfamiliar climate. Many European plums need substantially more winter chilling than our mild winters provide. The low-chill plums developed for Florida are generally Japanese-type plums, a different group; a plum that does well elsewhere is not automatically suited to Bradenton.

How to know it

Watch for the seasonal change

Watch a plum through the seasons. White, five-petaled flowers can appear before the canopy fills out; the later fruit has a seam down one side and a hard stone inside. Those changes tell more than a leafy tree alone.

Typical European Plum leaves are oval with finely toothed edges. A ripe fruit may wear a dusty-looking wax coating called bloom, made by the fruit itself.

How it grows

Cool winter weather helps satisfy dormancy before warmth starts the next cycle of growth. That is why a tree can survive in a warm garden yet give an unreliable fruit crop. Different cultivars have different chilling needs; a familiar fruit name alone does not tell you what to expect.

Take care

The ripe flesh is edible, but do not chew or eat the seed inside the stone. Plum leaves and seed kernels contain compounds that can release cyanide.
