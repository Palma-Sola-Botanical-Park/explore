Elephant's-foot

Beaucarnea recurvata

At a glance

  • A fat, wrinkled foot, a skinny trunk, and on top a mop of long hair, like Cousin Itt from The Addams Family. Few people stop for it, and they should.
  • A water tank for a foot. The swollen base of the trunk stores water, which carries the plant through dry spells.
  • It saves water by going hungry. In a drought experiment, the leaves held most of their water for about eight weeks, while the leaf pores closed down and photosynthesis fell to nothing. Watered again, the plants recovered.
  • Not a palm. Ponytail Palm is a common name, but it belongs to the asparagus family.

Cultural significance

A favorite with a wild cost

Nurseries grow it legally from seed for gardens and homes. Even so, wild seed, seedlings and grown plants are still collected illegally, so the familiar pot plant carries a cost back where it comes from.

Where to find it here

Walk south on the crushed-shell path with the cactus and succulent garden on your right. Near the south end, up on the tiers close to the two tall Madagascar Palms, look for a skinny trunk with a mop of long, drooping leaves on top. Once you spot the mop, look down for the foot.

Where it comes from

Rocky, dry Mexico

It comes from Mexico, where it grows on rocky ground in low forest that dries out for part of the year. Accounts differ on how wide the wild range is: one lists five Mexican states, another only central and northern Veracruz.

Either way, the wild plants are in trouble. The most recent global assessment of threatened species, in 2019, rated wild Elephant's-foot Critically Endangered and still declining, because its habitat is being turned into towns, pasture and farmland, and because wild plants are dug up illegally.

What it does

Waiting on flowers

When a plant is old enough, it can lift a branching spray of tiny cream-colored flowers above the leaves, and bees come to them. We do not know whether ours has flowered yet. If you catch it in bloom, we would love a photo.

How to know it

Start at the foot

Look at the base. The trunk flares out where it meets the ground, and with age the gray surface cracks, which is where the elephant's foot comes from. The broad foot and narrowing trunk give the plant a bottle outline.

Leaves like ribbons

Long, narrow, tough leaves grow in tufts at the stem tips. On a big plant they trail for several feet, twisting or curling toward the ends. The park's Bottle Palm also has a swollen trunk, but its fronds split into leaflets; these leaves are single long straps.

After the flowers

On a plant that sets seed, the small dry seed capsules have three wings and may be tinged red.

How it grows

Two kinds of plant

Male and female flowers generally grow on separate plants, though occasional flowers with both are reported. A flowering male provides pollen; a female can set seed after pollination. Flowers alone do not tell you which one you are looking at.

Slow, then branching

It starts as a single stem and can branch with age or after flowering, each branch ending in its own tuft of leaves. Growth is slow, and a plant in a pot stays much smaller than one with room in the ground. Sun and fast-draining soil suit it; roots that stay wet can rot.

Take care

The leaf edges carry fine teeth that can scratch bare skin.
