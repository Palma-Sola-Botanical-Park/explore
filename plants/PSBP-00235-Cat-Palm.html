Cat Palm

Chamaedorea cataractarum

At a glance

  • Named for rushing water. Cataractarum means "of the rapids." In the wild this palm grows on stream banks in southern Mexico, where floods pour through. Its other names, Cascade Palm and Cataract Palm, say the same thing.
  • No trunk to speak of. Short stems creep along the ground and fork, building a low, tangled base that holds the clump in place when water rushes over it.
  • The leaves give way. They are flexible, so in a flood they bend with the current instead of standing against it.
  • The seeds grow hooks. After a long soak, fine hooked fibers lift from the seed's hard coat. In a lab test, soaked seeds snagged on a screen where fresh ones rolled off.

Where to find it here

Two clumps sit back in the Shade Palms, in the northeast part of the park, just north of the Butterfly Garden. One grows on each side of the path, so you walk right between them.

Where it comes from

Cat Palm is native to southern Mexico, along streams. Here it grows where we keep the palms that like shade, well away from any rapids.

What it does

Built for floods

A stream-bank plant has to hold on. The forking stems knit into a network that anchors the clump, and the soft leaves bend with the water. In the wild, flood debris gets caught in the clumps and can mark how high the last flood rose.

Seeds with hooks

Put a cleaned seed in water and its hard coat changes. After about a day and a half, fine fibers lift from the surface, each one hooked. In a lab test, every soaked seed caught on a screen while every fresh one rolled off, and the soaked seeds also sank more slowly. The researchers speculate that birds eat the fruit, the seeds end up in a stream, and the hooks catch on a bank somewhere downstream.

How to know it

A mound, not a trunk

The fronds rise straight from a low base, and the stems stay hidden down in the clump. What you see is a mound of arching, dark green leaves.

Look at one leaf

Each frond is a feather, with many narrow leaflets lined up along both sides of the stalk.

Two relatives nearby

Reed Palm, in this same shady corner, carries its leaves on upright green canes you can see. Chamaedorea hooperiana, also in the Shade Palms, grows tall canes wrapped in tough brown leaf bases. Cat Palm keeps its stems low and out of sight.

How it grows

A grown clump can stand about six feet tall and spread even wider. We will see how big ours get in the shade here.

Take care

Leave the fruit alone

The fruit pulp can irritate skin. Avoid handling it, and don't eat it.
