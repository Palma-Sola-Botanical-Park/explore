At a glance

  • Coontie is Florida's native cycad. It looks something like a small palm or fern, but it belongs to an ancient line of seed plants that was here long before the first flower.
  • Most of the plant is underground: a thick stem that stores energy and sends up a new crown of leaves after fire or damage.
  • It is the only food plant of the Atala butterfly's caterpillars, which eat the toxic leaves and keep some of the poison for themselves.
  • People once turned its poisonous stem into an edible starch called Florida arrowroot, and dug so much of it that wild coontie nearly vanished.

Cultural significance

From toxic root to food

Indigenous people in Florida learned to turn coontie's toxic, starch-rich underground stem into food by grinding it and repeatedly washing away the harmful compounds. Seminole communities continued the practice, and settlers later adopted the processed starch as "Florida arrowroot."

From food to industry

By the late 1800s coontie starch was a commercial crop in South Florida. Around 1900 Miami mills were grinding as much as twenty tons a day, and the starch went into commercial crackers, a Nabisco wafer among them by most accounts. Whole stands were dug for a plant that grows back slowly, and wild populations have never fully recovered.

Where to find it here

Coontie is planted around the park by the dozen, in rows of five to fifteen. The largest groups ring the Big Pond, planted under a Tampa Bay Estuary Program grant to hold the pond banks, with smaller groups around the other two ponds. One stands near the Butterfly Garden, planted for a butterfly that has not arrived yet.

When

Coontie stays green through the year. Look closely in the warmer months for its cones, and for Atala caterpillars on the new growth if the butterfly ever finds us.

Where it comes from

Florida and the Caribbean

Coontie is native to Florida and nearby parts of the Caribbean. In Florida it grows naturally in well-drained places: pine flatwoods, hammocks and sandy uplands.

What it does

One plant, one butterfly

An Atala caterpillar has only one kind of food plant: coontie. Females lay their eggs on it, and the caterpillars feed on its leaves. Without coontie, an Atala population cannot reproduce.

A defense the caterpillar borrows

Coontie contains toxic compounds that discourage most animals from eating it. Atala caterpillars feed on the plant anyway and keep some of those chemicals in their bodies; their bright red coloring advertises that defense to predators.

A Florida comeback

The Atala disappeared from most of Florida as coontie was dug out, and by the 1960s it was presumed extinct in the state. In 1979 a naturalist searching Virginia Key near Miami for a rare tree found caterpillars feeding on coontie. As nursery-grown coontie returned to South Florida landscapes, the butterfly followed it.

Holding the banks

Around the ponds coontie does a plainer job. Its underground stem and tough, low crown hold the bank against wash and need almost nothing once established, which is why it was chosen for the pond-edge planting.

Waiting for the Atala

The Atala has been recorded in Bradenton within a few miles of this park, on iNaturalist, in the summer of 2026. It has not been seen at Palma Sola. The coontie near the Butterfly Garden is the invitation.

How to know it

Low and palm-like

Coontie usually forms a low clump of stiff, glossy green leaves rising from near ground level. Despite the name "Coontie Palm," it is a cycad, not a palm.

Leaves

Each arching leaf is divided into many leathery leaflets, giving the plant a fern-like look.

Cones

Coonties do not make flowers. Male and female plants produce different cones: pollen-bearing male cones are narrower, seed-bearing female cones larger and thicker.

Look for the Atalas

Bright red-orange caterpillars with yellow spots feeding together on the leaves would be the surest clue of all. Adult Atalas are small, dark butterflies with iridescent blue spots and a red-orange abdomen.

How it grows

Most of the plant is below you

What looks like a small plant above ground grows from a thick, fleshy underground stem called a caudex. It stores reserves that let coontie survive drought and send up new leaves after fire or damage.

Male or female

Each coontie plant is either male or female, and both must be present for pollination and seed. Instead of flowers, they reproduce with cones.

An ancient partnership

Coontie cones are pollinated by insects, not wind: small weevils live and breed in the male cones and carry pollen to the female ones. It is one of the oldest plant-and-insect partnerships still running.

Built for Florida

Coontie grows in sun or shade and tolerates dry conditions once established. The underground stem lets it recover after fire, a useful trait in the Florida habitats where it grows wild.

Take care

Toxic if eaten

All parts of coontie contain toxic compounds and should not be eaten. The starch was only ever made safe by long processing and repeated washing; raw plant material is dangerous.

Keep dogs away

Cycads are particularly dangerous to dogs. Eating coontie can cause severe poisoning, including liver damage. Keep pets from chewing the leaves, stems or seeds.
