At a glance

  • There are a zillion of these in Bradenton. Every hedge, every privacy screen, every hotel lobby. In the one place it started, a strip of white-sand forest on the east coast of Madagascar, it is getting hard to find. The commonest palm on earth in a pot is a scarce tree at home.
  • A grown clump is a thicket of slender canes, each ringed like bamboo and turning gold where the sun hits, which is where the names Golden Cane and Butterfly Palm come from.
  • Ours at the main entrance has had decades and room. It is what the houseplant turns into.
  • Bright yellow flower stalks droop from under the fronds in season, with strings of small fruit that ripen from yellow to nearly black.

Where it comes from

One coast of Madagascar

North-eastern Madagascar, in a narrow band of coastal forest on white sand, plus a few river banks and swamps inland. That forest is being cleared, and the wild palms are fewer every decade. Meanwhile botanic gardens spread it through the Caribbean as early as the eighteenth century, and by the twentieth it was in every hotel lobby on earth. In Florida it is planted as a screen so often that people forget it is a species at all. It is doing better in our yards than in its own.

Where to find it here

On the Welcome Island at the main entrance: the tall clump of ringed golden canes that meets you as you come in. There is a second clump in the Butterfly Garden.

What it does

The gateway palm

A living gate. The canes have gone up twenty feet and more and the fronds meet overhead, so the entrance is framed in gold and green. Bees work the yellow flower stalks, and where the palm has gone wild in warm places birds take the fruit. Here it is mostly architecture, and very good architecture.

How to know it

A clump of ringed canes

Many slender stems from one base, two or three inches thick, ringed all the way up, gold to orange toward the top.

Yellow in the stalks

The leaf stalks and the midrib of each frond run yellow to orange, and the leaflets stand up from the midrib in a V.

Drooping yellow flower stalks

Branched yellow stalks under the crown, then strings of egg-shaped fruit.

How it grows

New canes push up from the base for as long as the plant lives, so a clump widens to ten feet across. Sun brings out the gold; shade keeps it greener. It wants steady water and feeding on our sand, and goes yellow-spotted on the old leaflets without potassium. It seeds itself about in South Florida, so the county asks gardeners there to keep it in bounds; up here it stays put.
