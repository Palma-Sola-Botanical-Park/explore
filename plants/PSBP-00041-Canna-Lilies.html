At a glance

  • Cannas are not lilies. They belong to Cannaceae, a tropical American family related to gingers and bananas.
  • They spread underground by thick rhizomes, which is why plants can reappear well away from the original stem.
  • The park has used that growth habit as a teaching job: volunteers separate wandering cannas from nearby heliconias and move them into the canna bed.

Cultural significance

Indian shot

The very hard round seeds explain the old common name "Indian shot"; seeds of some cannas were historically used as beads and improvised shot.

A plant carried around the tropics

Cannas have been cultivated far beyond the Americas for food, starch and ornament, and numerous garden hybrids now blur simple species labels.

Where it comes from

Canna is native to tropical and subtropical America. Because the park record is only identified to genus, this page does not assign a species-level homeland.

What it does

A sorting lesson, done by hand

Cannas have grown around Cypress Pond for years, and mixed in with heliconias near the office for just as long. The park now uses that mix to teach its high school volunteers: students learn to tell the two apart by flower and leaf, dig out the entire canna rhizome without breaking it — several remark that it looks like ginger — and transplant it out of the heliconia bed and into the canna side. The two beds are now visibly split, cannas on one side and heliconias on the other, and every student who's done it has gotten their hands dirty learning a real identification skill.

How to know it

Paddle-shaped leaves

Broad leaves rise from sheathing bases around an upright pseudostem. If you're near the Office planting, see if you can tell the canna leaves apart from the heliconia leaves growing beside them — it's the same challenge we give our student volunteers.

Asymmetrical flowers

The bright flower is built from petal-like stamens and other modified floral parts rather than a simple lily pattern.

Hard round seeds

When fruit develops, the capsules can contain nearly spherical, very hard seeds.

How it grows

Rhizomes do the work

Cannas expand from underground rhizomes and can form broad clumps quickly in warm, moist soil.

Sun and moisture favor bloom

They flower best with good light and regular moisture, but established clumps are forgiving.
