California Fan Palm

Washingtonia filifera

At a glance

  • The shaggy skirt of dead fronds an old one carries is not neglect. It insulates the trunk against desert days and freezing nights, and in a fire it burns through fast enough to be gone before the trunk itself catches.
  • It is the only palm native to the western United States, and the largest palm native to North America.
  • Its name, filifera, means thread-bearing. Look along the edges of a frond for loose white threads hanging off, like a hem coming undone.
  • This is a desert plant in a wet place. Ours is doing fine, but it is not one the park would plant again here.

Cultural significance

The tree that makes an oasis

The palm canyons of the Colorado and Sonoran deserts are built around this one species — Thousand Palms in the Coachella Valley, the canyons of Anza-Borrego. In country with almost no large woody plants, a grove of these is the whole ecosystem, and its fruit was a food of the desert Southwest long before any of it was a park.

Where it comes from

Desert, and only where there is water

It grows wild in a narrow band of southern California, southwestern Arizona and the neighbouring corner of Mexico, and nowhere else. It cannot live on desert rainfall, so it grows only where groundwater surfaces — a spring, a seep, a crack along a fault. A stand of these on the horizon is a reliable sign of water underfoot.

Moved by coyotes

A mature palm drops thousands of small black fruits. Coyotes eat them and carry them off, and seed that has been through a coyote germinates at a measurably better rate than seed that simply falls.

Where to find it here

Outside the fence, along the road

This one grows outside the park, on the grass strip between the fence and the road. You are welcome to walk out and have a look — there is a whole line of palms along there, a good many of them worth stopping for, and they get enjoyed far less than they deserve.

What it does here

A desert palm in the wrong climate

Everything this palm is built for — hard sun, dry air, water far underground — is the opposite of a Gulf Coast summer. It is rated for Texas, Arizona and California rather than Florida, where wet ground can start root rot. Ours is established and worth looking at, but it is a specimen to appreciate rather than a species the park would recommend to anyone planting here.

Young yet

It has not built its trunk. What you see is a stout base and a big crown close to the ground, with only the first of the dead fronds hanging. The column and the full skirt come later.

How to know it

Find the threads

Look into the splits between the segments of a fan. Fine white threads hang loose there, and they are what the species is named for. Once you have seen them you will not mistake this palm again.

A thick trunk that does not taper

The one to compare it with is the Mexican fan palm, far more widely planted across the South. That one is tall and slim and narrows toward the top. This one is shorter, stockier, and stays about as thick all the way up.

Mind the leaf stalks

The stalks carry curved teeth along their edges, sharp enough to open skin. Worth knowing before you reach in among the lower fronds.

How it grows

Unhurried, and eventually tall

Moderate growth to thirty or fifty feet here, taller in the wild. It holds onto its dead leaves rather than dropping them, building the thatch that shades the trunk.

What lives in the skirt

In its own range that dead thatch is habitat. The western yellow bat roosts in it by day, and Hooded Orioles pull fibre from the older fronds to build hanging nests — sometimes stitching the nest directly onto a living leaf by pushing fibres through it. The bird is so tied to these palms that it followed them into the suburbs as people planted them.
