Peach River King Palm

Archontophoenix tuckeri

At a glance

  • New leaves often emerge bronze, reddish or pink before maturing to deep green. The colour is brief, so catching one at its best is partly luck.
  • Turn over a leaflet and the green disappears: the underside is silver-grey from a dense layer of tiny scales.
  • There are only six King Palms in the world. The park grows four of them, and three stand together in the same row - which is a rare chance to see how different close relatives can look.
  • This is a serious forest palm. In northern Queensland it can reach about sixty-five feet tall, with a trunk that flares strongly at the base.

Cultural significance

Named for Robert Tucker

When the species was formally described in 1994 it was named for Robert James Thomas Tucker, an Australian horticulturist and palm specialist who died in 1992 at thirty-seven. He did not live to see the palm carry his name.

Where it comes from

The top of Australia

Peach River King Palm belongs to Cape York Peninsula in far northern Queensland, where it grows in wet tropical forest from near sea level into the hills. Rivers, swamp forest and other reliably moist places are a recurring part of its habitat.

Peach River is a real place

The specimen used to define the species was collected in 1948 beside Upper Peach River at Bonanza Creek. The palm was not formally named until 1994.

Where to find it here

In the shaded palm area, with its relatives

Out in the shaded northeast corner of the park, past the Royal Palms, a row of collection palms runs along the path. This one is near the far end of that row, with the Purple King Palm and the Myola King Palm standing right beside it. Look low - ours is young and nothing like the sixty-five-foot tree of its home forests.

What it does

Three King Palms, side by side

The Purple King and the Myola King are its immediate neighbours in this row, and the Piccabeen Palm grows elsewhere in the park - four of the six species that exist, on one property. Look along the three and compare the crownshafts: the colour, the length, how much each one swells at the base. They are close relatives doing noticeably different things, and you will not often get to check that in one glance.

Ours is only getting started

This is a young palm with no trunk yet - the fronds still come up almost from the ground. Nearly everything the description promises, the tall grey ringed stem and the long lime crownshaft above it, is still years off. What it does have already is the silver leaf undersides, and those do not change.

How to know it

The lime-green crownshaft

A smooth, waxy crownshaft, pale to lime green and slightly swollen near the base. On a grown palm it stands out sharply against the grey ringed trunk below it. On ours there is no trunk yet, so the crownshaft sits right down at ground level - still the same colour, just not yet in the same place.

Silver underneath

The leaflets are green above but distinctly silver-grey underneath from fine scales. Their tips often droop slightly, giving the ends of the fronds a softer look.

Watch the newest leaf

A frond that has just opened can come out coppery or flushed with pink, then lose it over a few weeks as it hardens to green. Pure bonus - there is no telling when.

Young ones are tricky

Juvenile King Palms look frustratingly alike, which is exactly the problem ours presents. Crownshaft colour, the silvery leaflet undersides and the brick-red fruit all become far more useful once a palm begins to mature.

How it grows

Warm and well watered

It comes from wet tropical country and responds best to regular moisture with good drainage. In cultivation, steady water and warmth make a noticeable difference in pace.

Tall, on one trunk, eventually

It grows as a solitary palm rather than a clump. In time the trunk reaches roughly ten inches across and flares strongly where it meets the ground, and the crownshaft alone can run three to five feet long.

Cold is the limit here

This is still a tropical palm. Bradenton can grow it, but a hard freeze is the kind of weather most likely to test it.
