Japanese Privet

At a glance

  • A hedge is a privet that was never allowed to grow up. Left alone, Japanese Privet becomes a small tree with curved gray trunks and a crown that can spread 25 feet.
  • In the 1920s, Royal Palm Nurseries in Oneco sold it as a street and shade tree, not a hedge plant. For hedges, the same catalogs urged the glossy privet, which Florida now lists as Category I.
  • The flowers open in late spring with a strong scent. Some people find it sweet and many find it foul.
  • Florida rates it a high invasion risk in every region, yet the state's invasive species council has it only on a watch list, not on its Category I or II lists.

Cultural significance

A tree in Oneco's catalog

Royal Palm Nurseries, in Oneco, was listing Japanese Privet by 1919 as a small to medium-sized evergreen tree. By the early 1920s the catalog said its thick dark leaves strongly suggested northern lilac, and recommended it for street planting the way the Bauhinias were used (our Orchid Tree is a Bauhinia). It came as grafted trees, five to seven feet tall, at two dollars each. In the 1930 planting book it stood in the shade-tree list, right after the live oak, as a tub plant at $3.50.

The hedge they pushed instead

The hedge privet that same 1930 book urged on the whole state was a different species, the glossy privet, a big shrub with large dark leaves that it said could not be recommended too highly. That plant is now on Florida's Category I list for north and central Florida, the list of plants known to damage natural areas. The one sold as a tree is not on it.

Where it comes from

Japan and eastern Asia

Japonicum means of Japan, and the plant grows wild across Japan and eastern Asia. The Swedish botanist Carl Thunberg, who spent more than a year in Japan in the 1770s, gave it its name in 1780. It reached American gardens in the nineteenth century.

The mouse-dropping tree

In Japan it is nezumi-mochi. Nezumi means rat or mouse, and the name is usually explained by the fruit, which people there liken to droppings.

Who is the invader?

At home, the roles are reversed. The glossy privet is the newcomer in Japan, brought from China, and it crowds and hybridizes with the native Japanese Privet. In Florida it is Japanese Privet that is the garden import.

What it does here

The tree behind the hedge

Gray branches fork under the leaf cover on ours, so you can see the tree that a clipped hedge keeps hidden. A hedge is just this plant clipped back, over and over.

Olive and lilac cousins

Privet, olive and lilac are relatives, which makes the catalog's lilac comparison better than it sounds. Our Olive and our Japanese Privet both have leaves in opposite pairs and fruit with a hard stone inside. The privet's broad glossy leaves look nothing like the olive's narrow ones with pale undersides, but the family tie is real.

A crowd of small visitors

The flower clusters draw a wide range of insects, bees among them. Nobody seems to have recorded which butterflies, if any, take its nectar.

How to know it

Smell it

Late spring brings upright clusters of small white flowers at the tips of the shoots, and you will often smell them before you see them. The scent is strong and people disagree about it: some call it sweet, others rank. Ours was in flower on May 27, 2026.

Count the side veins

The leaves are thick, glossy and smooth-edged, one and a half to three inches long, set in opposite pairs. Hold one up and count the veins that run out from the midrib: three to five on a side.

Not the glossy privet

The bigger glossy privet looks similar. Its leaves run longer, up to five inches, with six to eight pairs of side veins and a long drawn-out tip, and it grows into a tree of 20 to 30 feet. Chinese privet has dull leaves.

Blue-black fruit

Small fruits about a quarter inch across ripen from green to blue-black and hang on most of the year, each with a hard stone inside.

How it grows

What the shears take

Flowers form at the tips of the shoots, so shearing a hedge cuts off much of its flowering wood and a clipped hedge flowers less. Whether that means less seed, nobody seems to have measured.

Garden forms

Nurseries have selected 'Howardii', with golden new growth, 'Recurvifolium', with wavy leaf edges, 'Variegatum', with cream-edged leaves, and compact 'Texanum', the Texas privet. 'Variegatum' has its own high-risk rating and is no longer recommended in Florida.

Trouble underground

Root-feeding nematodes, microscopic worms common in Florida's sandy soils, can bring on a serious, slow decline.

Beyond the hedge

Florida's current assessment rates Japanese Privet a high invasion risk in all three regions, and it sits on the state invasive species council's watch list. The record in the wild is thinner than that sounds: the assessment documents it in natural areas in only a few north and central counties, and an older Florida fact sheet called its stray seedlings usually no nuisance. Sources also disagree about whether birds go for the fruit. Birds are the presumed route for any escape.

Take care

Berries and leaves

The berries and leaves can make people sick, mostly with stomach pain, nausea and vomiting. Privet is toxic to dogs, cats and horses too: stomach upset is the usual sign, staggering and a racing heartbeat can follow, and death is rare. Keep children and animals from chewing it.
