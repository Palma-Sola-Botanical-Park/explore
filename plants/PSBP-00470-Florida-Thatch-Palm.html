Florida Thatch Palm

Thrinax radiata

At a glance

  • Florida lists it as Endangered even though the species is doing fine across much of the Caribbean. Here its wild range is squeezed into the very bottom of the state.
  • The name is literal. These fronds have roofed houses across the Caribbean for centuries, and been worked into brooms, baskets and food wrappings. In the Yucatan the trunks were built into lobster traps.
  • The fruit comes out white — small, hard and bead-like. If the palm is fruiting, that is one of the quickest clues you will get.
  • Caterpillars of the Monk Skipper, a stout mahogany-brown butterfly, feed on it.

Cultural significance

A roof you could grow

Thatch palms earned their name honestly. The fronds cut, dried and layered make a roof that sheds tropical rain, and the same leaves went into brooms, handicrafts and wrappings for food. Yucatan fishermen used the trunks for lobster traps.

Where it comes from

The Caribbean rim

Native around the northern Caribbean and along the Caribbean coast of Central America. In the United States it reaches only the far southern tip of Florida, where it grows on limestone and coastal soils close to sea level.

Where to find it here

The native palm row, along the south fence

At the back of the park, where a line of five Florida natives runs along the south fence. The Florida Thatch is second in the row, with the Dwarf Palmetto on one side and the Key Thatch on the other — which makes it the easiest place in the park to put two close relatives side by side and look for the differences.

What it does here

Well north of its wild Florida range

Around Bradenton this is a cultivated palm rather than part of the species' natural Florida population, which stops well to the south of us. A hard winter is one of the main things that tests it here.

Feeding the ones that stay

The flower stalks pull in bees, and the white fruit that follows feeds birds, which help move the seeds.

How to know it

Check the fruit

White. Small, round and hard, like strung beads. The palms most easily confused with it carry dark fruit, so if this one is fruiting you have a quick answer.

Look where the leaf joins the stalk

There is a forked split at the base of each leaf — an upside-down V where the blade meets its stem. It is a small thing and it is diagnostic.

No clean collar

Unlike a Royal Palm, there is no smooth green sleeve between trunk and crown. The fronds attach straight to the trunk, so old leaf bases hang on as a rough fibrous cuff near the top.

How it grows

It takes its time

About six inches of height a year, and it keeps that up for decades. This is not a palm that sulks or struggles — that is simply its pace. Any trunk you are looking at represents years of patient growth rather than a sudden spurt.
