Florida Thatch Palm

Thrinax radiata

At a glance

  • Florida lists it as Endangered, though the species is doing fine across the Caribbean. The whole of its wild Florida range is the Keys, the southern tip of the mainland, and one island in Collier County.
  • The name is literal. These fronds have roofed houses across the Caribbean for centuries, and been worked into brooms, baskets and food wrappings. In the Yucatan the trunks were built into lobster traps.
  • The fruit comes out white — small, hard and bead-like. That one detail separates it from the dark purple-fruited palms it otherwise resembles.
  • Caterpillars of the Monk Skipper, a stout mahogany-brown butterfly, feed on it.

Cultural significance

A roof you could grow

Thatch palms earned their name honestly. The fronds cut, dried and layered make a roof that sheds tropical rain, and the same leaves went into brooms, handicrafts and wrappings for food. Yucatan fishermen used the trunks for lobster traps. Very little of this palm went unused.

A rake, and a wheel

Thrinax comes from the Greek for a three-pronged fork, for the divided leaf. Radiata means radiating, for the way the segments spread from one point like the spokes of a wheel. Both halves of the name are describing the same leaf twice.

Where it comes from

The Caribbean rim

Native through the Bahamas, Cuba, Hispaniola, Jamaica, the Caymans and along the coasts of the Yucatan, Belize, Honduras and Nicaragua — always on limestone soils close to sea level. It reaches the United States only at the very bottom of Florida.

Where to find it here

The native palm row, along the south fence

At the back of the park, where a line of five Florida natives runs along the south fence. The Florida Thatch is second in the row, with the Dwarf Palmetto on one side and the Key Thatch on the other — which makes it the easiest place in the park to put two close relatives side by side and look for the differences.

What it does here

Grown well north of where it belongs

Its natural range stops three hundred miles south of us, and Bradenton sits at the edge of what it will take — fronds mark at twenty-eight degrees. That it is growing here at all is the interesting part, and a hard winter is the thing to watch.

Feeding the ones that stay

The flower stalks pull in bees, and the white fruit that follows feeds birds through much of the year. In the Caribbean bats and even spider monkeys spread the seed; here that job falls entirely to birds.

How to know it

Check the fruit

White. Small, round and hard, like strung beads. The palms most easily confused with it carry dark purple fruit, so if you can see fruit at all you have your answer.

Look where the leaf joins the stalk

There is a forked split at the base of each leaf — an upside-down V where the blade meets its stem. It is a small thing and it is diagnostic.

No clean collar

Unlike a Royal Palm, there is no smooth green sleeve between trunk and crown. The fronds attach straight to the trunk, so old leaf bases hang on as a rough fibrous cuff near the top.

How it grows

Six inches a year

That is the whole story of this palm. It is not sulking and it is not struggling — that is simply its pace, and it keeps it up for decades. Slow enough that a trunk is a rough measure of how long somebody has been looking after it.
