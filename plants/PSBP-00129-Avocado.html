At a glance

  • Avocado is native from central Mexico through Central America to Costa Rica.
  • Each flower opens twice, functioning first as female and later as male.
  • Cultivars are grouped into A and B flowering types. Their schedules can overlap and improve cross-pollination, although some Florida cultivars fruit well alone.
  • The familiar fruit is a single-seeded berry in botanical terms.

Cultural significance

An ancient cultivated fruit

Archaeological evidence from Mexico shows people eating avocado thousands of years ago, long before modern named cultivars.

Florida grows different avocados from the supermarket stereotype

There are West Indian, Guatemalan and Mexican horticultural groups and their hybrids. Florida cultivars often differ markedly from the thick-skinned Hass fruit that dominates U.S. supermarkets.

Where it comes from

Central Mexico to Costa Rica

Its native range runs from central Mexico to Costa Rica.

Where to find it here

South end, near the bridge

Like the mangoes, the park's avocados are toward the south end of the Rare Fruit collection, near the bridge. Multiple cultivars are planted here — check the signs at each tree.

What it does here

A flower-timing demonstration

Avocado makes pollination visible as a scheduling problem. A single flower changes sexual function between two openings, and neighboring cultivars can complement one another when their male and female phases overlap.

How to know it

Broad evergreen crown

Leaves are simple, leathery and alternately arranged.

Tiny flowers, large fruit

The greenish-yellow flowers are small and numerous compared with the fruit they can produce.

One large seed

Each avocado fruit normally encloses one conspicuous seed.

Cultivar matters

Fruit size, shape, skin thickness and season vary widely, so no single supermarket avocado represents the whole species.

How it grows

Drainage is critical in Florida

Avocado roots are highly susceptible to prolonged wet or flooded soil.

Different horticultural groups handle cold differently

West Indian types are least cold tolerant; Mexican and some hybrid types tolerate more cold. That matters along Florida's subtropical margins.

Grafting preserves the cultivar

Seedlings do not reliably reproduce the fruit qualities of the parent. Named cultivars are propagated vegetatively.

Take care

Keep the pit and plant material away from pets

The edible flesh is human food, but avocado contains persin in other tissues and the large pit is a serious swallowing hazard. ASPCA specifically lists avocado toxicity for horses; sensitivity differs among animal species. Leave park fruit and plant material in place.
