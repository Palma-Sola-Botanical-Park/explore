Macarthur Palm

At a glance

  • A clump of thin, ringed, cane-like trunks with bright red fruit. We have two: one in the shaded bed at the Pavilion Gazebo and one in the row of palms beside the Plumeria.
  • Native from western and southern New Guinea to the top end of Australia. For more than a century it was sold as Ptychosperma macarthurii, now treated as a synonym of P. propinquum.
  • The leaflet tips look bitten off, the family trademark, and the fruit ripen red, about half an inch long. Birds take them.
  • The common name honours Sir William Macarthur (1800–1882), a plant collector and nurseryman at Camden Park in New South Wales.

Where it comes from

New Guinea to Australia

Macarthur Palm is native to the lowland rainforests of western and southern New Guinea and nearby islands, and across the water to northern Australia, in Queensland and the Northern Territory. At home it grows in wet ground near rivers and springs. The Northern Territory lists its own wild population as threatened.

Two names

Nurseries and books have called this palm Ptychosperma macarthurii for well over a century, after Sir William Macarthur of Camden Park, New South Wales, who grew thousands of kinds of plants there. The cultivated palm is now treated as P. propinquum, with macarthurii as a synonym. The plant did not change; the label will take years to catch up.

Where to find it here

Pavilion Gazebo

The clump in the photo grows in the shaded bed at the Pavilion Gazebo, next to the Black-fruit Cluster Palm, with the Royal Palms of the lawn behind it. Both palms in that bed were planted by a longtime volunteer.

Beside the Plumeria

A second clump stands in the row of palms that runs beside the Plumeria row, on the pond side of that row.

What it does

The everyday one

This is the Ptychosperma that Florida knows: a screen palm planted for a century, and steady in wind because a clump of thin trunks bends instead of breaking. The park's other three, Solitaire Palm on the Big Pond walkway and Scheffer's Palm and the Ptychosperma Palm in the palm row south of Cypress Pond, are collectors' palms. Learn the genus here on the common one, then go and find the rare ones.

How to know it

Cane-like trunks

Many slim trunks rise together from one base, two or three inches across, grey below and green just under the crownshaft, ringed with closely spaced leaf scars. Visitors expecting a tree find something closer to a stand of bamboo.

Bitten-off tips

Each leaflet ends bluntly and unevenly, as if bitten off. The leaflets sit unevenly along the leaf stalk, though all in one plane, which gives the frond a soft, slightly ragged look.

Red fruit

Branched sprays hang below the crownshaft and carry round fruit about half an inch across, green at first and bright red when ripe. There is usually some fruit on the clump at any time of year.

How it grows

New trunks from the base

The clump adds new trunks from the base as it ages, and a single planting broadens into a screen. Gardeners can split a clump to make new plants; seed works too.

Fruit most of the year

Male and female flowers grow on the same spray, so one clump sets fruit alone, and it does so most of the year. It is a palm of the wet tropics and wants steady water and warmth.
