Moses-in-the-Cradle

Tradescantia spathacea
On this page

At a glance

  • Look low, where the leaves join the stem: a pair of purple, boat-shaped bracts (special leaves), an inch or two long, cradles a cluster of small white flowers. The folded pair is the 'cradle' in the name.
  • Where it grows wild, in the forests of the Yucatán and Belize, it grows on the ground, on rocks, and sometimes up in the branches of trees.
  • Its names all see the folded bracts as a little vessel for the flowers: Moses-in-the-Cradle, Boat Lily, Cradle Lily, and in Honduras señoritas embarcadas, roughly 'young ladies on board'.

Cultural significance

A famous chromosome ring

Since 1931 biologists have used it to study chromosomes. Its 12 chromosomes link end to end in a ring, or in chains, when it makes pollen and eggs, because pieces have swapped between them over time. It is a textbook case of the oddity.

Purple on the Maya

In the Yucatán, Maya people have used a purple liquid boiled from the leaves as a cosmetic, and the plant is said to be common on old Maya ruins there.

A genus of its own

For about a century botanists kept it in a genus of its own, Rhoeo, until a 1986 study folded it into Tradescantia, the spiderworts. The name may come from a Greek myth of a woman sealed in a chest and set adrift at sea, one more boat.

Where it comes from

Yucatán forests

Wild plants grow in lowland and lower mountain forest on the Yucatán Peninsula and in neighboring Belize and Guatemala, up to about 2,000 feet above sea level, and stand from about four inches to two feet tall.

Long in gardens

It has been a garden plant for more than two centuries. It was described in 1788 from cultivated plants, and in Florida it was already growing by 1933.

What it does here

A spiderwort cousin

Moses-in-the-cradle is a spiderwort, in the same genus as the park's Purple Heart. Purple Heart sprawls on long purple stems, while Moses-in-the-cradle gathers its leaves into tight rosettes.

Color by viewpoint

From above the rosettes look mostly green. Step to the side and the wine-purple undersides take over.

How to know it

A spiral of swords

The leaves spiral around a short stem, stiff and sword-shaped, six to twelve inches long.

Older plants stand up

Older plants lift the rosette on a short stem, and the lowest part of the stem often leans over and roots where it touches the ground.

How it grows

Tough, and happy dry

Full sun or shade, poor soil, long dry spells: little bothers it, and it will even grow in cracks in a concrete wall. Wet roots are the one thing that rots it. New shoots widen the clump, and pieces of stem root where they fall.

A small variety that skips seed

Most Moses-in-the-cradle in gardens is a small variety that rarely flowers and makes no seed, so it spreads only by splitting clumps and rooting pieces. Wild plants flower freely and set seed.

Not a plant for the south

In South Florida gardeners are asked not to plant it, because it escapes into natural areas and changes the plant communities there. In central Florida the advice is to keep it from escaping.

Take care

Mind the sap

The sap can sting and itch on skin and in the eyes, so wear gloves when cutting or dividing it. Chewing the leaves irritates the mouth and throat, and a large amount can cause nausea and vomiting. Keep pets and children from eating it.
