At a glance

  • One of the rarest palms still growing wild in the United States. Florida's last wild population is on Elliott Key.
  • An earlier Florida population on Long Key was dug up and sold as ornamentals in the early twentieth century. It is gone.
  • It grows extraordinarily slowly — one or two new fronds a year, and roughly fourteen years to reach reproductive maturity.
  • Blue-gray fronds that flash silver underneath, and clusters of bright red fruit through the year.

Where it comes from

A Florida palm at the edge of a Caribbean range

Buccaneer Palm grows on Caribbean coasts — the northern Caribbean, eastern Mexico and Belize — and reaches its northern limit in the Florida Keys. It was first formally documented in the United States in 1886, historically on Elliott Key, Long Key and Sands Key.

Collected out of existence

The Long Key population is gone, and gardeners took it. In the early twentieth century collectors dug and sold the entire wild colony as ornamentals, ending a population that had stood for thousands of years. The palms on Elliott Key survived partly by being harder to reach, and fewer than fifty wild adults are thought to remain.

Where to find it here

In the Palm Exhibit. This one is nursery-grown and was planted here in 2023.

What it does here

Conservation in cultivation

A legally propagated Buccaneer Palm keeps a species with very few wild Florida individuals in living collections. Cultivation is no substitute for habitat, but it does let a threatened plant be grown without another one being taken from the wild — which is the mistake that emptied Long Key.

The flowers draw bees, and the red fruit feeds birds through much of the year.

How to know it

Trunk

A single trunk ringed with prominent circular leaf scars, sometimes slightly swollen along its length.

Fronds

Blue-gray on top and distinctly silver underneath, so the whole crown changes tone as the light moves.

Fruit

Round red berries up to about three-quarters of an inch, often two- or three-lobed depending on how many seeds ripened.

The shape is the quickest read: a small, solitary, ringed-trunk palm that looks like a Royal Palm scaled down.

How it grows

A very slow palm

It puts out only one or two new fronds a year. Even in cultivation, with irrigation and feeding, it takes about fourteen years to reach reproductive maturity — longer in the wild. That pace is part of why a collected population does not come back.

Seeds that can travel by sea

It spreads by seed alone, never by suckering. The fruits become buoyant as they dry and may be carried between islands on ocean currents, which suits a palm that colonises rocky Caribbean coastlines.

Full sun, sand or limestone, sharp drainage. Its tolerance of salt, wind and drought is excellent — this is a coastal plant, built for exposure.

Take care

The berries

The fruit skin carries calcium oxalate crystals, which can irritate sensitive skin. It is not a food plant. Worth knowing because bright red berries invite handling.
