Buccaneer Palm

Pseudophoenix sargentii

At a glance

  • One of the rarest palms still growing wild in the United States. Florida's remaining wild population is on Elliott Key.
  • An earlier Florida population on Long Key was dug up and sold as ornamentals in the early twentieth century. It is gone.
  • It grows extraordinarily slowly — only a few new fronds a year, and many years to reach reproductive maturity.
  • Blue-gray fronds flash silver underneath, and bright red fruit can appear below the crown.

Where it comes from

A Caribbean palm reaching Florida

Buccaneer Palm grows on Caribbean coasts — the northern Caribbean, eastern Mexico and Belize — and reaches the northern edge of its range in the Florida Keys, where it was first formally documented in 1886.

Collected out of existence

Florida once had a wild population on Long Key. In the early twentieth century collectors dug up the palms for the ornamental trade until that population disappeared. Elliott Key now holds Florida's remaining wild population.

Where to find it here

In the Palm Exhibit. Our specimen is nursery-grown and was planted here in 2023.

What it does here

Conservation in cultivation

Growing a propagated Buccaneer Palm keeps the species in living collections without removing another plant from the wild. That distinction matters for a palm whose Florida history includes a population lost to collecting.

The flowers are visited by bees, and birds can feed on the red fruit.

How to know it

Fronds

Blue-gray above and distinctly silver beneath. Wind or changing light can make the whole crown seem to change colour.

Trunk

A solitary trunk marked with strong circular leaf scars, sometimes slightly swollen along its length.

Fruit

Rounded fruit turns bright red, and may look two- or three-lobed depending on how many seeds develop.

Go by the whole shape: a relatively small solitary palm with a ringed trunk and a compact crown, something like a Royal Palm reduced in scale.

How it grows

A very slow palm

It produces very few new fronds each year and takes many years to become reproductive. That pace helps explain why a wild population removed by collectors cannot quickly replace itself.

Seeds, not suckers

It reproduces by seed and does not form a clump of basal shoots. Dried fruits are reported to become buoyant, which may carry seeds between coastlines on ocean currents.

Full sun, sharp drainage, and sandy or limestone soil match the conditions it grows in naturally. Once established it tolerates salt, wind and drought well.

Take care

The fruit

The fruit skin contains calcium oxalate crystals that may irritate sensitive skin. Do not treat the bright red fruit as something to eat.
