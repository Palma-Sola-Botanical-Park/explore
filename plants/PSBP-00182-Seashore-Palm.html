Seashore Palm

Allagoptera arenaria

At a glance

  • No trunk, and there never will be one you can see. The stem runs along under the ground and the fronds come straight up out of it, which is how a palm survives on a Brazilian beach.
  • Its flower spike looks like an ear of yellow corn, and it carries male and female flowers on the same cob, so one plant can set fruit on its own.
  • The fruits are thumbnail-sized coconuts, and on the coast of Brazil people eat them straight off the spike.
  • The leaflets come off the stalk at odd angles instead of lying flat, so the whole frond looks ruffled. The genus name means “changing wing”.

Where it comes from

Brazil’s beaches

The Atlantic coast of Brazil, from Sergipe down to Paraná, in the restinga: the scrubby vegetation on the sand just behind the beach. It is one of the first plants onto a bare, shifting dune. It holds the sand, shades it, builds a little soil, and the plants that could not have lived there before move in behind it.

Where to find it here

On the south walkway of the Big Pond, the stretch we call Palm Alley, where palms line both sides of a narrow walk with the pond on one hand and an open field on the other. Ours is young: a spray of fronds a yard long rising out of the grass on the pond side, right at the water’s edge, with no trunk to look for.

What it does

The palm that stays down

Everything else on the palm walk is reaching up. This one keeps its growing point below the ground, out of the salt wind, the heat and, at home, the fire. It is the lowest palm on the walk and one of the toughest, and it is a useful reminder that a palm does not have to have a trunk.

How to know it

Fronds from the sand

Feather fronds, two to six feet long, straight out of the ground in a loose cluster. There is no stem to find.

Leaflets at odd angles

Look along one frond: the leaflets point in several planes, not one flat comb, and they are silvery underneath. In a breeze the whole plant seems to swirl.

A corn-cob spike

When it flowers you get an upright yellow cob on a stalk, and then a tight cluster of little orange coconuts. Nobody has caught ours at it yet. If you do, photograph it.

How it grows

Slow, low and wide: a clump three to six feet high that spreads gradually. Full sun and sand suit it, and it shrugs off drought once settled. It has one planting quirk that catches gardeners out: the heel of its root has to sit partly above the ground. Bury it like a normal plant and it slowly suffocates.
