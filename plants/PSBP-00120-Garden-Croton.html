At a glance

  • Every croton here is the same species. Broad yellow-splashed leaves, narrow gold-dotted ones, red and burgundy: all one plant, bred into hundreds of named forms.
  • The name is a leftover. It was first described as a Croton in 1753, then moved to a genus of its own, but the old name stuck.
  • The colors follow the light: the deepest reds and oranges come in full sun, and in shade a croton greens up.

Cultural significance

From island gardens to greenhouses

Islanders across its range planted the colorful forms around their homes long before Europeans arrived. In the 1800s crotons became a favorite of heated European greenhouses, and nurseries there named variety after variety.

Where it comes from

Wild crotons grow from Malaysia and Indonesia across New Guinea to northern Australia and the western Pacific islands. The wild plant is mostly green; the colors you see here are the work of long selection.

Where to find it here

Turn left at the entrance and follow the fence. The path runs beside a shaded bed on your right, under Wild Lime, and crotons of every shape and color fill it all the way to the gazebo by the pavilion.

And everywhere else

Crotons are scattered all over the park. A newer one, planted in June 2026, fronts the left side of the cactus garden.

What it does here

Gifts from home gardens

Many of our crotons came from volunteers' own yards, which is why no two match: each one is whatever its last gardener loved. Together they keep color in the beds all year.

How to know it

Leather and loud color

Thick, glossy, leathery leaves, splashed, spotted or veined with yellow, orange, red or purple. Leaf shapes run from broad ovals to long narrow straps.

It does flower

Thin spikes rise from among the leaves. The male flowers are tiny white starbursts; the female flowers, on separate spikes, are smaller and plainer. Most people never notice them.

How it grows

Most stay a dense, upright shrub of three to eight feet. It wants warmth and bright light; a night below about forty degrees Fahrenheit can make it drop leaves, and it regrows from the stems.

Take care

Milky sap

A broken leaf or stem bleeds milky sap that irritates skin and eyes. Wash your hands after handling it. Don't eat any part of it, and keep pets from chewing it.
