Mauve Indian Mallow

Abutilon hulseanum
On this page

At a glance

  • A Florida native with orange-pink, hibiscus-like flowers that open in late winter and spring.
  • It is named for an Army surgeon who collected the first specimen on Tampa Bay in the 1830s, so science met this plant on our own stretch of coast.
  • It grows tall and loose, more a framework of stems than a dense shrub.
  • Ours were planted at the edge of Cypress Pond as part of our native bank planting.

Cultural significance

Named on Tampa Bay

The species name honors Gilbert White Hulse, a United States Army surgeon who collected plants around Tampa Bay in the 1830s. The specimen he sent north was the one John Torrey and Asa Gray described in 1838, and it is still the reference every later identification is measured against. Few plants in the park were introduced to science from so close to home.

Two names, one plant

Nurseries sell it as Red Indian Mallow and as Mauve Indian Mallow, and both are right. Botanists describe the petals as yellowish, fading pink; growers see orange-pink to mauve. The color varies from plant to plant and changes as each flower ages.

Where it comes from

It is native to Florida, Louisiana and Texas, south through Mexico and into the Caribbean. In Florida it is a plant of disturbed coastal ground: shell mounds, dunes, old groves and pastures, not marshes. A pond edge suits it for the light and the room rather than the water.

Where to find it here

At Cypress Pond, beside the crushed-shell path. Face the water and look to the left of the native Golden Cannas.

What it does here

Part of the bank planting

We bought four of these from a Florida native nursery and planted them at the pond edge beside the native Cannas, part of our work to hold the banks with native plants. In two years they went from about a foot tall to about three.

Flowers, then a colony

Bees and butterflies work the open flowers, and the plant seeds itself, so a planting spreads along the bank on its own. On a pond edge that is exactly what we want from it.

How to know it

Loose and upright

Tall, loosely branched stems, often leaning, with plenty of air between them.

Soft leaves

Heart-shaped leaves with scalloped edges, covered in fine soft hairs. Touch one and it feels like felt.

Flowers, then seed heads

Orange-pink flowers with five petals and a central column of stamens, one to a stalk, followed by round, ribbed seed heads that dry brown and split into a ring of pointed segments. By late May the flowers are over and the stems carry seed heads instead.

How it grows

Each plant is short-lived, but it seeds itself readily, so a planting can carry on as a colony. It flowers from late winter into spring and sets seed through the summer. Left alone it grows rangy, and it comes back fuller if cut back now and then.
