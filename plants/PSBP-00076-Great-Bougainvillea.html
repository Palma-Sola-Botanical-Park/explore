Great Bougainvillea

Bougainvillea spectabilis

At a glance

  • The colour is not the flower. Those papery magenta “petals” are bracts, modified leaves, and the actual flowers are the three tiny cream tubes tucked in the middle of each cluster.
  • It was collected at Rio de Janeiro in 1767 on the first French voyage around the world, by the naturalist’s assistant, Jeanne Baret, who had signed on disguised as a man. The plant was named for the commander, who stayed with the ships.
  • The thorns are hooked, and they are the plant’s climbing gear.
  • Of our two bougainvilleas this is the hairy one. Run a finger along a stem or the back of a leaf and you can feel the difference.

Cultural significance

Named for the wrong person

Louis Antoine de Bougainville commanded the expedition and got the genus. The collecting in Brazil was done by Philibert Commerson and his assistant Jeanne Baret, whose part in it was left out of the story for two centuries. By the end of the voyage she had become the first woman known to have sailed around the world.

Where it comes from

Brazil

Native to eastern and southern Brazil, scrambling through dry forest and scrub and hooking its way up whatever it can reach. From there it went everywhere warm on earth, and “paper flower” is its nickname in a dozen languages.

Where to find it here

Along Palm Alley, on the fence line. In a good spring the fence disappears under it.

What it does here

One of a pair

We grow both common bougainvilleas, and this is the place to learn to tell them apart. This one is hairier and bulkier; the Lesser Bougainvillea is smooth and a little tamer. Garden bougainvilleas are hybridised and mislabelled so freely that this is one of the few chances you will get to see the two parents side by side. The thorny thicket is also good real estate for small nesting birds. Not much gets in after them.

How to know it

Three bracts, three tiny flowers

Look into a cluster. Three papery bracts, and inside them three small cream-white tubes, which are the flowers proper.

Fuzzy stems and leaves

The stems and the undersides of the leaves carry a fine velvet of hairs. That is the quickest way to separate it from its smooth cousin.

Hooked thorns

Stiff and curved back, spaced along the stems. They are for hanging on, not for you, but they will take a sleeve.

How it grows

A woody climber that can reach thirty or forty feet given a tree, or be held to a hedge with pruning; it flowers on new growth, so cutting it back costs nothing. It blooms best a little stressed: full sun, lean soil, a deep drink now and then. Feed and water it kindly and you get a magnificent green plant with hardly any colour. It is the toughest of the bougainvilleas in a cold snap.

Take care

Mind the thorns, and the sap can irritate sensitive skin. Nothing on it is for eating.
