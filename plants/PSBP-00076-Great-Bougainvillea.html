Great Bougainvillea

Bougainvillea spectabilis

At a glance

  • The colour is not the flower. Those papery magenta “petals” are bracts, modified leaves, and the actual flowers are the three tiny cream tubes tucked in the middle of each cluster.
  • It was collected at Rio de Janeiro in 1767 on the first French voyage around the world, by the naturalist’s assistant, Jeanne Baret, who had signed on disguised as a man. The plant was named for the commander, who stayed with the ships.
  • The thorns are hooked, and they are the plant’s climbing gear.
  • Of the two common bougainvillea species, this is the hairy one. Run a finger along a stem or the back of a leaf and you can feel the velvet.

Cultural significance

Named for the wrong person

Louis Antoine de Bougainville commanded the expedition and got the genus. The collecting in Brazil was done by Philibert Commerson and his assistant Jeanne Baret, whose part in it was left out of the story for two centuries. She stayed behind on Mauritius when the ships went home, and when she finally reached France, years later, she became the first woman known to have sailed around the world.

Where to find it here

On the fence across the driveway, outside the garden proper. In a good season it does a second job: it hides the water treatment plant on the other side of the fence.

Where it comes from

Brazil

Native to eastern and southern Brazil, scrambling through dry forest and scrub and hooking its way up whatever it can reach. From there it went everywhere warm on earth, and “paper flower” is its nickname in a dozen languages.

What it does

How this one got here

A student in the park’s high-school volunteer program grew this bougainvillea and sold it at a 4-H plant sale. The park bought it and planted it on the fence, where it has been earning its keep ever since.

One of a couple

The park has a couple of bougainvilleas, this one and the Lesser Bougainvillea over by the Pavilion. This is the bigger and hairier of the two, and the thorny thicket it makes is good real estate for small nesting birds. Not much gets in after them.

How to know it

Three bracts, three tiny flowers

Look into a cluster. Three papery bracts, and inside them three small cream-white tubes, which are the flowers proper.

Fuzzy stems and leaves

The stems and the undersides of the leaves carry a fine velvet of hairs. That is the quickest way to separate it from its smooth cousin.

Hooked thorns

Stiff and curved back, spaced along the stems. They are for hanging on, not for you, but they will take a sleeve.

How it grows

A woody climber that can reach thirty or forty feet given a tree, or be held to a hedge with pruning; it flowers on new growth, so cutting it back costs nothing. It blooms best a little stressed: full sun, lean soil, a deep drink now and then. Feed and water it kindly and you get a magnificent green plant with hardly any colour.

Take care

Mind the thorns, and the sap can irritate sensitive skin. Nothing on it is for eating.
