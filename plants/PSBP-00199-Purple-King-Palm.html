Purple King Palm

Archontophoenix purpurea
On this page

At a glance

  • The palm in front of you is a youngster, knee-high with no trunk yet. In its Queensland rainforest home a grown one can stand about 80 feet tall.
  • The purple belongs to the grown palm. Just below its crown of leaves is a smooth collar of overlapping leaf bases, dark blue-grey under a waxy dust and flecked with reddish scales. Together they read as plum or purple. The scientific name, Archontophoenix purpurea, ends in the Latin word for purple.
  • King palms are a small group of six species, all from eastern Australia. Wild Purple King Palms are known from just three mountains in northeastern Queensland, and four of the six species grow in this park.

Cultural significance

Named in 1994

Botanists named the species in 1994 from a plant collected on Mount Lewis on April 17, 1993, among granite boulders at about 3,900 feet. The genus name, Archontophoenix, means ruling palm: king palm.

Where it comes from

Creeks on granite

It grows wild on Mount Lewis, Mount Spurgeon and Mount Finnigan, in rainforest along creeks on granite soil, from about 1,300 to 3,900 feet.

Where to find it here

Along the 17th Avenue path

Purple King Palm grows along the path that runs inside the 17th Avenue fence, toward the Tides End end of the park. Three king palms stand in a row there between the path and the lawn, with the tall Royal Palms behind them: Purple King Palm, then Myola King Palm, then Peach River King Palm.

What it does here

Three in a row

Purple King is the first of three king palms standing together here. Myola King Palm and Peach River King Palm are the other two, and Piccabeen Palm, the fourth species, grows elsewhere in the park. Young king palms are hard to tell apart, so the signs do the work.

Come back for it

For now there is little to see: a few feather-shaped leaves close to the ground. The trunk, the purple collar, the flowers and the large red fruit all belong to years ahead.

How to know it

Young

A young king palm has a few leaves close to the ground, each one a feather: a central stalk with leaflets spread along it. The youngest leaves may still be only partly divided.

Grown

A grown palm has a single trunk ringed with leaf scars, topped by the purple collar and long arching leaves, dark green above and silvery below.

Silver is not enough

The silvery undersides do not set it apart: Myola and Peach River have them too, and only Piccabeen is green on both sides. In the botanists' description, what is special about Purple King is silvery scales together with red-brown scales along the midrib of each leaf, a combination none of the other five king palms has.

How it grows

Slow work

Growers report that it is slower than other king palms, and the purple collar takes several years to appear.

Flowers and fruit, later

A mature palm bears branched clusters of cream flowers, tinged green, just below the collar, with male and female flowers on the same palm. The fruit that follows is crimson, three-quarters of an inch to an inch long, the largest of any king palm.

Steady water, some shelter

In the wild it grows along creeks, so it likes steady moisture, and growers advise shelter from wind.
