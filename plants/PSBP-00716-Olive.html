At a glance

  • Straight from the tree, an olive is too bitter to eat, so people cure it in salt, brine or lye. About 6,600 years ago, villagers on the coast of Israel were already doing it in large quantities; these are the oldest table olives known.
  • Olive trees need a stretch of cool winter nights to flower well. In trial plantings in southwest Florida, flowering and fruiting have been uneven, probably because winters here offer too few.
  • The silver on the underside of each leaf is a coat of tiny shield-shaped scales that reflect sunlight. When a breeze turns the leaves over, the whole tree flashes.

Cultural significance

Wood at Tel Tsaf

At Tel Tsaf, a village site in Israel's Jordan Valley, archaeologists found olive charcoal about 7,000 years old. The valley lies outside the wild olive's range. Fruit and stones can be traded over long distances, but wood suggests the trees grew nearby.

The oldest olive is two trees

The famous ancient olive of Vouves, on Crete, is said to be more than 4,000 years old. Its genes show that the fruiting top was grafted onto an older rootstock, and its age is an estimate, since the heartwood has rotted away. Girth is a poor guide: an olive judged centuries old by its size had a core that dated only to the 1800s.

Where it comes from

Where it began is still argued

Olives come from the Mediterranean region. Genetic studies disagree on whether they were domesticated once, in the northern Levant, or in more than one place around the Mediterranean.

In Florida by 1907

Florida growers were writing about olives by 1907. Researchers are still trialing varieties to find ones that crop reliably here.

What it does here

Hornworms on the menu

In Florida the big green caterpillars of the rustic sphinx moth sometimes eat olive leaves. They have been reported on olives from the Panhandle south to Polk County.

A tree with several trunks

This one divides low into several pale gray trunks under a wide, open crown. Which variety it is has not been recorded.

How to know it

Narrow leaves in pairs

The leaves are narrow and lance-shaped, set in opposite pairs along the twig, gray-green above and silvery beneath.

Pale gray trunks

Old olives divide low into several twisted trunks with pale gray, furrowed bark.

Small flowers, one hard stone

In spring, small cream-colored flowers with four lobes open in clusters along last year's shoots. If fruit sets, each olive holds one hard stone.

Not the Texas Olive

The park's Texas Olive is no relation. It has broad, rough leaves and white trumpet flowers, and its small fruit only looks like an olive.

How it grows

A heavy year, then a light one

Olives tend to crop heavily one year and lightly the next. The seeds inside the fruit are thought to be the reason: they signal the tree to skip flowering the following year.

A variety that skips the cold

A Spanish variety called Arbequina, grown in pots in a controlled test, flowered and fruited without the usual cold nights. In the Florida trials it also survives best, though its crops are still uneven.

Pollen on the wind

Wind carries the pollen. Some flowers are male only and others have both sexes, and many varieties set better fruit with pollen from a different variety.

Take care

Pollen

Olive pollen is a leading cause of hay fever and asthma where olives are grown. People who are allergic to it may feel it in spring.
