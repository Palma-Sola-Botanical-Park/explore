At a glance

  • The genus Sagittaria takes its name from the Latin for arrow — most of its members have arrowhead-shaped leaves. This one is the exception: its leaves are narrow and <b>lance-shaped</b>, which is just what ‘lancifolia’ means.
  • The starchy underground tubers — <b>duck potatoes</b> — were a staple food for Indigenous peoples across the southeastern United States and were gathered and traded widely. Ducks and muskrats eat them too, which is where the folk name comes from.
  • An <b>emergent aquatic</b> native — grows with its roots in the water and its foliage above the surface. Stabilizes shallow pond banks by rhizome.
  • The flowers are <b>three-petaled</b> and white with a yellow center — simple, clean, and worth a look up close.

Where it comes from

Worth knowing

Lanceleaf Arrowhead is one of the more historically significant plants at the water's edge. The tubers — called duck potatoes — were a reliable starchy food source for Indigenous peoples across the Southeast, harvested by wading into shallow water and loosening them from the mud with their feet or toes. The tubers float to the surface once freed.

Native to the southeastern United States and tropical America. Common in Florida freshwater marshes, pond edges, and slow-moving water. Member of Alismataceae — the water-plantain family.

They were eaten roasted or boiled and were traded between communities. European settlers adopted the same practice.

The plant is doing active ecological work in the ponds. The rhizome network stabilizes the shallow bank zone, and the emergent foliage creates habitat structure at the water surface used by fish, frogs, aquatic insects, and wading birds. Ducks and muskrats eat the tubers; blackbirds and other waterbirds eat the seeds.

Its leaves are the giveaway to its name. The genus Sagittaria is named for the bold arrowhead foliage of most of its members, but this one — lancifolia, literally ‘lance-leaved’ — is the exception, carrying smooth, narrow, tapering blades rather than deep arrowhead lobes. The common name Lanceleaf Arrowhead captures exactly that contradiction.

Where to find it here

In the Cypress Pond.

What it does here

The tubers are eaten by ducks, muskrats, and other wetland mammals. Seeds eaten by blackbirds and waterbirds. The emergent foliage creates surface habitat structure for fish, frogs, aquatic insects, and wading birds foraging at the water edge.

The rhizome network contributes to shallow bank stabilization — relevant to the park's ongoing pond erosion prevention work.

How to know it

Flowers

Three white petals with a yellow center, on branched stalks rising above the foliage. Male and female flowers sometimes on separate stalks on the same plant. Clean and simple up close.

Fruit and Seeds

Rounded heads of small flattened seeds eaten by waterbirds.

Tubers

Starchy underground tubers (duck potatoes) along the rhizomes. Edible — historically roasted or boiled. Eaten by ducks, muskrats, and other wetland wildlife.

Leaves

Lance-shaped (lanceolate) to elliptic, smooth, on long petioles rising from the water — to about 10 inches long, and variable in size. Unlike most arrowheads, the blades lack the deep arrowhead lobes; that narrow lance shape is the key ID feature for this species.

Form

Emergent aquatic perennial, 2 to 5 feet above water. Spreads by rhizomes to form colonies in shallow water and saturated soil.

Narrow, lance-shaped leaves on long petioles — not the deep arrowhead lobes the genus name suggests. The white three-petaled flowers on tall stalks above the foliage are the clincher when in bloom.

How it grows

Light

Full sun

Soil

Pond and lake edges, freshwater and brackish marshes, muddy stream margins

Salt

Low; little tolerance for direct salt spray

Size

2 to 5 feet (occasionally taller); clumps 1 to 3 feet wide

Habit

Emergent aquatic perennial, clump-forming
