Lanceleaf Arrowhead

Sagittaria lancifolia
On this page

At a glance

  • Lanceleaf Arrowhead, also called Duck Potato, is native to Florida and much of the southeastern United States through tropical America.
  • It grows with roots and rhizomes in saturated soil or shallow water while the leaves and flowers stand above the surface.
  • Unlike the deeply arrow-shaped leaves of some Sagittaria, this species has long lance-shaped blades.
  • UF/IFAS says it is not salt tolerant. That matters at Palma Sola, where bay-connected ponds have repeatedly made this species difficult to establish.

Where it comes from

Kew accepts Sagittaria lancifolia and gives a native range from the southeastern United States through tropical America. UF/IFAS describes it as common in Florida swamps, wet prairies, ditches and shallow pond, lake and stream margins.

What it does here

The pond chemistry decides

Lanceleaf Arrowhead is a common recommendation for fostering pond health, but the park has struggled to grow it in its brackish water — the ponds are saltier than typical inland freshwater ponds because they connect to the bay by pipe. A lone survivor was photographed at Cypress Pond, and an earlier sighting years before that turned up in the same area — two independent glimpses of a plant that keeps trying to hold on.

A freshwater plant waiting for its moment

The salt content in the ponds is going down every month. As that continues, the park expects freshwater plants like this one — common in freshwater ponds all over Florida — to have a resurgence.

How to know it

The leaf is the shortcut

Long, firm lance-shaped blades rise on tall petioles. They lack the deep backward-pointing lobes people expect from a classic arrowhead leaf.

Three white petals

Flower stalks rise above the foliage and carry conspicuous three-petaled white flowers.

A fan from below

Leaves emerge in a rosette from underground rhizomes.

Seed heads become rough balls

Many small hooked fruits crowd together after flowering.

How it grows

Shallow water or saturated soil

UF/IFAS recommends it for pond and lake margins and other continuously wet sites.

Rhizomes build colonies

Underground growth lets an established plant spread sideways through suitable mud.

Fresh is better than salty

UF/IFAS explicitly describes Duck Potato as not salt tolerant. The park's survival pattern is therefore an ecological clue, not just bad luck.

Useful on pond edges

Florida stormwater guidance recommends the species for the water's-edge zone, where emergent vegetation can help stabilize margins and take up nutrients.
