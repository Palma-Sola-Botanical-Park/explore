At a glance

  • Moringa is nicknamed the Drumstick Tree for its long pods, which are eaten as a vegetable. Ounce for ounce, a raw pod has about two and a half times the vitamin C of an orange.
  • Despite its feathery, bean-like leaves, moringa is a relative of the papaya, not of beans or peas.
  • It grows fast. From seed it can reach as much as 20 feet in a single year.
  • Crushed seeds make muddy water clump and settle, which is why people in places without clean water use them. The cleared water can still carry germs, so it isn't safe to drink.

Cultural significance

A vegetable that grows on a tree

In South Asian cooking the young green pods go into curries and sambar. Eaters scrape or chew the soft pulp and seeds out and leave the tough, stringy casing behind. The small leaflets are cooked as greens, and in some places the flowers are eaten too.

The leaf claims

Posts about moringa often say the leaves beat oranges for vitamin C, milk for calcium and bananas for potassium. Fresh leaves are a good food, with about as much vitamin C as an orange and more calcium and iron than spinach, but less potassium than a banana. Dried leaf powder is far more concentrated, and that is where the big comparisons come from.

Oil in the seed

Its species name, oleifera, means oil-bearing. About two-fifths of a mature seed is a pale oil, called ben oil, used in perfume and cosmetics and as a fine lubricant. The park's Neem is another tree whose seeds are pressed for oil, though the two are not related.

Where it comes from

Dry forest in India

Moringa probably began in the dry lowland forests of northwestern India and neighboring Pakistan, though few recent collections exist to say for sure. People carried it across tropical Asia and Africa long ago, and by the 1870s it was in Caribbean gardens. The oldest Florida specimen we know of was pressed in Key West in 1891.

The slim one of its genus

The moringa genus has 13 species. Four of them, in Namibia, Kenya, Ethiopia and Madagascar, are bottle trees with swollen trunks that store water. The moringa here is the slim, fast one.

What it does

Shoots from a stump

That sawed-off trunk ends in a flat top crowned with leafy new shoots. This is how moringa answers being cut or frozen back: it regrows from the trunk, and growers cut it on purpose to keep the leaves within reach.

Flowers for bees

Cream-colored flowers open in loose clusters among the fine leaves, and honey bees collect the nectar over a long season. In South Asia, carpenter bees and sunbirds are the main pollinators.

How to know it

A leaf within a leaf

Each leaf is divided two or three times: the stalk branches, and each branch branches again into small, round-tipped leaflets. What looks like a twig of tiny leaves is one large leaf.

An open crown

From a distance it is a light, open crown on pale gray bark, with sky showing through the branches.

Three-sided pods

The hanging pods have three angled, ribbed sides and are often a foot or longer. Dry ones split into three to release seeds with three papery wings, and they can stay on the tree for months.

A smell of horseradish

The foliage smells of horseradish, and botanists use that smell to tell it from legumes with similar leaves.

How it grows

Sun, sand and a dry season

Full sun and well-drained soil suit it. A dry season makes it drop its leaves, and a tree under steady drought makes far fewer, but it can flower while bare. Cuttings root easily when pushed into the ground.

Take care

Avoid the roots

Do not eat the roots or root bark. They contain an alkaloid reported to be harmful.
