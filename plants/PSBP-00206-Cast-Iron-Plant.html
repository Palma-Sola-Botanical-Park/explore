Cast Iron Plant

At a glance

  • The leaves are the plant. The flowers open at the soil surface, under the leaves and the litter, and most people who grow it never see one.
  • Those flowers look and smell like small mushrooms, and fungus gnats pollinate them. For a century textbooks guessed slugs.
  • No stem: each leaf rises on its own from a creeping rhizome under the mulch.
  • From the forest floor of southern Japan. A Victorian parlour plant, and the plant George Orwell put in the title of a novel.

Cultural significance

The aspidistra in the window

The aspidistra was the houseplant of Victorian and Edwardian Britain because it survived gas light, coal smoke and forgetful watering, and by the twentieth century it stood for respectable, unadventurous home life. George Orwell built his 1936 novel Keep the Aspidistra Flying on that meaning, and the music-hall singer Gracie Fields had a hit with The Biggest Aspidistra in the World two years later.

Where to find it here

Shade Garden

In the Shade Garden, at the bottom of the steps below the office, beside the Triangle Ficus. It is one small clump in the mulch for now.

Where it comes from

Southern Japan

Cast Iron Plant grows wild on the forest floor of southern Japan, under evergreen broadleaf trees where little light reaches the ground. That is the whole explanation of its fame: it is built for dim, dry, root-filled places, which is what a parlour or a porch is to a plant. It reached Europe in the early nineteenth century and the American South not long after.

The name

Aspidistra comes from the Greek for a small round shield, for the shape of the stigma that caps the flower; elatior means taller.

What it does

A trial for deep shade

This clump was planted in August 2026 as a trial of a groundcover for deep shade. If it takes, more will follow. It is a plant that asks for patience: it spreads a few inches a year, and if it ever flowers here, the flowers will be at your feet, under the leaves, in late winter or spring.

How to know it

Leaves from the ground

Dark green, leathery, strap-shaped leaves, up to two feet long and three or four inches wide, each rising straight from the ground on its own stalk. No stem, no branches.

Look under the leaves

Part the leaves at the base in late winter or spring and look in the mulch. The flowers are fleshy, purplish-brown, about an inch across, with a domed cap in the middle, and they sit at the soil surface.

A flower dressed as a fungus

That domed cap is the stigma, and the flower's shape and musty smell copy a small mushroom. Fungus gnats looking for a place to lay eggs crawl in, find nothing, and carry pollen to the next flower. A field study in southern Japan, published in 2017, watched them do it.

How it grows

Most of it is underground

Most of the plant is underground: a network of thick rhizomes that stores food, sends up leaves and flowers, and creeps outward slowly. New leaves appear around the edge of the clump. Gardeners divide the rhizomes to make more plants.

Shade is the point

It wants shade. In Florida sun the leaves bleach and scorch, so the Shade Garden is the right home, not just a convenient one. Once settled it puts up with dry soil better than most shade groundcovers. Slow at every stage.
