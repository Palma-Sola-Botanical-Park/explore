Old Man Palm

Coccothrinax crinita

At a glance

  • The shaggy coat is not bark and not moss. Every new leaf brings a skirt of pale fibre with it, and the fibre stays after the leaf is gone, so the trunk wears years of leaves at once.
  • It grows wild in one corner of western Cuba and nowhere else. Cuba's own plant red list rates it Critically Endangered; the IUCN lists it as Endangered.
  • Cubans have put the fibre to work for generations: rope, brooms, hats and mattress stuffing.
  • Ours stands on the west bank of Big Pond, a few palms up from its short-haired cousin at the corner.

Cultural significance

A useful beard

The fibre that makes this palm so easy to spot has been useful for as long as people have lived near it. In western Cuba it was twisted into rope, bound into brooms, woven into hats and purses, and pulled off in armfuls to stuff mattresses and pillows; the trunks went for fence posts. One of its Spanish names, palma petate, means the woven-mat palm. The same usefulness is now part of the problem: with so few palms left, every beard harvested is a wild palm stripped.

Where it comes from

Western Cuba

Old Man Palm is native only to Cuba. Botanists recognise two subspecies. The long-haired one, the palm on this page, grows in the west around Bahía Honda, on open, stony ground including scrub on serpentine soils that few plants tolerate. The short-haired subspecies comes from central Cuba.

Down to one district

The wild population is small and has been shrinking, from land clearing, grazing and fibre cutting. Cuba's plant red list assessed the long-haired subspecies as Critically Endangered in 2017; the IUCN's global listing for the species is Endangered. Counts published over the years disagree with each other, so this page gives none.

Where to find it here

West bank of Big Pond

Old Man Palm grows on the west side of Big Pond. From the south walkway, turn north up the west bank: the Short Hair Old Man Palm stands at the corner, then Caranday Palm, Queen Palm and European Fan Palm, and the Old Man Palm is next, its beard right beside the water.

When

The fan leaves and the beard are there all year. Flower stalks, when they come, hang below the crown in spring and summer, and the small fruit ripen dark by late summer.

What it does here

Both Cuban subspecies on one bank

Most of the rare palms in this collection could pass for their neighbours from ten feet away. This one cannot. It is part of a collection of unusual palms that a longtime volunteer planted here over many years, and both Cuban subspecies went in on the same bank: the long-haired Old Man Palm here and the Short Hair Old Man Palm at the corner. Walk the few palms between them and you have seen the two ends of Cuba side by side.

How to know it

The beard

Long, pale brown fibres wrap the trunk from the ground to the crown. On a young or middle-aged palm like ours they hide the stem completely; the trunk inside is only a few inches across.

Fan leaves, silver below

Above the fibre, the crown is a set of nearly round fan leaves, not feather fronds. They are green on top and distinctly paler, almost silver, underneath; turn one over.

Compare with the Short Hair Old Man Palm

The Short Hair Old Man Palm at the corner of the pond is the same species from the other end of Cuba. Its fibre is shorter and thinner, and its trunk shows through. If you can see bare trunk between the fibres, you are at the corner, not here.

How it grows

Slowly, one leaf at a time

This is a very slow palm, and the coat is the record of it. Each leaf adds one layer of fibre, so a thick beard means a great many leaves and a great many years. Old palms in Cuba eventually lose the lowest fibres to wind and show a slender bare trunk beneath a shaggy top.

Poor ground, full sun

At home it grows on poor, stony, seasonally dry ground in full sun, and it asks for the same here: sun, sharp drainage, no standing water. Its flowers carry both male and female parts, so a single palm can set fruit on its own.
