Pentas

Pentas lanceolata

At a glance

  • If you plant these for butterflies, the variety matters more than the colour. The newer compact dwarf ones were bred to stay tidy and many of them barely produce nectar - pretty, and close to useless to a butterfly. The older, taller, looser kinds are the ones worth buying.
  • Most of the country grows pentas as a summer annual that dies with the first cold. Ours have simply never stopped, and bloom straight through the year.
  • The flowers are shallow on purpose. Butterflies with short tongues cannot reach into deep blooms at all, so a shallow tube like this opens the plant up to a far wider range of visitors than most nectar flowers get.
  • It is in the coffee family. Wild Coffee, Firebush and Jungle Flame elsewhere in the park are the other cousins.

Cultural significance

The plant people buy when they want butterflies

Pentas has become one of the standard nectar plants of Florida gardening, and for good reason - it blooms without a break, it is easy, and the insects find it. Its one catch is the breeding. Decades of selecting for compact, uniform, shippable plants have produced varieties that look the part and do not feed anything. It is worth knowing before you spend the money.

Where it comes from

Grassland and forest edge in East Africa

Native to East Africa and Yemen, in open grassland and along the margins of forest - bright, disturbed, sunny ground, which is exactly the situation it has been given here.

Sunbirds there, hummingbirds here

In Africa these flowers are worked by sunbirds, which fill much the same role hummingbirds do in the Americas. The plant crossed an ocean and found the arrangement waiting for it. Ruby-throated Hummingbirds moving through Tampa Bay in autumn stop at pentas readily, and go for the red ones first.

Where to find it here

Right outside the office

They are planted along the pavers at the office, framing the way into the shade garden. You will pass them without trying.

What it does here

Planted by students, and still theirs

These went in during 2025, put there by high school volunteers, and the same crew has been back to weed and water them ever since. They are not a display that was installed and forgotten. If they look good when you walk past, that is a group of teenagers keeping their word.

The busiest few square feet at the entrance

Because they flower without a break, there is nearly always something feeding on them. Butterflies and bees most days, hummingbirds in autumn - often several kinds at once on a single plant.

See how many you can count

Stand at the pavers on a warm morning and give it two minutes. Count the different butterflies working the flowers, not the number of butterflies. Three or four species at the same time is a normal day here. Tell us if you beat it.

How to know it

Count to five

Every single flower is a five-pointed star - five petals, and five of everything else behind them. The plant is named for it and it never varies. Worth checking once just to see that it holds.

Domed clusters at the tips

The flowers gather into dense heads about three inches across at the end of each branch, in red, pink, lavender or white. From any distance the clusters are what you see, not the individual stars.

Soft, ribbed leaves

Lance-shaped and medium green, with fine hairs over both surfaces that give them a slightly soft feel, and strong veins standing out underneath. The leaf shape is where lanceolata comes from.

How it grows

Three or four feet, and quick about it

An upright evergreen shrub that grows fast, stays under about four feet, and rewards having its spent flower heads pinched off - it responds by making more.

Sun, and not much else

Full sun and decent drainage is the whole requirement. It is short-lived compared with a real shrub, but stem cuttings root easily, so a planting like this one is simple to keep going indefinitely.
