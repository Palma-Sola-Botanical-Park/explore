At a glance

  • There are three latan palms in the world, one for each of the Mascarene Islands east of Madagascar: the Red Latan on Réunion, the Blue Latan on Mauritius, the Yellow Latan on Rodrigues. We grow the red and the blue, seven palms apart.
  • The red belongs to young plants. New leaf stalks, veins and leaf edges flush wine-red; as the palm ages the whole crown goes silver-green. Ours has.
  • It is Endangered on Réunion, where a few wild stands survive on the coast.
  • Male and female flowers grow on separate palms. Ours has flowered; no fruit so far.

Cultural significance

Latanier rouge

On Réunion this is the latanier rouge, and the genus name Latania is that island word carried into botanical Latin. Most palm names honour a botanist or describe a leaf; this one keeps the name the people of the Mascarenes already used for the plant before anyone arrived to describe it.

Where it comes from

Réunion

Red Latan is native only to Réunion, the volcanic French island east of Madagascar. It grew along the dry, sunny coast, and most of that coast has been cleared for farms and towns over the past two centuries. The IUCN listed it as Endangered in 1998; what survives in the wild is a handful of coastal stands, and far more of the species now grows in gardens than on the island.

Three islands, three latans

The genus has exactly three species, each on its own island, and all three are on the IUCN's threatened lists. Our Blue Latan page tells the Mauritius half of the story: a small island where goats and rabbits were removed and the palms came back.

Where to find it here

Along Cypress Pond

Red Latan Palm grows in the row of unusual palms just south of our Plumeria collection, on the south side of Cypress Pond. Starting from the Red Cabbage Palm end of the row it is the second palm in. The Blue Latan Palm is seven palms further along the same row.

Go ahead and get closer

There is no formal path through this palm collection yet. You are welcome to walk across the open grass to see the palms and read their signs.

What it does here

Half of a pair

This palm and the Blue Latan Palm seven places along give visitors a comparison no book can: two island species whose names promise a colour difference, standing at the same age in the same row, and looking the same. They are part of a collection of unusual palms that a longtime volunteer planted here over many years. Look at both, then look at the newest leaf on each; that is where any colour will be.

How to know it

Look at the newest leaf

Do not judge the name by the old leaves. The red is strongest on young leaf stalks, veins and edges, and fades as each leaf matures. On our palm the crown is silver-green all through; if any red is left it will be on the newest leaf at the centre.

The fan and the trunk

Big, stiff fans on long stalks, with a rib that continues into the blade and folds it like a half-open book. The trunk is short so far and wrapped in old leaf bases woven in a diamond pattern. Small teeth run along the edges of the leaf stalks; mind them.

Three silver fans

From a distance a mature Red Latan, a Blue Latan and a Bismarck Palm all read as big silver fans. The Bismarck Palm further along this row is much larger; the Blue Latan seven palms along is the honest comparison, and at this age only the label separates them.

How it grows

Sun and drainage

A palm of dry, sunny coast: it wants full sun and ground that drains, and it handles a dry spell once established. Bradenton is at the cool edge of its range, and a hard winter can brown the fronds.

Separate sexes

Each palm is male or female. The branched flower stalks come from among the leaf bases in spring, and only a female palm with a male within reach of the pollinators can set the pear-shaped brown fruit. Ours has flowered; we have not seen fruit, and we are watching.
