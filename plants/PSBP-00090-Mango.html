At a glance

  • Mango belongs to the cashew family, the same plant family as poison ivy. The ripe flesh is edible, but sap and peel can cause contact dermatitis in sensitive people.
  • New leaves often emerge coppery red or pink before hardening to glossy green.
  • A mango fruit develops from one of hundreds or thousands of tiny flowers carried in a branched panicle at the end of a shoot.
  • Kew's current native-range treatment is narrower than the old record: Assam through Myanmar and Thailand to southern Yunnan, rather than all of South Asia.

Cultural significance

Thousands of years of cultivation

Mango has been cultivated in South and Southeast Asia for millennia and was carried through tropical trade networks long before it reached Florida.

A Florida fruit tradition

UF/IFAS records the first Florida introduction in 1833. South Florida later became an important center for named cultivars and home fruit growing.

Where it comes from

Assam to southern Yunnan

Kew accepts Mangifera indica and gives its native range from Assam through the eastern Himalaya, Myanmar and Thailand to southern Yunnan in China. It is now cultivated throughout the tropics and warm subtropics.

Where to find it here

South end of the collection

The mangoes stand toward the south end of the Rare Fruit collection — further from the main entrance, on the right. Several trees grow together here, each labeled with its cultivar. Step off the crushed shell path to read the signs and see the different named trees for yourself.

What it does here

More than one mango

As of September 2026, the collection includes eight named cultivars — Bailey's Marvel, Cogshall, Dot, Falan, Ice Cream, Mallika, Rosigold and Pickering — each with its own fruiting season, flavor and growth habit, turning “mango” from one generic tree into a small demonstration of cultivated diversity. Trees come and go; check the signs at each tree for the current list.

How to know it

Dense evergreen crown

Mature trees develop a broad canopy of long, leathery leaves.

Watch the new growth

Fresh leaves can be pink, amber or reddish before turning dark green.

Flowers come by the hundreds

Branched panicles at shoot tips carry many tiny pale flowers.

Fruit varies with the cultivar

Shape, size, skin color, fiber and flavor differ enormously, so fruit color alone is a poor species-level identification clue.

How it grows

Florida fruiting depends on a dry, cool season

UF/IFAS notes that mango flowering is favored by cool temperatures and dry conditions. Warm wet winters can reduce bloom.

Trees can become large without pruning

UF/IFAS gives unpruned landscape heights of roughly 30 to 100 feet depending on cultivar and conditions; fruit trees are commonly kept much lower for harvest and storm management.

Standing water is a problem

Mango tolerates seasonal dryness better than prolonged flooding and does best in well-drained ground.

Take care

Ripe flesh is food; sap and peel are different

Mango sap and peel contain allergenic compounds related to those in poison ivy. People who react strongly to poison ivy may develop a rash after handling unpeeled fruit or cut stems. Leave park fruit and plant material in place.
