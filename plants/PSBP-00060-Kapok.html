At a glance

  • The fluff inside the seed pods is hollow and waxy, so it floats and sheds water. Kapok filled life jackets through the Second World War.
  • A hurricane snapped off the top third of our tree and dropped it into Cypress Pond. The canopy has grown back strongly since, so look up.
  • The flowers open at night, on bare branches, and are pollinated by bats.

Cultural significance

The World Tree

The Maya held the ceiba sacred as the World Tree: roots in the underworld, trunk in the world of people, branches in the heavens. It is Guatemala's national tree, and across its range an old kapok often stands where a village gathers.

Stuffing, not thread

The fiber is too short and slippery to spin into thread, so it went into pillows, mattresses and cushions instead, until foam and synthetics took over.

Where it comes from

Kapok is native to tropical America, from Mexico and the Caribbean to northern South America, and to West Africa. Few trees are native on both sides of the Atlantic.

Relatives in the park

Its closest relative here is the Silk Floss Tree by the office, in the same genus. The Red Silk Cotton Trees beside it and the Baobab toward Serenity Garden belong to the same branch of the family.

Where to find it here

Coming from the office, it is the first big tree you reach at the front of Cypress Pond, on the right of the crushed-shell path.

Two spiny neighbors

To its left stand two Red Silk Cotton Trees. Their gray, spiny trunks look much like the Kapok's, but they grow in a completely different way: low, level tiers of branches, where the Kapok holds its crown high on a bare trunk. Look up to see the difference.

What it does here

Back from the storm

A hurricane in the early 2020s broke off the top third of the tree, and that whole section came down in Cypress Pond. The trunk carried on. New branches pushed out below the break, and the canopy has filled back in far faster than anyone expected.

The osprey's table

Before the storm, an osprey would carry a tilapia up to one of the Kapok's high branches, a branch that is gone now, and eat it there, dropping the bones on a volunteer spreading mulch below. If you see an osprey up there again, tell us.

How to know it

Look up

A tall, straight gray trunk runs bare for much of its height before the crown spreads out. That silhouette is the quickest clue from a distance.

Spines, but scattered

Small, sharp spines are scattered over smooth gray bark. The Red Silk Cotton Trees beside it are studded far more thickly.

Leaves like a hand

Each leaf is five to nine narrow leaflets spread from one point, like fingers.

How it grows

Kapok grows fast. In rainforest it is an emergent, pushing above the forest canopy to 200 feet or more.

Buttresses

At ground level the trunk flares into broad buttress roots, which brace a tall tree in soft, wet ground.

Flowers before leaves

It drops its leaves in the dry season and flowers on the bare branches. The flowers open after dark and smell sour to us; the scent is for bats.

Take care

Watch the trunk

The trunk carries sharp spines. Look before you lean on it.
