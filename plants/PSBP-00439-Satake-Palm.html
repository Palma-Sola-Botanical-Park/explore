Satake Palm

Satakentia liukiuensis

At a glance

  • It grows wild on two small Japanese islands and nowhere else, and it is the only species in its genus. Its nearest relatives are not other Asian palms but two from Vanuatu and Fiji, thousands of miles to the south. Nobody has explained how it got there.
  • The seeds die if they dry out even for a day. They have to go into soil almost as they are picked, which is most of why this palm is rare in gardens.
  • It is named for Toshihiko Satake, who ran a rice-milling company and spent his life on palms. He found it on Ishigaki; a colleague at Cornell put his name on it.
  • Watch the crownshaft, the smooth collar under the fronds. On a mature palm it turns reddish-brown to purple.

Cultural significance

A protected grove

The main wild grove, at Yonehara on Ishigaki, is a national natural monument in Japan. Nothing may be dug or collected there, and a small museum near the grove is dedicated to Satake and his palm.

Where to find it here

At the far east end of Palm Alley, the walk along the south side of the Big Pond. Coming over the Rare Fruit bridge it is the first palm you meet, pond side, with the Açaí next along. The walk turns back here.

Where it comes from

Two islands in the Ryukyus

Ishigaki and Iriomote, at the far southwestern tip of Japan’s Ryukyu chain, on sheltered hillsides in moist subtropical forest kept mild all year by the ocean. The wild trees are few and seem to be getting fewer, and nobody is sure why.

What it does

A rare palm that can take our winters

A palm that is scarce everywhere, growing outdoors in Bradenton. Its tropical cousins would not survive a cold night here; this one takes a brief dip to the mid-twenties, which is unusual for its family and the reason it is worth trying. Ours is young, the trunk just forming, the crownshaft still green. The colour comes with age.

How to know it

A ringed trunk

Smooth and columnar, marked with neat rings where each frond has fallen.

The crownshaft

The collar of leaf bases below the crown is smooth and, on a grown palm, reddish-brown to purple. On ours it is still green; come back for it.

A tidy crown

Ten to fourteen fronds with evenly spaced leaflets, so the crown looks combed rather than shaggy. Fruit ripens from yellow to black.

How it grows

Moderately slow. Young plants like some shade and steady moisture; grown ones take full sun. It is easy about soil, limestone included. Forty to sixty-five feet in the wild; expect thirty or forty here, in time.
