Ptychosperma Palm

Ptychosperma microcarpum

At a glance

  • A clump of thin ringed trunks from one small corner of Papua New Guinea: the lowland forest around Port Moresby, in Central Province.
  • The name means small fruit. They ripen red, about half an inch long, on sprays below the crownshaft.
  • One of four Ptychosperma palms in the park, and by far the rarest in cultivation. It was hardly grown anywhere outside New Guinea before the 1970s.
  • Not known to be threatened at home, but hardly anyone has looked: the IUCN lists it as Least Concern and notes that few collections have been made since the 1980s.

Where it comes from

Near Port Moresby

Ptychosperma microcarpum is native to the Central Province of Papua New Guinea, in lowland rainforest near the coast around Port Moresby. Its whole known range is about 940 square miles.

Little known

Botanists have collected it only a handful of times since the 1970s and 80s. The IUCN Red List rates it Least Concern and asks for new surveys, noting that road-building near Port Moresby could press on its forest. It reached specialist palm collections in the 1970s and botanical gardens in Hawaii and Florida in the 1990s, and it is still rare outside them.

Where to find it here

Cypress Pond

This palm stands in the row of palms along the path on the south side of Cypress Pond, right at the edge of the path, a few steps from Scheffer's Palm, its clumping cousin.

What it does here

The fourth Ptychosperma

The park grows four palms of this genus, and it is a good place to learn to see it: a smooth green crownshaft, leaflets with bitten-off tips, red fruit around a grooved seed. Solitaire Palm on the Big Pond walkway grows one trunk; Macarthur Palm at the Pavilion Gazebo, Scheffer's Palm a few steps from here, and this one all grow as clumps. This is the one you are least likely to meet anywhere else.

How to know it

A clump, not a tree

Many thin grey-brown trunks rise together from one base, each closely ringed with old leaf scars and each topped by its own green crownshaft. It reads as a thicket, not a tree.

Small red fruit

The fruit are small, red when ripe, and about half an inch long, each with one grooved seed. The name microcarpum records the size.

Strands of flowers

The flower sprays come out below the crownshaft as a bundle of thin pale green branches, each strung with tiny flowers, male and female on the same spray.

Bitten-off tips

Each leaflet ends bluntly and unevenly, as if bitten off. Every palm in this genus has that, so it tells you the genus, and the signs tell you the rest.

How it grows

A lowland palm

At home it grows in wet lowland forest, often near rivers. It wants steady water and some shade while young.

New trunks from the base

New trunks keep coming up from the base while the oldest die back, so the clump renews itself. It is one plant; count the crownshafts and you have counted its trunks.
