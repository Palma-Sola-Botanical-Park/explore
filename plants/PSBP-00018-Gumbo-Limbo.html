Gumbo Limbo

Bursera simaruba

At a glance

  • Copper-red bark peels away in thin curls — which is why Florida calls it the Tourist Tree.
  • A cut branch stuck in the ground will root, even a surprisingly large one.
  • The fruit splits to show a red fatty coat around the seed that birds come for.
  • It is the caterpillar host for the Dingy Purplewing.

Cultural significance

Living fence posts

Because large cuttings root so readily, people have planted branches as fence posts and let them grow, a practice known from Florida's Indigenous peoples and across the tropical Americas — a fence that is alive instead of dead wood.

Resin and wood

The resin has been used for glue, varnish and incense, and the soft light wood carves easily.

Where it comes from

Native to tropical America and the Caribbean, reaching the United States naturally in southern Florida, where it is characteristic of tropical hardwood hammocks in the Keys and the far southern peninsula.

Where to find it here

At the Front Pond.

When

The peeling bark identifies it year-round. Look for the small fruits in spring.

What it does here

Fruit for birds

The capsule opens to expose a seed half-wrapped in red, fatty tissue. Fruit-eating birds take the tissue and carry the seed away.

A butterfly host

Dingy Purplewing caterpillars feed on Gumbo Limbo. That butterfly belongs to the Keys and extreme South Florida — so this is a relationship worth knowing about rather than something to expect to see here.

How to know it

Start with the bark

Smooth copper-red bark peeling in thin curling sheets, paler bark underneath.

Leaves

Each leaf divides into several narrow leaflets rather than being one blade.

Fruit

Small capsules that split to show the bright red coat around the seed.

Take in the whole tree: thick spreading branches, an open irregular crown, and that bark. Nothing else here looks like it.

How it grows

A branch can become a tree

It roots from cuttings more readily than almost anything else here — even a substantial piece of branch will take when it is planted into suitable ground.

Drops leaves when water is scarce

It is semi-deciduous and can shed part of its canopy in a drought, cutting water loss until the rain returns.

Fast growth in warm conditions, and it takes sandy, rocky and limestone soils. An established tree needs less water than its lush look suggests.

Take care

The fruit

The fruit is edible but not reliably pleasant, and it is not worth sampling from the tree.
