At a glance

  • The holiday amaryllis is not an Amaryllis. It is a Hippeastrum, a bulb from Central and South America; the true Amaryllis is a South African lily.
  • Planted outdoors in Florida, it skips the holidays and flowers in early spring. Ours have bloomed in March and April.
  • Its leaves feed the convict caterpillar of the Spanish moth, even though the bulb is poisonous to people and pets.

Cultural significance

Knight's star

In 1821 the English botanist William Herbert separated these American bulbs from the African Amaryllis and named them Hippeastrum, which he put into English as knight's-star-lily. He left the old name to the South African belladonna lily out of deference to Linnaeus. Gardeners went on calling his new genus amaryllis anyway, and botanists argued over the name for decades until the International Botanical Congress settled it in 1987, Herbert's way.

The watch-hand maker's cross

Around 1799 a Mr. Johnson of Prescot, near Liverpool, who made gold hands for watches, crossed two species in his greenhouse and raised the first recorded amaryllis hybrid. An accident wrecked the greenhouse in 1802; only five or six bulbs survived, and he gave them away. Breeders have been crossing species ever since, and most of today's big-flowered hybrids come from Dutch and South African nurseries.

Where to find it here

Framing the office parking area

As you pull into the office parking area, look at the bed that frames it. Amaryllis grows there among the Giant Milkweed, the Firecracker plant and the Beautyberry. Come in spring for the flowers; the rest of the year it is a clump of long strap leaves.

Where it comes from

American bulbs

The wild species, more than a hundred of them, grow from Central America south through South America, most of them in Brazil and the Andes of Peru and Bolivia. In the wild many are pollinated by hummingbirds, which suits a big red trumpet. Garden amaryllis are hybrids of several species, so no single wild range fits the plants in our bed.

What it does

The convict caterpillar

Amaryllis is a food plant for the Spanish moth, whose caterpillar is hard to mistake: up to two inches long, black ringed with cream bands, orange at the head and the tail, with an eye-like spot at each end so it is hard to tell which end is which. They feed in groups on the leaves and even tunnel into the bulbs. Our Tree Crinum is on their menu too.

Lubbers like it

Eastern Lubber Grasshoppers, big and slow, commonly strip the leaves of amaryllis and its crinum relatives in Florida. The poison that makes the bulb dangerous to us does not keep either insect away.

How to know it

Trumpets on a bare stalk

A thick, leafless stalk, hollow inside, rises from the bulb and opens two to six big trumpets at its top. On the large hybrids each flower is five to eight inches across.

Strap leaves

Long, smooth, strap-shaped leaves come from the base, with or after the flowers, and stay through the warm months.

Red means bruised

Look for red streaks on the leaves or stalks. Almost any injury to an amaryllis turns its tissue red, so a red mark is usually a bruise rather than a disease.

How it grows

One bulb becomes a clump

Each bulb makes small offset bulbs around its base, and they flower after about two years. In Florida the bulbs can stay in the ground for years, so a planting thickens into a clump instead of fading after one show. Through the summer the leaves are filling the bulb for next spring's flowers.

Why the pot blooms at Christmas

Holiday bulbs are forced: dug, dried and stored by the grower, then potted to flower three to six weeks later. Put in the ground here, a bulb goes back to its own calendar and flowers in early spring.

Take care

Poisonous, the bulb most of all

Every part is poisonous to eat, the bulb most of all. In people it causes nausea and vomiting. In dogs and cats it causes vomiting, drooling and diarrhea, and a large amount can affect the heart. Keep pets from chewing the leaves or digging up bulbs.
