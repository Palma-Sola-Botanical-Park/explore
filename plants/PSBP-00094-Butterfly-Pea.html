Butterfly Pea

Clitoria ternatea

At a glance

  • Steep the flowers and the water turns deep blue. Add lemon and it shifts to purple, then pink: the pigments change color with acidity.
  • That blue colors rice in Malaysia and iced drinks in Thailand, and since 2021 it has been approved as a food color in the United States.
  • The flower grows upside down. The big petal sits at the bottom, so a bee that lands on it picks up pollen on its back.
  • It is named for the butterfly shape of the flower, not for butterflies.

Cultural significance

A rare natural blue

Blue is scarce among natural food colors, and the few there are tend to fade. These flower pigments, called ternatins, hold their color better than most, even in baking. In Malaysia they tint nasi kerabu, a blue rice; Peranakan cooks use them in sticky-rice sweets; in Thailand they go into a blue iced drink.

A name botanists objected to

In 1678 the botanist Jacob Breyne named the flower for its resemblance to part of the female anatomy, and Linnaeus kept the name, Clitoria, in 1753. Nineteenth-century botanists objected and proposed replacements, among them Vexillaria, meaning flag-like. None stuck. Ternatea refers to Ternate, an island in the Moluccas.

Pasture, pest spray, folk medicine

Australian farmers grow it for pasture and hay, with yields comparable to alfalfa. An extract of the plant, rich in small ring-shaped proteins called cyclotides, has been registered in Australia since 2016–17 as an insecticide for cotton. In India's Ayurvedic tradition it is Aparajita, one of several plants sold as a memory herb.

Where it comes from

Nobody is quite sure

It has been carried around the tropics for so long that its homeland is a puzzle. The name points to the Moluccas, and Linnaeus listed it from India, but the most careful botanical accounting treats both as introductions and places its native range in tropical Africa and Arabia, with outposts in southern China and Taiwan.

In Florida

It is not native to Florida. It has escaped in a few southern counties, but it is not on the state's invasive plant list.

What it does here

Root partners

Like most legumes it forms nodules on its roots, where bacteria turn nitrogen from the air into fertilizer the plant can use. That is why farmers plow it back in as green manure to feed the soil.

Butterflies after all

The name comes from the flower, but Florida butterfly gardeners list it as caterpillar food for two skippers, the Long-tailed Skipper and the Dorantes Longtail. If you find a leaf folded over and tied with silk, a skipper caterpillar may be hiding inside.

Our vine

Our photographs show it winding up a leaning pole and threading along a wire, with the flowers hanging below the leaves. It has no tendrils; the stem itself does the climbing.

How to know it

Upside down

The big rounded petal, blue with a white-and-yellow center, sits at the bottom of the flower, with the smaller folded petals above it: the reverse of a garden pea. Flowers are about one and a half to two inches across, one or two where a leaf meets the stem.

Count the leaflets

Follow one leaf stalk: it carries five to seven oval leaflets, sometimes nine, with one at the tip. Florida's native look-alikes, Atlantic pigeonwings and spurred butterfly pea, have just three, and paler lavender flowers.

Flat pods

Flat pods two to four and a half inches long follow the flowers, drying brown and splitting to release six to ten dark, shiny seeds.

How it grows

Fast and tender

It grows fast and flowers over a long season in warm weather. A light frost kills the tops and it regrows from the stems or the base; farther north it is grown as an annual.

It plants itself

Dry pods split, and seedlings come up nearby, so a patch can spread beyond the reach of its vines. In Western Australia and the Northern Territory it is listed as an environmental weed.

Take care

Leave the seeds

Cooks use the flowers, not the seeds. The seeds are a strong laxative; don't eat them.
