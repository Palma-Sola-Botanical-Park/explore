Mexican Plumeria

Plumeria rubra

At a glance

  • Its flowers make no nectar. Sphinx moths come for the scent at night and leave with pollen but nothing to eat.
  • Flower color changes from tree to tree in our collection: white and yellow, pale pink, hot pink.
  • In winter it drops its leaves, and the thick bare branches can look dead. They usually aren't.

Cultural significance

Lei flowers

In Hawaii plumeria is one of the best-known lei flowers, the blossoms strung by the dozen into garlands.

Where it comes from

It is native to Mexico, Central America and northern South America. It was carried around the tropics so long ago that many people think of it as Hawaiian or Asian.

Where to find it here

Walk south on the crushed-shell path past the cactus garden. Beyond the cycads at its south end, the Plumeria row runs along the south side of Cypress Pond, with the palm collection beyond it.

What it does here

A row of individuals

Every tree in the row is a little different in color and in scent, which makes it worth walking slowly in bloom season. Smell two or three and compare.

How to know it

Candelabra branches

Thick, blunt, forking branches, like a candelabra.

Flowers at the tips

Clusters of waxy, five-petaled flowers sit right at the branch ends, with long, glossy leaves crowded just below them.

How it grows

Bare in winter

It drops its leaves for the cool, dry season and grows them back with the warmth, flowering through the warm months.

Kept true by cuttings

A cut branch, left to dry, will root. That is how a favorite color is kept true; a tree grown from seed can come out different.

Take care

Milky sap

Broken stems and leaves bleed a milky sap that irritates skin and eyes. Leave the branches unbroken.
