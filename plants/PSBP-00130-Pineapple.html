Pineapple

Ananas comosus
On this page

At a glance

  • Pineapple is a bromeliad, related to the air plants and Spanish moss visitors see in Florida.
  • What looks like one fruit is a fused structure produced from many flowers.
  • A single rosette fruits once. New side shoots can continue the plant after the original fruiting rosette declines.
  • The leafy crown of a harvested pineapple can be rooted and grown into another plant.

Cultural significance

Cultivated in the Americas for thousands of years

UF/IFAS describes pineapple as an ancient crop of Indigenous tropical American and Caribbean peoples. European voyages then carried it around the tropics.

Where it comes from

Costa Rica through tropical South America

Kew accepts Ananas comosus. Its species-level native range extends from Costa Rica through tropical South America, while Kew also notes that the familiar cultivated pineapple is an ancient seedless cultigen whose exact domestication history is complicated.

What it does here

A bromeliad you can eat

The park's pineapple makes the family connection easy to see: a ground-level rosette of stiff leaves, not a fruit tree, producing one central flower-and-fruit structure.

This one has already fed the park

The Cacti and Succulents plant fruited in 2026, and park staff ate one of its pineapples.

How to know it

A ground rosette, not a tree

Long stiff leaves radiate from a short central stem.

Flowers fuse into the fruit

Individual flowers are visible on the developing central structure; their ovaries and surrounding tissues merge into the familiar pineapple.

The “eyes” preserve the pattern

Each polygonal eye on the mature fruit corresponds to part of that original flower cluster.

A crown grows on top

The tuft of leaves above the fruit is a vegetative shoot capable of rooting.

How it grows

One main fruit per rosette

After the primary rosette flowers and fruits, it does not simply repeat the same cycle indefinitely.

Side shoots carry the planting forward

Suckers and slips can develop around the parent and are commonly used for propagation.

Warmth and drainage matter

UF/IFAS describes pineapple as well adapted to warm climates and well-drained soils, with poor tolerance of frost and waterlogged roots.
