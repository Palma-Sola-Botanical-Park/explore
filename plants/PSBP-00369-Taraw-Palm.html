At a glance

  • Mind the teeth. Big, curved teeth line both edges of the leaf stalks. Give this palm some room.
  • The fan droops. A deep green fan splits into long segments, and the tips of those segments are split too and hang down.
  • A rib bends the fan. The stalk runs on into the blade as a central rib that curves the whole leaf, and the segments are folded into V-shaped pleats.
  • The fruit is blue. When it fruits, the round, grape-sized fruit ripens to a glossy blue. Each one holds a single seed.
  • Flowers tuck into the crown. Small yellow flowers appear among the leaf bases, on stalks that branch again and again.

Cultural significance

A broom, with those teeth?

In the Philippines, people have made brooms from this palm. Some accounts say they used the young leaves while they were still folded, which gives a long, flexible broom about five feet long. Picture the stalks, lined with teeth, and we can only guess how they handled them.

Roofs, hats and a blowpipe

The leaves have been used for thatching, and some accounts say they were laid on house roofs like shingles and sewn down with strips of rattan. They have also been sewn together into raincoats and sun hats. The stiff midrib of a leaf is said to have served as a blowpipe. We are not sure we believe that one 100%.

Where it comes from

Taraw is a palm of Southeast Asia, including Indonesia and the Philippines. In the wild it grows in forest, even in freshwater and peat-swamp forest, so it is a palm that is comfortable with wet feet. People also plant it as an ornamental well beyond its home.

Where to find it here

At the end of the line

Ours stands outside the gate at the southwest tip of our fence line, the last of the palms we planted along the outside. That gate opens only to haul out debris and let service vehicles in, so almost no one has ever stopped to look at this one. There is no sign. It has stood out here unlabeled and unnoticed, and now at least you know its name.

Is it worth the trip? We think so. Those teeth are something to see, and almost no one has seen them up close. If you are out along the fence line, stop and say hello. And if you know how it came to be planted out there, tell us.

How to know it

Start with the stalk

Heavy teeth arm the leaf stalk between the trunk and the blade. On some palms the stalk is tinged red or orange. Look at the stalk before you look at the fan.

A rib inside a pleated fan

The stalk carries on into the blade as a central rib, which bends the whole fan into a curve. A full-sized fan can be about five feet across. The segments are folded into V-shaped pleats, and their split ends hang down past the stiffer middle. Stand back and you will see the droop.

Marks left by old leaves

The trunk is a single upright stem, and it wears the wedge-shaped bases of its old leaves. Look low on the trunk.

How it grows

Flowers on a branching stalk

Flower stalks emerge among the leaf bases and divide again and again into smaller branches. Each yellow flower has both male and female parts.

Blue fruit

The round fruit is about the size of a grape, and a single seed develops inside. It ripens to a glossy blue, or a blue-violet. Check the crown if you visit, and tell us if you spot any.

Take care

Keep clear of the leaf stalks. The large teeth can puncture or cut skin. I mean, look at those things!
