Yellow Trumpet Tree

Tabebuia aurea
On this page

At a glance

  • The last wild Spix's Macaws, the blue parrots of Brazil, lived among these trees along dry-country creeks and nested in hollows in the old ones. The last wild bird vanished in 2000, and in 2022 Brazil began releasing captive-bred birds back into those same woods.
  • It drops its leaves first and then flowers, so for a few weeks in winter or early spring the whole crown is yellow with nothing green in it.
  • The silver in the leaves is a coat of tiny scales, not hairs. That one detail decides which group of trumpet trees it belongs to: the hairy-leaved yellow ones were moved to a new genus in 2007, and this one stayed.
  • It has the weakest wood of any tree on Florida's tropical wind-resistance list. After a storm, look for broken limbs.

Cultural significance

Ant wood

The genus name is from the Tupi language of Brazil and means ant wood, because many of its relatives have hollow twigs that ants move into.

Where it comes from

Dry South America

It comes from the seasonally dry country of Brazil, Bolivia, Paraguay, Peru and northern Argentina, not from the Caribbean, whatever one of its names says. It was planted in south Florida as a street tree and has seeded itself into a few disturbed spots there; nobody has reported it causing trouble.

What it does

The macaw's tree

In Brazil's Caatinga, the last Spix's Macaws roosted, fed and nested in old caraibeiras along the seasonal creeks near Curaçá, using woodpecker holes and the hollows left by broken branches. In 2013 surveyors mapped the habitat from the air by its October bloom. The birds released since 2022 nest in boxes strapped to the trees.

Who pollinates it

In a Brazilian study, fourteen kinds of bee visited the flowers but only a few big ones, carpenter-sized Centris bees and a bumblebee, actually pollinated them. A flower needs pollen from another tree, and about one in six set a pod, each holding around ninety winged seeds.

How to know it

Silver leaves, yellow trumpets

Each leaf is a hand of five to seven oblong leaflets, pale and silvery from the scales on both faces. The flowers are yellow trumpets in loose clusters at the branch tips, and the pods are slender capsules that split to release papery winged seeds.

How it grows

Small here, big in Coral Gables

A landscape tree usually stops at fifteen to twenty-five feet, though Florida's champion, at a botanical center in Coral Gables, stands fifty-three feet. It grows at a moderate pace in full sun and well-drained soil.
