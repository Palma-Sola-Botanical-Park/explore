Arikury Palm

Syagrus schizophylla

At a glance

  • Look closely at the leaflets: many end in a small notch or split rather than a simple point. Even the scientific name, schizophylla, carries the same idea — Greek for divided leaf.
  • The petiole bases carry conspicuous flattened teeth — unusual on a feather palm, and one of the sharpest clues that you have the right plant.
  • The orange fruit is edible, with sweet fibrous pulp — gathered from the wild in coastal Brazil the way you might pick fruit off any backyard tree.
  • For a genus that includes the towering Queen Palm, this one stays remarkably small. A Florida landscape specimen is usually only around twelve feet tall and six feet wide — and ours is nowhere near that yet, still a low clump of fronds at the foot of the Baobab.

Cultural significance

An old Manatee County connection

Arikury Palm has been part of Florida horticulture for more than a century, and some of that history happened almost next door. Reasoner's Royal Palm Nursery in Oneco — Florida's oldest nursery, founded in 1881 — was probably the first Florida nursery to grow it, sometime in the early 1900s. By 1918 or 1919, seed from those Oneco plants had already been sent on to botanical collections in Cuba.

What looks like a small collector's palm has had much more ordinary uses where it grows naturally. Beyond the edible fruit, the leaves have been used for roofing and for weaving hats and other handicrafts.

Where it comes from

Brazil's Atlantic coast

Arikury Palm is native only to eastern Brazil, from Pernambuco south to northern Espírito Santo. It is especially associated with restinga — sandy coastal vegetation growing just inland from the Atlantic, in soils that are low-nutrient, seasonally dry, and exposed to salt spray.

Fruit gathered from the wild

In coastal Brazil the edible fruit is collected from wild palms for local use — a practical food crop from a plant that elsewhere gets treated as a collector's palm.

Where to find it here

Coming from the Galleria along the Big Pond, use the Baobab as your landmark. The Arikury is the low, compact palm directly behind it — easy to overlook beside one of the biggest personalities in the garden.

What it does

There is something useful about seeing this palm next to the Baobab. The Baobab dominates the view; the Arikury stays low and contained. It is a quiet reminder that a palm does not have to become a fifty-foot tree to be fully grown.

How to know it

What you will see today

A low clump of deep-green feather leaves at the foot of the Baobab, with no trunk to speak of. The tight, upright crown of a grown Arikury is years away; for now the leaves fan out close to the ground. Even so, step back and the plant reads as compact and slightly bristly — nothing like the loose, arching fountain of a young Queen Palm.

Leaflet tips

Many of the leaflets end in a notch or split rather than a clean point. It is a small detail, but once you know to look for it the leaves become much easier to recognize — and it is already there on a plant this young.

Petiole teeth

The leaf stems carry conspicuous flattened teeth near their bases — one of the most distinctive features of the species, and one of only a few Syagrus palms with toothed petioles. Look rather than grab.

A trunk still to come

On an established plant the old leaf bases stay attached rather than dropping cleanly, building a rough, spiraling texture up the slender trunk. On ours the trunk is a fibrous stub a few inches high. Come back in a few years and that spiral is the thing to look for.

How it grows

This is naturally a small palm, not a young giant waiting to happen. Around twelve feet tall and six feet wide is a normal Florida landscape size, and that is roughly its ceiling.

Slow, but Florida helps

Arikury is not a fast palm anywhere, but Florida's warmth speeds it up considerably compared to cooler climates where collectors work for years to see much trunk at all.

Flowers and fruit

It starts flowering while still young. The small orange-yellow fruit follows winter and spring flowers, ripening in clusters along the inflorescence.

What it needs

Full sun and well-drained soil suit it best. It handles dry spells well once established, and its restinga background gives it moderate tolerance for salt spray — useful for a coastal Florida garden, though not among the most salt-proof palms in the genus.
