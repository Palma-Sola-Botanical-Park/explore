Chinese Fan Palm

Livistona chinensis

At a glance

  • Look for the droop. The stiff, ribbed fan ends in long, split tips that hang, so the whole leaf spills over like a fountain. That fits its other common name, Fountain Palm.
  • Bristly stalks mean a young palm. The leaf stalks of a young Chinese Fan are lined with sharp spines. On an adult there are usually fewer.
  • It takes its time. A young palm can sit close to the ground for years before a trunk starts to rise.
  • A heavy crop bends the crown. When fruit ripens on the long flower stalks, the weight can push the lower leaves down. The crown then looks like two tiers.
  • It needs elbow room. A grown palm's crown spreads ten to twelve feet across.

Cultural significance

In China, dried fronds have been made into hand fans, brooms and umbrellas, and by some accounts rain hats and raincoats.

In Japan this palm is called biro, and it has a long link to the imperial court. In the Heian period, they say, leaves from Aoshima, a small island off Miyazaki, were sent to Kyoto to cover the ox carts of nobles. In Okinawa it grows in sacred groves, where priestesses are said to have made ritual fans and mats from its leaves.

Where it comes from

Chinese Fan Palm is an East Asian palm. People plant it in warm places around the world for its broad fans and hanging leaf tips. Around here it is a fairly common landscape palm, so you may already know it.

Where to find it here

This palm stands on the back side of the Butterfly Garden, just to the left of the Fiji Fan Palm. Don't miss the Fiji Fan: it is the more dramatic of the two. Not many visitors wander back this far, because no obvious trail steers you here. But you are free to explore, so come on back and take a look around.

What it does

In Hong Kong, bats fold the leaves into tents

In Hong Kong, Short-nosed Fruit Bats roost under these fronds. A male bites through the midrib of a leaf, and the leaf folds down into a tent that shelters the bats beneath it.

How to know it

Start with the leaf stalk

The stalk runs on into the blade as a central rib, and the blade then splits into many narrow segments. Look for the rib and the droop together, because the fan shape alone does not tell this palm from other fan palms.

Look for the waterfall

Stand back and look at the edge of the fan. Every stiff segment ends in a tip that hangs, so the fronds dip like a waterfall. Together with the rib in the leaf stalk, that droop is the quickest way to know this palm.

How it grows

Seeds travel with animals

Birds and small mammals eat the fruit and carry the seeds away. The seeds sprout easily, so young palms can turn up away from the parent.

Wait for brown fronds

Like many palms, this one passes potassium back to the tree from a frond as it dies. So trim only fronds that are completely brown. A dead frond can hang on for a year or longer, and you can cut it sooner.

Take care

Keep hands clear of the sharp leaf-stalk spines, especially on young palms.
