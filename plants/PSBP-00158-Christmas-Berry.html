Christmas Berry

At a glance

  • Wintering whooping cranes on the Texas coast feast on these berries. In early winter they can supply up to half of a crane's energy.
  • It is a Florida native of the salt-marsh edge and grows wild in Manatee County. Its close cousins give us goji berries.
  • In a dry spell it drops its leaves to save water, then grows a new set after the next rain.

Cultural significance

A December name

The red berries are most plentiful around December, which is where the name comes from. It also goes by Carolina wolfberry and Carolina desert-thorn.

Same name, other plants

In Florida, "Christmas berry" is also a nickname for Brazilian pepper, the invasive tree that is now illegal to sell or plant here, and Hawaii uses the name for the same pest. In California it means toyon. This is the native one.

Goji's wild cousin

Goji berries come from two Asian species in the same genus. Some wild-food writers call our native's ripe berries edible, sweet with a hint of tomato, which fits a plant in the tomato and pepper family. Others won't vouch for them, and the green fruit is considered toxic. Ours are left for the birds.

A borrowed name

Lycium comes from lykion, an ancient Greek name for a thorny shrub from Lycia, in what is now southwest Turkey. That shrub was probably a buckthorn. The name ended up on these plants instead.

Where it comes from

The salty edge

It grows wild along the coast from South Carolina to Texas and on into Mexico: at the upper edge of salt marshes, along mangroves, on dunes and on old shell mounds. Salt spray and salty soil that would kill most garden shrubs don't bother it.

Close to home

It is native to Manatee County. Botanists collected it in 2017 at the edge of the high salt marsh at Terra Ceia, near the mouth of Tampa Bay.

What it does here

Nectar, then fruit

The flowers feed butterflies, among them Great Southern Whites and Gulf Fritillaries, as well as sphinx moths and bees. Birds eat the berries that follow.

Crane food

The last wild flock of whooping cranes reaches Aransas, on the Texas coast, in late October and November, just as the berries peak, and eats them alongside blue crabs. In feeding trials, the berries gave cranes more usable energy and fat, pound for pound, than any other winter food tested.

Ours is young

Our photographs from May 2026 show young, slim upright shoots with no flowers or fruit yet. Given time, suckers from the base should thicken a plant into a many-stemmed shrub. Come back in late fall to look for flowers and berries.

How to know it

Touch a leaf

From a few steps away, a young plant looks like a sprig of rosemary. Touch a leaf: it is plump, smooth and soft, a little club about an inch long, not a stiff needle.

Lavender stars, red eggs

The flowers are about half an inch across, with four (now and then five) spreading lobes, deep lavender to bluish white. The berries are egg-shaped, up to about half an inch long, green at first and bright red when ripe.

Thorn-tipped

Older branches end in stiff, sharp points. A young shoot may show none yet.

Not the other Christmas berry

Brazilian pepper is a tree. Its leaves are made of several leaflets and smell peppery when crushed, and its small red berries come in dense clusters. This is a low shrub with single, fleshy leaves.

How it grows

Leaves on demand

When the ground dries out it sheds its leaves, which saves water, and the next wet spell brings new ones. A bare plant in the spring dry season is usually resting, not dead.

From one stem to a thicket

Suckers come up around the base and turn one plant into a many-stemmed thicket, usually three to eight feet tall.

The season

It flowers mainly from late summer into fall, with a few flowers almost any time of year. The berries ripen through late fall and early winter.

Take care

Thorns and green fruit

Older branches are thorn-tipped. Don't eat the green fruit or the leaves; like other plants in the nightshade family they carry toxic alkaloids. Keep pets from chewing it.
