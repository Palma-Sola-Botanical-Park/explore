At a glance

  • A fan palm from the middle of Australia's desert. Its wild home is a handful of gorges on the Finke River, where groundwater seeping from the sandstone keeps a few thousand palms alive in country that gets under a foot of rain a year.
  • For a century botanists called them survivors of a wetter age. A 2012 genetic study found them almost identical to a palm six hundred miles to the north and separated from it only about fifteen thousand years, recent enough that people may have carried the seed south. Arrernte tradition had said as much.
  • Young leaves come up red to bronze in full sun and turn green as they age. That is the red in the name; a mature palm shows none of it.
  • Ours stands at the Plumeria end of the palm row south of Cypress Pond, the first palm in the row from the crushed-shell path.

Cultural significance

Whose palms

For the Western Arrernte people Palm Valley is a living place, and their tradition told of the palms coming from the north long before the genetics did. Whether people actually carried the seed is unproven; that the story and the DNA point the same way is the interesting part.

Cabbage

The cabbage in the name is the growing bud, eaten as a vegetable, as with Florida's own Cabbage Palmetto. Cutting it kills the palm.

Where it comes from

Central Australia

Native to the southern Northern Territory, almost entirely within Finke Gorge National Park: Palm Valley and a few neighboring gorges of the Finke River, where water seeps from the rock all year. Around them is red desert. The palms live on hidden water.

A six-hundred-mile puzzle

Its nearest relative, the Mataranka palm, grows some six hundred miles to the north. The 2012 study found the two nearly identical and dated their split to roughly fifteen thousand years ago, far too recent for the old story of a rainforest relic. The authors suggested people carried the seed south. It remains a hypothesis, not a documented event.

Where to find it here

South of Cypress Pond

Take the crushed-shell path south past the Cacti and Succulent tiers to Cypress Pond. Beyond the pond, the Plumeria and the row of palms open out on your left. The Red Cabbage Palm is the first palm in that row from the path, right beside the Plumeria: a single trunk with a big round crown of fans.

Go ahead and get closer

There is no formal path through this palm collection yet. You are welcome to walk across the open grass to see the palms and read their signs. Watch for low fronds and other plantings as you go.

What it does here

Head of the row

It is the first palm most people reach in this collection and the easiest to name. From here the row runs along the Plumeria, twenty-odd palms from around the world, each with a sign.

A desert palm in a wet park

Its wild population is small and listed as Vulnerable under Australian law (as of 2026). Palms grown far from Finke Gorge, like this one, carry some of that very small gene pool.

How to know it

A big round crown

One straight trunk with a dense, almost spherical crown of large fan leaves, each split into segments whose tips hang down.

Old leaf bases

The trunk keeps the split, woody bases of old leaf stalks for years. They fall away with age and leave a grey, ringed column.

Teeth on the stalk

Young leaves carry teeth along the edges of the stalk, a close-range clue while the palm is small.

Red only when young

On a young palm in full sun the newest leaves are red. Ours is past that stage, so look for the crown and the trunk, not the color.

How it grows

Slow at first

Years as a stemless rosette of fans, then a trunk that can reach fifty feet here and a hundred in the wild.

Sun and drainage

Full sun and drained ground. It takes drought once established; regular water and feeding move it along faster.

Fruit

Round black fruit, the largest of any Australian fan palm, taken by birds where it grows wild.

Watched at home

At home the threats named in the national recovery plan are groundwater pumping, fire, invasive grasses, tourism and a drying climate.
