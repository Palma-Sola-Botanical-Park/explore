Longan

At a glance

  • Longan and lychee are close relatives in the soapberry family.
  • Peel away the thin tan shell and the translucent flesh reveals one glossy dark seed, producing the eye-like appearance behind the name “dragon eye.”
  • Fruit hangs in loose clusters and ripens in Florida mainly during summer.
  • Kew gives a much broader native range than the old record's “southern China through Southeast Asia” summary: the Indian subcontinent through southern China and into northwestern Peninsular Malaysia.

Cultural significance

A long-established Asian fruit

Longan is eaten fresh and dried and is used in drinks, desserts and cooked dishes across parts of Asia. Its “dragon eye” name comes from the appearance of the peeled fruit rather than from the tree itself.

Where it comes from

Indian subcontinent to Malaysia

Kew accepts Dimocarpus longan and gives a native range from the Indian subcontinent through southern China to northwestern Peninsular Malaysia, with additional native populations across parts of Southeast Asia.

Where to find it here

Along the path, before the gazebo

Right along the crushed shell path, on the right side, before you reach the Rare Fruit gazebo.

When

UF/IFAS gives July through August as the main Florida fruit season, although timing varies with weather and the tree.

What it does here

A useful comparison with lychee

Longan lets visitors compare two close relatives without needing flowers or a botanical key: longan fruit has a smooth tan shell, while lychee is better known for its rough red skin.

How to know it

Look for paired leaflets

The evergreen leaves are compound, with several leathery leaflets arranged along a central stalk.

Small cream flower clusters

Branched panicles appear before the fruit crop.

Tan fruit in bunches

The round fruits hang in clusters and develop a thin leathery tan-brown shell.

One dark seed

The peeled fruit's translucent flesh and single shiny seed are its easiest memorable clue when fruit is present.

How it grows

Warm climate, well-drained soil

UF/IFAS recommends sunny, well-drained sites and notes that established trees can become large unless pruned.

Flowering can be unreliable

In Florida, inconsistent bloom is a major production problem. Commercial growers in some regions use potassium chlorate to induce flowering, but that is a production technique, not a visitor-care recommendation.

Near Threatened in the wild

Kew currently carries an IUCN Near Threatened assessment for the species. Cultivated abundance does not necessarily describe the condition of wild populations.
