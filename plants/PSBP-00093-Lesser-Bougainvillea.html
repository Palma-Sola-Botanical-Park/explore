Lesser Bougainvillea

Bougainvillea glabra
On this page

At a glance

  • Glabra means smooth. Stroke a stem: no fuzz. That is the difference between this and the Great Bougainvillea.
  • As with every bougainvillea, the colour is in papery bracts, not petals. The real flowers are the little cream tubes at the centre.
  • Most bougainvilleas in gardens are hybrids, and this smooth species is the parent of a great many of them.
  • It blooms hardest when life is a bit hard: sun, poor soil, not much water.

Where it comes from

Brazil

Native to Brazil, like its hairier cousin, in the same dry scrubby country. It is the more compact and more free-flowering of the two, which is why the nursery trade built so many of its favourites on it.

Where to find it here

In the open field by the Pavilion, on the side nearest the Pavilion Gazebo, close to the fence along the front of the park.

What it does here

The other one

The park has a couple of bougainvilleas: this one, the smooth species, and the Great Bougainvillea on the fence across the driveway, the hairy one. The dense spiny growth shelters small birds’ nests, and bees and butterflies visit the true flowers, small as they are.

How to know it

Smooth stems and leaves

No velvet on the stems or under the leaves. The leaves are a slightly lighter, brighter green too, and a touch smaller.

Papery bracts, tiny flowers

Three bracts around three cream tubes, magenta on ours. The bracts last for weeks after the flowers inside are done.

Thorny and leaning

It would rather scramble than stand up. Left alone it leans on a neighbour; pruned, it makes a rounded shrub.

How it grows

Ten to twenty feet as a climber, easily kept smaller. Full sun, poor well-drained soil and a stingy hand with the hose bring the colour; shade and rich feeding bring leaves. A mature plant in the right spot is nearly indestructible and can live for decades.

Take care

Thorns, and a sap that some skins dislike. Not for eating.
