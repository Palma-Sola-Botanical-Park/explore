Pink Rain Lily

Zephyranthes robusta
On this page

At a glance

  • A heavy rain sets off the flowers. Within a few days a patch of grass-like leaves fills with pale pink blooms, each lasting only a day or two, and then the show is over until the next downpour.
  • Each bulb makes baby bulbs beside it, so a handful can grow into a border. Volunteers here have divided crowded clumps, moved bulbs to bare beds and brought some from their own gardens.
  • It comes from southern Brazil, Argentina and Uruguay, where dry spells alternate with rain. Rain lilies in the wild drop their leaves in dry weather and wake when the rain returns.

Cultural significance

A name that moved

You may see this plant labeled Habranthus robustus, the name William Herbert gave it in 1829. A later botanist moved it into Zephyranthes in 1888. Zephyranthes means west-wind flower, after Zephyrus, the Greek god of the west wind.

Where it comes from

Carried by gardeners

Gardeners carried it out of South America to warm climates around the world, and it is recorded as introduced in places as far apart as Florida and Mauritius.

Cousins in the park

It belongs to the amaryllis family, and the park's Tree Crinum and Amaryllis are relatives.

Where to find it here

Along the edges of the plant beds around the office, and on into the Cacti and Succulents garden. One volunteer brought the bulbs over from their own yard a few at a time and tucked them in along the bed edges wherever there was room. Most days you walk past without noticing. Come right after a rain and the edges turn pink.

What it does

In flower here

Here it has flowered in early June and late July 2026, and in June 2025. Between flushes the patch looks like a tuft of grass.

Who visits

Nobody has recorded which insects visit the flowers here. In a Brazilian relative, bees did the pollinating. A bee on one of these flowers would be worth a look.

How to know it

Grass, then flowers

Narrow, grass-like leaves grow in tufts from the bulb. The flower stalks rise separately, usually 6 to 12 inches tall, each topped by one funnel-shaped flower of six pink to lavender-pink segments around pale stamens. Look at a flower from the side: it is held at a slight tilt, not straight up.

How it grows

Bulbs that multiply

Small offset bulbs crowd around the parent bulb, and a clump can be divided, each bulb growing on its own. A pollinated flower makes a capsule of shiny black seeds.

Rain beats the hose

Watering can bring some flowers, but not as many as a real rain does. Between flushes the bulb waits underground.

Take care

Do not eat

Do not eat the leaves, flowers or bulbs. Rain lilies contain lycorine, a poison that can cause vomiting and diarrhea and, rarely, worse. Keep pets and children from digging up the bulbs. The sap may irritate skin.
