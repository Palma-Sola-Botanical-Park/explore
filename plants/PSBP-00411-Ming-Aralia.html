Ming Aralia

At a glance

  • Stress it, with drought or a hard prune or a move, and it gives off a smell from its leaves somewhere between spice and stale coffee. It is the plant’s way of complaining.
  • In Vietnam it is a kitchen herb and a tonic: leaves in fish salad and spring rolls, roots steeped in rice wine. They call it the poor man’s ginseng.
  • The leaves are cut and cut again until they look like parsley or a fern, on a woody shrub that can be trained like a bonsai.
  • “Ming” is a marketing name from the American nursery trade of the 1950s. The plant has nothing to do with China.

Cultural significance

A herb, not a houseplant

Across Vietnam and Southeast Asia this is an everyday plant, called đinh lăng, grown by the kitchen door for its leaves and dug for its roots. The tonic reputation rests on the same family of compounds found in true ginseng. Whether it does what ginseng is said to do is another question; that it has been used that way for centuries is not.

Where it comes from

Somewhere in the western Pacific

Nobody quite knows. It has been in cultivation so long, from Sulawesi and the Moluccas through New Guinea to northern Australia and across the old trade routes into Asia, that the wild original is lost in the garden versions.

Where to find it here

In the Shade Garden: a narrow column of frilly green beside the bench and the little statue, with the stepping stones at its feet.

What it does here

Lace among the big leaves

Everything around it in the Shade Garden has big leaves. This has the finest foliage in that corner, a haze of green that breaks up the broad shapes. It is at its cold limit in Bradenton and will not flower for us, so it is here for texture, and for the smell if you brush it.

How to know it

Fern-like leaves on a woody shrub

Each leaf is divided two or three times into narrow, glossy, raggedly toothed leaflets, so a single leaf reads as a spray of parsley. Ours is the compact form sold as ‘Elegans’.

A crooked column

Upright and many-stemmed, the branches a little gnarled, the foliage bunched along them. Left alone it makes a narrow shrub taller than you.

How it grows

Slowly, in shade or morning sun, in soil that stays moist but never sodden. Dry it out and it drops leaves; freeze it hard and it dies back. It takes shaping well, which is why people bonsai it. Cuttings root easily.

Take care

The leaves are eaten in Vietnam, but ours are not on the menu. Nothing here needs a warning beyond that.
