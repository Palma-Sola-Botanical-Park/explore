Bottle Palm

At a glance

  • The bottle is a young palm's shape: the trunk swells hardest early on and stretches out as the palm grows taller.
  • In the wild it grows only on Round Island, off Mauritius. By 1988 rabbits and goats had left just eight mature trees; once the rabbits were gone, seedlings came up by the hundreds.
  • It carries only four to eight leaves at a time, so the crown always looks a little sparse.
  • Our Spindle Palms are its sisters. The Bottle swells near the ground; the Spindle is fattest around the middle.

Cultural significance

Flask and fodder

The species name, lagenicaulis, is Latin for flask-stemmed. The genus name, Hyophorbe, given in 1791, is Greek for pig food, probably because the fruit of these palms was fed to pigs.

Common in gardens, rare at home

It is planted in warm gardens around the world, South Florida included, for that trunk. The recovery on Round Island did not come from garden palms, though. It came from the island's own last trees, and those few turned out to carry more genetic variety than the garden palms do.

Where it comes from

Round Island

Round Island is a steep volcanic island about fourteen miles off the north coast of Mauritius, roughly forty to fifty times the size of our park. It holds the last scrap of the palm-rich lowland forest that once covered the dry coast of Mauritius.

Rabbits, goats and erosion

Rabbits were on the island by 1810 and goats arrived in the mid-1800s. Between them they stripped the hardwood forest, the soil washed into the sea, and young palms stopped surviving. The last goat was shot in 1979 and the rabbits were poisoned out in 1986. Seedlings started surviving again almost at once, and young palms from that wave were bearing fruit by the late 1990s.

Still on the list

It has been listed as Critically Endangered since a 1998 assessment, and in 2025 the island's conservation managers reported strong, continuing regeneration. All five Hyophorbe palms come from the Mascarene Islands, Mauritius, Réunion and Rodrigues, and all are threatened; one is down to a single known tree.

What it does here

Bottle and spindle

Its sister, the Spindle Palm from Rodrigues, grows here too: on Welcome Island in front of the Galleria, and along the Big Pond's south walkway. Seeing both is the quickest way to learn the difference, and the Spindle is the more likely of the two to come through a frost.

Flowers from below

Our Back Gate palm flowered in May 2026. The flower stalks push out from just below the crownshaft, curved like horns at first, then open into a broom of small cream flowers, male and female on the same stalk. The fruit that follows is about an inch across and ripens from green to black.

How to know it

Step back for the bottle

From across the path the outline gives it away: a smooth gray trunk, fattest near the ground and narrowing upward, a short bright green crownshaft, and a few arching leaves on top.

Close rings

Up close, the trunk is banded with closely spaced rings, the scars of old leaves, right up to the crownshaft.

Leaflets in a V

Each long, arching leaf carries narrow leaflets about two feet long, angled up from the midrib so the leaf folds into a shallow V.

How it grows

What the bottle is for

What the swelling does is less settled than you would think. It is often said to store water and just as often said not to, and we have not found anyone who measured it.

Slow, and only from seed

It grows slowly and never suckers, so every new palm starts as a seed. That is why animals eating the seedlings could stall the whole species.

Near its cold limit

A freeze will most likely kill it, which puts us near the northern edge of where it can grow outdoors. Salt spray barely bothers it, as you would expect of a palm from a wind-swept ocean island.

Take care

Gloves for the fruit

The fruit can irritate skin. Wear gloves if you handle it.
