Mexican Petunia

Ruellia simplex
On this page

At a glance

  • A wet capsule is a catapult. When rain wets a ripe seed capsule it splits open, and tiny hooked stalks inside fling the seeds as far as eight to ten feet.
  • Despite the name, it is no close relative of the garden petunia. It comes from Mexico, western South America and the Caribbean islands.
  • University of Florida breeders made seedless varieties, the Mayan series, so gardeners can keep the flowers without the seeds. Those still spread by underground roots, so they need watching.
  • Florida's plant experts advise against planting the seed-making kind anywhere in the state, because it escapes into ditches, shorelines and wet natural areas.

Where to find it here

Serenity Garden

Look for it in the Serenity Garden, at the far end of the big field behind the Galleria.

Where it comes from

Home range

Its home is Mexico, western South America and the Caribbean islands. It came to Florida as a garden plant and now grows on its own in ditches, along shorelines and in other wet, disturbed ground.

Not Florida's wild petunia

Florida has its own native wild petunia, a foot or two tall and easy to mix up with this one. The Mexican one is the taller plant, with long, narrow leaves.

What it does

Worth recognizing

The useful story here is recognition: visitors can learn the common landscape plant before encountering it outside the park.

Persistent, not terrible

At the park it is not a terrible spreader, but it is persistent.

How to know it

Purple trumpets

Flowers are violet to purple and funnel-shaped, opening above narrow leaves.

Long narrow leaves

Leaves are opposite, lance-shaped and strongly veined.

Colony-forming habit

Established plants commonly form dense clumps as underground stems spread.

How it grows

Built to spread

It tolerates a wide range of light and moisture conditions. That flexibility, combined with vegetative spread and seed, helps explain its success in Florida.

Seeds that stick

Each seed rides on a small hooked stalk inside the capsule, and that stalk is the catapult arm. A seed that lands on damp ground grows a coat of sticky hairs, and the gel they make glues it to the soil as it dries.

Fruitless kinds, and the rest

The Mayan purple and white kinds, released in 2012, make no fruit at all, and the pink one that followed sets only a few, with seeds that do not sprout. Their roots still creep, so even a fruitless plant needs watching. Other garden-center kinds, including Chi Chi, Snow White and the dwarf Katie types, still set seed, and Florida's advice is against planting those too.
