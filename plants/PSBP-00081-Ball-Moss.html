Ball Moss

Tillandsia recurvata
On this page

At a glance

  • It is not a moss. It is a bromeliad, in the pineapple family and the same genus as Spanish moss.
  • It grows just as well on a power line as on a branch. The tree gives it a place to sit, not a meal.
  • Its leaves do the drinking: tiny gray scales soak up rain, dew and the minerals in dust. The roots only hold on.
  • Its first flowers of the season often never open. They pollinate themselves inside the closed bud, so a single seed can start a colony.

Where it comes from

From Arizona to Argentina

It is native here, and grows wild from Arizona to Florida, through Mexico, Central America and the Caribbean, and south to Argentina and Chile. In Mexico it grows on giant cactus and on electrical cable.

Where to find it here

Look up

It grows on branches all over the park. The best places to look are the bare inner limbs of older trees, where clumps collect over the years.

What it does here

A lodger, not a parasite

Its roots are anchors and nothing more; some plants barely have any. It makes its own food from sunlight like any green plant and takes nothing from the branch it sits on.

Blamed for bare limbs

Ball moss piles up on the bare inner limbs of old trees and gets the blame when those limbs die. They were failing anyway, shaded out as the crown grew above them, and old enough to have collected years of ball moss. The pale lichen that often crusts the same bark does no harm either.

Not always a free ride

In the dry scrub of Mexico, researchers have found it slows the growth of some kinds of host tree and leaves others alone. On a healthy tree here it is a lodger.

How to know it

A ball, not a curtain

Look for gray-green tufts a few inches across, sitting on a branch or tucked against the bark. Spanish moss, its cousin, hangs in long strands instead.

Leaves bent back

The narrow, wiry leaves curl back on themselves, which is what recurvata means.

Flower stalks

Thin stalks stand several inches above the tuft, each tipped with one or two pale violet flowers no bigger than a match head. The stalks are easier to spot than the flowers.

How it grows

Gray scales, borrowed nitrogen

The tiny scales that soak up water are also what make it look gray. Bacteria that can pull nitrogen from the air have been found living inside the plant, likely a help for something with no soil to draw on.

Breathing at night

It keeps its pores shut through the heat of the day and opens them at night to take in carbon dioxide, losing far less water. That is how it survives on a sun-baked wire.

A record of the air

Everything it takes in arrives through the air, so it soaks up whatever the air carries. Scientists in Mexico have used it to map metals and soot downwind of industry.

Seeds on the wind

Each seed capsule splits in three, and every seed carries a tuft of silky hairs about half an inch long. The seeds drift far, and the hairs snag on rough bark, which is how a new tuft turns up on a bare branch.
