At a glance

  • Black Sapote belongs to Diospyros, the same genus as persimmons and many ebony trees.
  • The fruit can remain green-skinned even when the flesh inside has ripened to dark brown.
  • Unripe fruit is firm and strongly astringent. Fully ripe fruit becomes very soft.
  • Kew accepts Diospyros nigra; the older name Diospyros digyna is a synonym still encountered in horticultural literature.

Cultural significance

A dark-wood relative

Diospyros gives the world two very different things from one genus: soft, custard-fleshed fruit like this one, and ebony, among the most prized dark timbers in history. The park's tree is grown for the fruit, not the wood.

Where it comes from

Mexico to Colombia

Kew gives the native range from Mexico through Central America to Colombia. It is now cultivated in other warm regions, including Florida.

Where to find it here

Right at the entrance

A big sign greets you right at the start of the Rare Fruit Garden, on the left — a big, full tree, hard to miss.

Look past it too

Continue past the Black Sapote toward the fence and you'll find an unsigned Pond Apple, one of the park's other Rare Fruit trees, closer to the fence line.

When

Fruit season in Florida varies by tree and cultivar. The best clue is not the calendar but fruit that has reached full size and then softened after harvest.

What it does here

A fruit that defeats the usual color test

Visitors often expect ripe tropical fruit to advertise itself with yellow, orange or red skin. Black Sapote can stay green outside while becoming dark and custard-soft inside.

How to know it

Glossy evergreen leaves

The tree carries large, dark, leathery leaves.

Green round fruit

Fruit is broad and rounded, with a persistent green calyx at the stem end.

The inside changes most

Fully ripe pulp becomes soft and chocolate-brown.

Check the genus connection

The large persistent calyx and berry-like fruit make more sense once you know this is a persimmon relative.

How it grows

A tropical fruit tree that handles South Florida soils

UF/IFAS describes good adaptation to well-drained Florida soils, including calcareous soils, but recommends avoiding prolonged flooding.

Size can be managed

Mature trees can become substantial, and UF/IFAS recommends selective pruning to limit height and open the canopy.

Wild status is currently secure

Kew carries a Least Concern assessment for the species.
