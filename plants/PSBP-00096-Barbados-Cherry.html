At a glance

  • One small red fruit carries many times the vitamin C of an orange. That is not a garden legend; it is why acerola turns up in supplement powders and juices all over the world.
  • You almost never see the fresh fruit for sale because it goes soft within a day or two of picking. It is a fruit for the people standing next to the tree.
  • In Puerto Rico it is simply cereza, cherry, as if there were no other.
  • It flowers and fruits in waves, often a few days after a good rain, several times a year.

Cultural significance

A fruit that stays home

Acerola has been eaten fresh across the Caribbean and Central America for as long as anyone has kept track, and grown commercially in Brazil and Puerto Rico for the vitamin C trade. Almost all of that harvest is juiced, pulped or dried within hours. The whole fresh cherry is a local pleasure, and a park with a tree is a place to have one.

Where it comes from

Mexico to Colombia, and the islands

Native from southern Mexico through Central America to the north of South America, and long grown through the Caribbean, which is where the Barbados in the name comes from. Older books call it Malpighia glabra. It is the same plant.

Where to find it here

In the Rare Fruit Garden, not far from the path.

What it does here

An edible-garden tree that feeds the birds first

The cherries ripen a few at a time and the mockingbirds and catbirds know it before we do. If you find a ripe one it is tart, juicy and gone in a bite. The little pink flowers keep bees busy between crops.

How to know it

Small pink flowers in clusters

Five crinkled pink petals on a stalk, in little groups along the branches, sometimes while fruit is still hanging.

Bright red, slightly lobed fruit

About the size of a small cherry, thin-skinned, often faintly three-lobed. Ripe when it gives to the touch.

A dense, small-leaved dome

Glossy oval leaves in opposite pairs on a rounded shrub or small tree six to fifteen feet tall. It reads as fine-textured next to a fruit tree with big leaves.

How it grows

Full sun for the best crops, though it manages in part shade. It is happy in sand and takes a dry spell once established. A frost hurts it; ours are mild enough. Pick ripe fruit the day you see it, because tomorrow it will be on the ground.
