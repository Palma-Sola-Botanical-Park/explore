At a glance

  • In the scrub it comes from, fire burns this oak to the ground every few years and it comes back from the roots as a knee-high thicket. Nobody burns it here, and it has quietly grown into a thirty-foot tree with a proper trunk.
  • The leaves are small, oval and leathery, with edges that roll under. Nothing about them says “oak” until you find an acorn.
  • Those acorns are a staple for the Florida Scrub-Jay, a bird that lives nowhere but Florida scrub.
  • One of Florida’s three scrub oaks, and the one with the smallest leaves.

Where it comes from

Florida’s white-sand scrub

Native to the southeastern coastal plain from South Carolina down through Florida, but really at home in one place: the ancient sand ridges of Florida scrub, where the ground is white sugar sand, the nutrients are close to nil, and fire comes through every few years. Most plants cannot live there. This one has arranged its whole life around it.

Where to find it here

In the Natives area, and you will be looking for a tree rather than a bush. It stands on its own with open ground around it, which is how you get a proper look at the trunk.

What it does here

The same oak, two shapes

This is the best lesson in the park about what fire does to a plant. In burned scrub, Myrtle Oak is a sprawling shrub a few feet high, its trunk more or less permanently underground. Take the fire away and it stops hedging its bets. Ours has put everything into one trunk and a spreading crown, and looks like a different species from its wild relatives. Same genes, different neighbourhood.

How to know it

Small leathery leaves, edges rolled under

About an inch or two long, oval, dark and glossy on top, paler beneath, and the margins curl under slightly. That rolled edge is the tell among the small-leaved oaks.

Little acorns, slow to finish

Small and rounded, and because this is a red oak they take two summers to ripen, so a branch can carry this year’s and last year’s at once.

Rough grey-brown bark

On our tree the trunk has furrowed into proper oak bark. In the scrub you would rarely see enough trunk to notice.

How it grows

Slow, tough, and wants nothing from you. Full sun, sand, no watering once it has settled, and wind does not bother it much. It is evergreen, though it drops a share of old leaves each spring as the new ones come. Hairstreak and duskywing butterflies use the leaves for their caterpillars; the flowers are wind-pollinated catkins with nothing in them for bees.
