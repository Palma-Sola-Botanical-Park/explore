Texas Olive

Cordia boissieri

At a glance

  • It flowers after rain. With enough water it can bloom all year, and each white flower lasts only three to five days.
  • Starting one from seed is hard. The seeds are locked in a tough pit, and even the best treatment tried in Texas sprouted fewer than half.
  • It handles cold better than most tropical-looking trees. Hardy to about 20 degrees Fahrenheit, it only drops its leaves in a hard freeze. Its orange-flowered cousin, the Geiger tree, can freeze to the ground.

Where it comes from

A borderland tree

Texas Olive is native to the southern tip of Texas and to northern Mexico, where it grows in dry, thorny scrub. In Mexico it is called anacahuita. The "olive" is only about the fruit, a yellow-green fruit about an inch long that looks like a small olive. The tree itself is no relation.

What it does here

Long-styled, short-styled

Trees come in two kinds. The style, the stalk that holds the pollen-receiving tip, is long on some and short on others, and each tree keeps its own. In one stand in northeastern Mexico the two kinds were split almost evenly.

A changing center

The throat of the white flower is usually yellow, but it can be yellow-orange or partly white, and each tree keeps its own pattern.

How to know it

Crumpled white trumpets

The flowers are about three inches across, with a crinkled, tissue-paper look, and they flare from a narrow base. They open in clusters, buds beside open blooms.

A coat on the leaves

The broad leaves are gray-green, a little rough on top and velvety underneath. Older trunks turn gray and deeply grooved.

Beside the true Olive

The true Olive here has narrow leaves; this tree's are far broader, and it belongs to the borage family. Only the fruit looks alike.

How it grows

A low, rounded tree

It usually stays a small tree with a rounded crown rather than a tall one, and its older branches arch outward as it widens. Full sun and well-drained soil suit it, even alkaline soil, and once established it gets through dry spells.
