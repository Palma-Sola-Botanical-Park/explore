Joannis Palm

Veitchia joannis

At a glance

  • A tall palm from Fiji's rainforests, where it stands above the canopy at 100 feet. Ours is about four feet.
  • Named in 1868 for John Gould Veitch, who collected it in Fiji for his family's London nursery. The genus is named for the family.
  • When it fruits, look for the beak: each scarlet fruit, up to two inches long, ends in a point.
  • It stands at the fork in the path by the Baobab, where you can go either way round the tree.

Cultural significance

Niusawa

In Fiji the palm is niusawa, and it is eaten as well as admired: the young seeds are a snack there, and the tender leaf bud is eaten raw.

Where it comes from

Fiji

Joannis Palm is native to Fiji, where it grows in rainforest from near sea level to about 1,300 feet, on well-drained slopes and ridges, and comes up again in regrowing forest. It has also gone wild in Tonga.

Veitch's palm

John Gould Veitch collected the palm in Fiji on his Pacific expedition of 1864 to 1866, plant-hunting for the Veitch nurseries of London and Exeter. The German botanist Hermann Wendland described both the genus and the species in 1868, in Berthold Seemann's flora of Fiji, so this palm is the one the genus Veitchia was built around. The name joannis is Latin for John's. Veitch died at 31, and the palm carries his name wherever it grows.

Where to find it here

At the fork by the Baobab

Coming from the south, the shell path forks to pass either side of the Baobab, the tree between the two Royal Poincianas of the Serenity Garden. The Joannis Palm stands at that fork, just west of the Baobab. It is young, about four feet tall, with rocks set at its base.

What it does here

Two Veitchias

Palma Sola grows two palms of this Pacific-island genus: this one from Fiji and Montgomery Palm from Vanuatu, on the walkway along the south side of Big Pond. Both are young, so for now they look much alike. As they grow the differences will show: this one's older fronds droop below level, and its fruit end in a beak.

How to know it

Now: the fronds

One stem, with long arching fronds. The leaflets are dark green on both faces, set in one flat plane, and their tips are notched and uneven rather than drawn to a point.

Later: the crownshaft

As the palm grows, the leaf bases will form a crownshaft, a smooth whitish-green sleeve flecked with grey and olive, above a grey trunk ringed with old leaf scars.

Fruit with a beak

The fruit ripen scarlet, an inch and a half to two inches long, and each ends in a pointed beak, which is the mark botanists use to tell this species from its relatives.

Drooping old fronds

On a mature tree the older fronds sag well below the horizontal, so the crown looks layered and a little weeping. Montgomery Palm holds its fronds higher.

How it grows

Built tall

At home this is a canopy tree, one trunk lifting a compact crown a hundred feet up. In Florida it stays shorter but grows the same way, one growing point at the top of one stem, and it never suckers.

Seed and rings

Male and female flowers share a spray below the crownshaft, so a lone tree sets fruit. The seeds are large and come up readily. Old fronds fall clean away and leave the rings on the trunk.
