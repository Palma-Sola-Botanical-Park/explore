Cocoplum

At a glance

  • A Florida native of the coast, from Pasco County south, that grows right up to the dunes and shrugs off salt spray.
  • The plum-sized fruit is edible, and ripens white, pink or deep purple depending on the plant. Birds usually get there first.
  • It comes in two forms: a low coastal mound of six feet or less, and an inland shrub or small tree of fifteen to twenty-five feet.
  • Gopher tortoises swallow the fruit whole and pass the big seed intact; a Florida study found cocoplum thickets following the tortoises' trails.

Cultural significance

Fruit, jelly and the seed inside

Ripe cocoplum is eaten fresh and made into jam and jelly across the Caribbean and in Florida. The oily kernel inside the stone can be roasted and eaten too, and in the West Indies roasted kernels were once strung on a splinter of palm frond and burned as a candle. The name Fat Pork, used in the islands, is for the pale soft pulp around the stone.

Where it comes from

A coastal native

Cocoplum grows wild along both Florida coasts from about Pasco and Brevard counties south through the Keys, and on through the Bahamas, the West Indies, Mexico, Central America and northern South America. Kew also counts the cocoplum of West and Central Africa as the same species. In Florida it lives on dunes and coastal strand, in scrub and at the edges of hammocks and wetlands.

Golden acorn

It is one of only two Florida natives in its family; the other is the gopher apple, a knee-high shrub of the sand ridges. Chrysobalanus is Greek for golden acorn, for the stone inside the fruit.

Where to find it here

Welcome Island

On the Welcome Island, the island the driveway circles in front of the Galleria: a dense green mound of round leaves.

What it does here

A tortoise can move a large seed

At a suburban preserve in Jupiter, Florida, four cocoplums planted as ornamentals spread across the preserve in fourteen years, and a study published in 2020 showed how: gopher tortoises ate the fruit, passed the large seed unharmed, and the seeds they passed sprouted sooner. Young cocoplums came up thickest along the tortoises' trails. The seed is too big for most songbirds, so a shrub like this depends on the larger animals that can swallow it. Here the fruit goes to whatever finds it.

A native in the front garden

Cocoplum is the hedge of the Florida coast, planted along beach roads and canals from here to the Keys. Ours is a native of this coast growing in the front garden, in the company of plants from every warm part of the world.

How to know it

Round leaves in a V

Leathery leaves, one to three inches long and nearly round, alternate along the stem and tilt upward, so a branch seen end-on makes a shallow V. The tip is rounded or shallowly notched with a tiny point, and the edge rolls slightly under.

Dotted twigs

Older twigs are grey-brown with pale raised dots, the lenticels, a useful mark when there is no flower or fruit.

Flowers and plums

Small white flowers sit in clusters at the leaf bases in most months, heaviest in winter and spring. The fruit, an inch or two long, may be white, pink or purple, with a ridged stone inside.

How it grows

Two forms

The coastal form stays low, roots where its branches touch the ground and spreads into a thicket. The inland form grows fast and upright to a small tree. Nurseries sell named forms of each, raised from cuttings, since seedlings do not come true.

Built for the coast, not for cold

Sun or light shade, sand or heavy soil, salt wind, a few days of standing water: an established plant takes them all. Cold is its limit. Bradenton is at the northern edge of where it grows reliably, and a hard freeze can strip the leaves.
