At a glance

  • From the Mascarene Islands, Mauritius, Réunion and Rodrigues, in the Indian Ocean east of Madagascar, and from nowhere else. It is the only species in its genus.
  • Ours is the golden form, var. aureum, from Rodrigues. Its crownshaft is tan to bronze rather than the white the name promises.
  • Named for standing up to cyclones: old fronds drop cleanly in a storm, and new ones come up as stiff spears.
  • In late 2024 a windstorm brought down the last wild tree of the Round Island form. The Mauritian Wildlife Foundation reported in February 2025 that it now survives only in cultivation.

Cultural significance

Palmiste blanc

In the Mascarenes this is the palmiste blanc, the white palm, and its heart, the growing bud at the top of the trunk, was a food. Cutting the heart kills the palm, and that harvest, with the clearing of the islands' forests, left few wild palms standing. The heart is still eaten there, from palms grown for the purpose.

Where to find it here

South side of Cypress Pond

Walk the row of palms along the shell path on the south side of Cypress Pond. Coming from the Big Pond walkway, it is the third palm: first the Talipot, then the Lifou Palm, then this one, on the pond side of the path.

Where it comes from

The Mascarenes

Hurricane Palm grows wild in the Mascarene Islands, a small volcanic group east of Madagascar: Mauritius, Réunion and Rodrigues, plus Round Island, an islet off Mauritius. Each island has its own variety, and all are rare in the wild after centuries of clearing and cutting for palm heart.

Net-seed, white

Dictyosperma is Greek for net-seed, from the netted surface of the seed, and album is Latin for white, for the waxy crownshaft of the Mauritius form. Ours is the Rodrigues variety, aureum, golden, whose crownshaft and new leaves run to tan and bronze.

What it does

Common here, rare at home

Nurseries sell this palm all over South Florida, and in its home islands it is nearly gone from the wild: the Round Island form since 2024, the others long reduced to scattered trees. This one grows in the row of unusual palms planted by a longtime volunteer. It is also the palm to watch after a storm, to see what a clean-shedding crown looks like the morning after.

How to know it

The crownshaft

Ours has a tan to bronze crownshaft, the smooth sleeve of leaf bases above the trunk. The Mauritius form is white there, so do not go by colour alone.

The spear

A new frond stands straight up from the crown as a stiff green spear before it opens. There is usually one showing.

A clean ringed trunk

One grey trunk, ringed with old leaf scars and a little swollen at the base. No dead fronds hang from it.

Fruit in a ring

Sprays of small flowers come out in a ring below the crownshaft and are followed by fruit about half to three-quarters of an inch long, purple to black when ripe.

How it grows

One growing point

One trunk with one growing point at the top. It wants warmth, sun and steady water; Bradenton is near the cool edge of where it grows outdoors, and a hard freeze marks it.

Why it stands up to storms

The crown holds 20 to 30 fronds. Old ones let go cleanly at the crownshaft, and in a high wind they go first, which leaves the storm less to pull on.

Seed

Male and female flowers share each spray, the males opening first, so a lone palm sets fruit. Birds take the fruit and the seed comes up readily.

Take care

Fruit and heart

The purple-black fruit are not for eating. The heart is a food in the Mascarenes, and cutting it kills the palm, so it stays in the tree.
