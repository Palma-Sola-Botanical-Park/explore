At a glance

  • Its fan leaves are coated in wax. The gray-blue cast is the same carnauba wax that ends up in polishes and candy glazes.
  • The name carnaúba is often traced to a native Brazilian word for a tree that scratches, after the hooked spines on its leaf stalks.
  • Ours is young, still a low fan of leaves with no trunk.

Cultural significance

From leaf to wax

In northeastern Brazil, harvesters cut the leaves and lay them out in the sun to dry. Beaten, the dried leaves shed their wax as a powder, which is melted and refined into hard wax for polishes, coatings and cosmetics.

Where it comes from

It comes from northeastern Brazil, where it grows in dense stands in the river valleys and floodplains of a region with a long, hot dry season.

Where to find it here

Come down the stairs from the Galleria and head down the left side of the Galleria Field toward the Big Pond. It stands near the water with the Kukui Tree and the Jacaranda.

What it does here

The wax before the polish

Here you can see the plant behind a familiar product: the sheen on its fans is the wax, still on the leaf.

Young for now

Ours is still a low clump of fans with no trunk. With age it lifts its crown on a single gray trunk.

How to know it

Gray-blue fans

Round, stiff fan leaves, silvery gray-green, on long stalks.

Yellow stalks

The leaf stalks are yellowish, with hooked spines along their edges.

How it grows

Wax against drought

The wax slows water loss from the leaves, which helps the palm stay green through a long dry season.

Mature palms send long, branched flower stalks out from among the leaves, followed by small round fruits that ripen dark.

Take care

Spiny stalks

Hooked spines line the leaf stalks. Keep your hands clear of them.
