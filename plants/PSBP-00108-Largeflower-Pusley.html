Cultural significance

Not Mexican, not a clover

Mexican clover is the usual name for the whole group of pusleys in the genus Richardia, and it fits this one badly. It comes from South America, and it belongs to the coffee family, nowhere near the clovers. Several relatives share the names pusley, Mexican clover and Florida snow, so a name alone can mean quite different plants. This is the one with the large flowers.

Named for a doctor

Richardia honors Richard Richardson, an English physician and botanist who lived from 1663 to 1741. Pusley is an old shortening of purslane, a succulent garden weed that is no relation. And grandiflora, large-flowered, only means large for a pusley.

A lawn with two audiences

To someone who wants an even lawn, it marks thin turf: it moves into bare patches and thin sod, and rarely beats a well-established grass. To a bee or butterfly it is a place to feed in the cooler months. It keeps flowering even when it is heavily mowed.

Where it comes from

South America

Its home is Brazil, Bolivia, Paraguay, Uruguay and northeast Argentina. In Florida it grows in lawns, along roadsides and in open sandy ground, from Miami-Dade County to the western Panhandle.

How it got here isn't recorded

Nobody has shown how it arrived. The usual guess is that it was planted as an ornamental or ground cover, and that is only a guess. A 1969 specimen from Miami is the earliest date we have, so it has been here more than half a century.

What it does here

Down among the leaves

We list what turns up on its own as well as what we planted, and pusley is one of those. It threads its stems between larger plants and lifts its flowers into the gaps, a layer below the shrubs that draw your eye.

Coffee-family stars

Our Pentas and Jungle Flame are relatives, despite their different sizes. Count the points on the flowers: Pentas has five, Jungle Flame usually four, Largeflower Pusley six. All three gather small tubular flowers into clusters.

A late-fall feed

A wide variety of bees and butterflies work the flowers. It peaks in late fall and early winter, a stretch when few other plants are in flower.

How to know it

Follow the low outline

Stems run outward close to the ground, then lift their flowering tips. Among taller plants those tips can look like separate little plants until you follow the stems back.

Count the lobes

Each flower usually has six lobes. Flowers are white, pink, lavender or lilac and about half an inch across or a little more, much larger than the white flowers of the other pusleys.

Paired and hairy

Leaves grow in opposite pairs, and stiff hairs cover the stems and leaves. Leaf shape varies and isn't a reliable way to tell the pusleys apart, so go by the flowers.

How it grows

A joint becomes an anchor

Stems can run 30 inches along the ground, and where a joint touches soil it can root. Its two small-flowered cousins rarely do. Broken stem pieces can root too, so a patch thickens into a mat that is harder to get rid of than theirs.

Fruit in three parts

After flowering, the small dry fruit splits into three pieces. Their surfaces are warty, which helps tell this plant from Brazil pusley, whose pieces are hairy. Seeds start new patches while rooting stems enlarge old ones.

All year, with a peak

Flowers can be found all year, with the heaviest display in late fall. A hard freeze can kill it back to bare ground.

Worth watching

Florida's invasive-plant experts keep an eye on it. It has become more common, but nobody has shown it changing natural areas, and the state university advises keeping it from spreading into them.
