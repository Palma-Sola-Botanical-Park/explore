Marsh Fleabane

Pluchea odorata
On this page

At a glance

  • Look for small fuzzy pink puffs, each under half an inch across. Each is a cluster of tiny flowers, with no ring of petals like a daisy's.
  • The leaves carry camphor-scented oil, and the scent lingers in the dried leaves of winter. The species name, odorata, means fragrant.
  • Its seeds sprout only in the light, so it grows where the ground is bare and open, like a muddy bank.
  • Summer and fall flowers offer nectar to visiting butterflies and bees.

Where to find it here

Look on the north side of the Front Pond. From the parking lot, keep the pond on your left and follow the fence for about thirty yards. You are welcome to walk around the pond even where there is no defined path.

Where it comes from

Marsh Fleabane is native to Florida and grows in both fresh and salt marshes, swamps and coastal hammocks. Damp ground is the common thread, rather than one particular kind of water.

A French abbe

The genus name, Pluchea, honors Noel-Antoine Pluche, an eighteenth-century French priest whose popular books on natural history were among the most widely read of his day.

What it does

On the pond bank

Marsh Fleabane likes moist ground, which suits a pond bank, and its loose, branching growth fits an unclipped edge better than a tidy border.

A late-season flower

When it blooms, pause beside the small flower heads. Bees and butterflies come for the nectar, among them a small sweat bee, Halictus ligatus, which has been recorded on the flowers.

How to know it

Look at the branch tips

Look for an upright, branching plant with clusters gathered at the ends of its stems. The pink heads are small enough that the cluster makes more of an impression than any individual flower.

The leaves alternate along the stem. Their surfaces are rough, their edges toothed, and the foliage is aromatic. As flowering ends, pale seed fluff replaces the pink.

How it grows

This is a short-lived perennial that can renew itself from seed. Fine hairs on the seeds help carry them away from the parent. It fits loose pond-edge planting better than a tightly clipped border, and needs moisture rather than a prolonged dry spell.

Light to sprout

Its seeds need light to sprout, so seedlings tend to appear where damp soil lies open to the light.
