At a glance

  • A palm from Vanuatu, in the southwest Pacific, and nowhere else. The IUCN Red List rates it Endangered (assessed 1998).
  • The name honours Colonel Robert H. Montgomery, who founded Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden in 1938. Botanists now file his palm, Veitchia montgomeryana, under Veitchia arecina.
  • Ours is young, about five feet, on the pond side of the Big Pond walkway. Give it years: at home this palm reaches 100 feet.
  • One of two Veitchia palms in the park. The other, Joannis Palm from Fiji, grows by the Baobab.

Where it comes from

Vanuatu

Veitchia arecina grows wild only in Vanuatu, a chain of volcanic islands west of Fiji. It is a palm of lowland and foothill rainforest, up to about 1,100 feet, on volcanic soil where rain is reliable. The IUCN Red List assessed it as Endangered in 1998, with forest clearing the threat.

The Montgomery in the name

Colonel Robert H. Montgomery (1872–1953) was an accountant and lawyer whose real work was collecting palms and cycads in South Florida. His collection became Fairchild Tropical Botanic Garden, which he founded in 1938 and named for his friend, the plant explorer David Fairchild. The palm named for him, Veitchia montgomeryana, is still on nursery labels; botanists now treat it as the same species as V. arecina.

Where to find it here

Big Pond, south walkway

Take the walkway along the south side of Big Pond. Counting from the bridge into the Rare Fruit Garden at the southeast corner, the Montgomery Palm is the tenth palm, between the Seashore Palm and the Christmas Palm, on the pond side where riprap lines the bank.

What it does here

Two Veitchias

Palma Sola grows two of this Pacific-island genus of tall crownshaft palms: this one from Vanuatu and Joannis Palm from Fiji, by the Baobab in the Serenity Garden. The Christmas Palm a few steps along the walkway was a Veitchia too, until botanists moved it into its own genus, Adonidia. Three palms from three sets of islands, all built the same way: one ringed trunk, a smooth crownshaft, red fruit.

How to know it

Start with the leaves

Ours has no trunk yet, so start with the fronds. The leaflets sit evenly spaced in one flat plane, and each tapers almost to a point but stops just short, neither squared off nor needle-sharp.

Crownshaft to come

The stem is green and already ringed where fronds have dropped. As the palm grows, the leaf bases will form a smooth pale green crownshaft above a grey ringed trunk.

Red fruit, later

Flower sprays come out below the crownshaft, and the fruit that follow are oval, one to two inches long, and bright red. Nothing has flowered on ours yet.

Compare with Joannis Palm

Joannis Palm by the Baobab is the same genus and about the same age. As they mature, the differences will show: Joannis's older fronds droop below level, and each of its fruit ends in a pointed beak.

How it grows

A rainforest palm

At home it stands in humid rainforest with steady rain. It does poorly in salt wind, so a sheltered spot by fresh water suits it; here the pond is at its feet.

One growing point

One trunk, one crown, one growing point; it never suckers. Male and female flowers grow on the same spray, so a lone palm sets fruit, and birds carry the seed.
