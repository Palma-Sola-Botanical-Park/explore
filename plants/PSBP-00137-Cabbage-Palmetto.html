At a glance

  • This is Florida's state tree, a native palm found throughout the state.
  • The tender growing point at the top is the original swamp cabbage, or heart of palm. Remove it and the whole palm dies.
  • The crisscrossed pieces covering many trunks are old leaf bases, called boots. They make places where other plants and animals can live.
  • One of ours in the Shade Garden has a Dragon Fruit climbing high into it. Look up or you will miss both plants at once.

Cultural significance

Swamp cabbage

The common name comes from the palm's edible central bud, traditionally cooked as swamp cabbage or eaten as heart of palm. But a Cabbage Palmetto has only one growing point. Cutting out the heart kills the entire palm.

A palm people used

Long before it became Florida's state tree, Cabbage Palmetto supplied people here with food, thatch, fiber and building material. Florida's Indigenous peoples used the palm extensively, and later Floridians kept up many of the same practical uses of the leaves, fibers, trunk and heart.

Where it comes from

A Florida native

Cabbage Palmetto grows naturally throughout Florida and north along the southeastern coastal plain. In Florida it occupies an unusually broad range of places, from flatwoods and hammocks to wetlands and coastal forests. It tolerates conditions that would be difficult for many trees, including salt spray and periodic flooding.

Where to find it here

Easy to pass by

Cabbage Palmetto is Florida's state tree, and here it gets less attention than almost any other palm. The collection runs to about seventy palm species, some of them rare, and the common one is the one nobody stops for. Two grow along 17th Avenue between the sidewalk and the street, another stands outside the nursery gate, and a couple grow in the Shade Garden.

Look up in the Shade Garden

One of the Shade Garden palms, near the office, supports the Dragon Fruit that climbs overhead. Both are easy to miss if you only look at what is growing around you at eye level.

What it does here

A tree that becomes a garden

When old leaf bases stay attached to the trunk, they form pockets where fallen material collects and other living things can take hold. Ferns and other plants commonly grow out of Cabbage Palmetto trunks.

A support for another feature plant

In the Shade Garden, one Cabbage Palmetto is doing that kind of structural work on a larger scale. A Dragon Fruit uses it as the support for its climbing stems.

How to know it

Follow the fold

The large fan-shaped leaves are not flat fans. A strong midrib runs partway into each blade and bends it downward, which gives the crown its curved look. Botanists call this costapalmate.

Boots or bare trunk

Some trunks keep the crisscrossed bases of old leaves, the boots. Others eventually shed them and become fairly smooth and gray. Both are the same species.

Look beyond the leaves

In early summer, mature palms send long branching flower stalks out beyond the crown, carrying thousands of tiny creamy-white flowers. Small black fruits follow in the fall.

How it grows

It can wait years for a trunk

Young Cabbage Palmettos can spend years in a trunkless stage. Only later does the familiar trunk begin to rise. A trunkless Cabbage Palmetto will not flower or fruit either, which helps separate young ones from some other native palms in the same genus that flower without a trunk.

One growing point

A palm does not grow like an oak or any other branching tree. Cabbage Palmetto depends on a single growing bud at the top of its trunk. Damage that kills that bud kills the palm, which is why harvesting swamp cabbage is fatal to the plant.

Size

Slow, and eventually tall: a mature palm stands 40 to 50 feet, often less, with a crown 10 to 15 feet across.

Take care

Taking the heart kills the palm

The tender central bud is edible and traditionally prepared as swamp cabbage or heart of palm. It is also the palm's single growing point. Removing it kills the entire plant.
