At a glance

  • Each bump on the lumpy green fruit marks one flower. A whole head of small white flowers fuses into a single fruit, and the flowers open a few at a time over many weeks.
  • Ripe fruit smells of cheese, or worse. The smell comes from fatty acids that are toxic to ordinary fruit flies, yet one fruit-fly species is immune to them and lives on noni.
  • Noni juice is sold worldwide as a health drink, but studies in people have not shown that it helps any health condition.

Cultural significance

A mulberry from India

Linnaeus named it in 1753 from an illustration in a book of plants from India's Malabar coast. Morinda is usually explained as Latin for “Indian mulberry,” and citrifolia means “with citrus-like leaves.”

Color from a root

In Hawaii the roots dyed kapa, the cloth beaten from bark. The dye ran from yellow to red and orange, depending on the part of the plant and how it was prepared.

A chant about gathering

A Hawaiian story recorded in 1861 has the pig god Kamapuaʻa taunt the volcano goddess Pele with a chant of women gathering, scratching, pounding and marking with noni. The steps sound like making dye, and scholars read the chant differently: as dye-making, as a jab at Pele's red eyes, or as eating noni in a famine.

A canoe plant

Noni is on the standard list of the plants Polynesian settlers carried in their canoes, valued for dye and traditional medicine. Whether it was ever much of a food, even in a famine, is doubtful: one researcher who asked experts across the Pacific thought it unlikely.

Where it comes from

A map with blurred edges

Noni comes from tropical Asia and the western Pacific, including northern Australia, but people moved it so early that the original limits are hard to draw. One DNA study suggests it was already in the western Pacific before the Polynesians, who then carried it east. In Hawaii it is believed to have come by canoe.

Seeds that float

Each seed has an air pocket and a water-resistant coat, so seeds float on seawater for months. That is how noni may have reached some islands on its own. In Hawaii it is one of the first plants to take hold on young lava, and it also grows beside brackish tide pools.

What it does here

A coffee cousin

Noni is in the coffee family, along with Firebush, Pentas and Jungle Flame. The glossy leaves in facing pairs are the family look, on a much larger scale.

Fruit in winter, flowers in summer

Green fruit hung among the leaves here on Christmas Day 2025, and flowers were opening on June 24, 2026.

How to know it

Quilted leaves

The leaves are big and glossy, up to a foot and a half long, in pairs facing each other across the stem. The side veins curve out from the midrib and press into the surface, which gives it a quilted look.

Square shoots

Young shoots are four-sided rather than round.

A flower head in slow motion

White flowers open a few at a time from a green head, each a narrow tube that ends in about five spreading lobes. Each flower has both pollen and ovary.

Soft and pale

As the fruit ripens it changes from hard and green to soft, translucent and pale yellow-white, and that is when the smell arrives.

How it grows

In no hurry to sprout

Untreated seed sprouts only sporadically, over six to twelve months. A fruit can carry travelers that float for months and then wait months more.

A tropical tree

In the tropics it flowers and fruits nearly all year. It is a plant of warm places that tolerates about 40°F at the least, so a cold snap here is a hazard.

Take care

Juice and supplements

Noni products are not a harmless health food. The fruit is rich in potassium, which matters for people with kidney disease, and noni can interact with medicines. Check with a doctor before using it as a medicine.
