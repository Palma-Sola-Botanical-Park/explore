Tropical Sage

Salvia coccinea

At a glance

  • A Florida native wildflower with slender red tubes of flowers, shaped for hummingbirds.
  • When a hummingbird reaches in for nectar, the flower's stamens dust pollen onto its head, and the bird carries it to the next flower.
  • It seeds itself freely, so it comes back on its own and fills in gaps.
  • It is a true sage, in the same genus as the kitchen herb. Brush a leaf and you get a faint herbal smell.

Cultural significance

Sage, for health

Salvia comes from the Latin for being well; the genus gave us garden sage and a long list of old remedies. The species name, coccinea, means scarlet-dyed. Scarlet Sage and Blood Sage are its other common names.

Where it comes from

It grows wild from the Southeast through Mexico and the Caribbean to northern South America, in open woods, clearings and disturbed ground. In Florida it turns up on shell mounds and limestone bluffs as readily as on roadsides.

A century of argument

Whether it was in Florida before Europeans arrived was argued for more than a century. William Bartram listed it as a wild plant in 1792. A botanist in the 1890s decided it had escaped from gardens. The standard Florida flora of 1929 called it native again, and the Florida references we follow all treat it as native, so we do too.

Where to find it here

In the Butterfly Garden near the pavilion, close to the Bottle Palm and the Firewheel.

What it does here

Nectar, not leaves

Its flowers feed hummingbirds, butterflies and bees. Feeding adults is a different job from feeding caterpillars: no Florida butterfly is known to raise its caterpillars on this plant.

Who comes

Hummingbirds pass through here in spring and fall, and the long red tubes are made to their measure. Butterflies and bumblebees work the flowers as well, and small songbirds have been seen taking the nectar.

How to know it

Soft and upright

Soft, upright stems with heart-shaped, softly hairy, toothed leaves in pairs.

Red, mostly

The flowers are usually scarlet, though pink and white forms turn up. In our bed, coral and pink-and-white plants grow among the red.

Dark cups

When a flower drops, its dark purple cup stays on the stem, so a spike is dotted with them from bottom to top long after the color has gone.

How it grows

Single plants don't live long, but they seed themselves, so the patch carries on. A freeze can cut it to the ground; the roots often survive, and seed already in the soil comes up when the weather warms. It can flower in any month here.

From the bottom up

Each spike opens from the bottom up, so one stem can carry spent flowers below, open ones in the middle and buds at the tip. See if you can find all three.
