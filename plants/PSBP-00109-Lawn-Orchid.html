Lawn Orchid

Zeuxine strateumatica
On this page
At a glanceCultural significanceWhere it comes fromWhat it does hereHow to know itHow it grows

At a glance

  • This is a real orchid, four to eight inches tall, with a crowded spike of white flowers that each have a yellow lip. It grows in the ground, not in a tree.
  • It showed up in Florida in the 1930s. The Harvard botanist who traced it guessed it came in with centipede grass from China, and he called that a conjecture. It has never been proved.
  • It flowers from October to February. By spring the spikes have usually withered.
  • Its flowers barely open and seem to pollinate themselves. In one population in India, seeds formed with no fertilization at all.

Cultural significance

A company of soldiers

The species name, strateumatica, comes from a Greek word for an army or a company of soldiers, and a patch of upright spikes does look drawn up in ranks. That is also the idea behind Soldier's Orchid. Linnaeus described it in 1753 from a specimen collected in Ceylon, now Sri Lanka.

A tonic in South Asia

In parts of India, Nepal and Bangladesh its small underground tubers have been used in traditional tonics.

Where it comes from

Asia, and ricefields

It is native from Iran and Central Asia through tropical and subtropical Asia to Japan and New Guinea. It grows in the ground in grass, damp open places and roadsides, and in parts of India, Malaysia and Indonesia it turns up as a weed in rice fields.

A detective story

The earliest known Florida plants were photographed west of Fellsmere in January 1936. The first report in print came from a nursery in Ormond a year later. By 1938 the botanist Oakes Ames had records from six or more counties, and he asked the obvious question: was this an overlooked native? He decided it wasn't. Botanists had searched those places for years, and colonies had turned up all at once, in widely separated places, near roads and houses.

The centipede grass guess

Centipede grass came from China to the United States in 1917, from regions where this orchid grows wild. Ames suspected the orchid's tiny seeds or young plants had hitched a ride on the grass, and wrote that until someone found out for sure, conjecture was the only recourse. A newer review still says it is thought to have arrived with grass seed. Nobody has proved it.

Now

It has spread through most of Florida and into Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi, Louisiana and Texas. Manatee County is among the Florida counties where it has been recorded.

What it does here

An orchid nobody planted

We have recorded it growing on its own on our grounds. Ours was photographed in flower in January 2023, with several spikes crowded together. Ames once counted fifteen flower stalks in under a square foot beside a ditch. It is an orchid you meet by crouching down.

Two kinds of orchid

We also grow an Orchid Tree, but that tree belongs to the bean family and gets its common name from the shape of its flowers. This small plant is an orchid in the botanical sense. The tree holds its broad leaves overhead. This orchid keeps its narrow leaves down among the grass.

Ground orchids that like trouble

Ames noticed that Florida's native ground orchids are fussy and shun disturbed ground, while this one acts as if it were made for it. It turns up in lawns, roadsides, nurseries and farm fields.

How to know it

Change your viewing height

A flowering plant stands four to eight inches tall. From above it is a pale spike in the grass. Crouch down and the spike resolves into many separate flowers crowded around a central stem, up to about fifty on a good one.

A yellow lip

Each flower is white with a bold yellow lip, and the white parts partly shelter it. A flower is smaller than a pea.

Leaves that wrap the shoot

Five to twelve leaves wrap the lower stem in a spiral. They are dark green in shade and reddish-bronze in full sun. Surrounding grass can hide them while the pale flowers show above.

Come in the cool season

In Florida the flower spikes appear from October to February. Outside those months there is little to see.

How it grows

Seeds with or without a partner

In most orchids a small structure called the rostellum keeps a flower from pollinating itself. Here it is so reduced that the pollen can reach the same flower's stigma, and the flowers barely open. A researcher once noted small flies at the flowers. In a population near Chandigarh, India, embryos formed from ovule tissue with no fertilization at all, a trick called apomixis, and plants in Hong Kong are reported to do the same. Nobody has tested whether Florida plants do. Self-pollination and apomixis are different things, and the reports don't agree on which one Florida plants rely on.

A partner underground

Orchid seeds are dust-sized and need a fungus to germinate. Ames cultured a fungus from the roots of Florida plants and failed to get seed to germinate with it. In 1942 a Harvard botanist, working with Florida material, grew seedlings on a fungus called Rhizoctonia mucoroides, which took six to eight months. His conclusion was that the match between fungus and orchid is a specific one.

A short season above ground

Ames watched a stand flower in January, set abundant seed within a few weeks and wither away by mid-March. Most plants die after fruiting, though a few send up a new shoot from the old roots, and one source describes a short rhizome. Sources differ on whether it is truly an annual.
