Red Silk Cotton Tree

At a glance

  • In late winter, Red Silk Cotton drops its leaves and covers its bare branches with large red flowers. With the foliage gone, almost nothing hides the display.
  • Later, woody pods split open and release masses of white silky fiber around the seeds, the "silk cotton" that gives the tree its name.
  • Two mature trees grow together beside our Cypress Pond, near a Kapok and a Baobab. All are related, and the family resemblance is much easier to see when they're growing almost side by side.
  • If you've confused Red Silk Cotton with Silk Floss, you're in good company. Both have spiny trunks, hand-shaped leaves and silky seed fiber, but they are different trees from different parts of the world.

Cultural significance

The other kapok

The silky fiber around the seeds has long been gathered in Asia for stuffing mattresses and pillows and for making rope, the same job the true Kapok's fiber does. That shared use is why this tree is often called Red Kapok, even though the real Kapok is the tree standing beside it.

Where it comes from

From Asia to Australia

Red Silk Cotton is native across a remarkably broad area of tropical and subtropical Asia, from the Indian subcontinent through Southeast Asia and southern China to New Guinea and northern Australia. It is now cultivated well beyond that range.

Where to find it here

Start at Cypress Pond

Two mature Red Silk Cotton Trees stand beside each other along the edge of Cypress Pond. Nearby are a Kapok and, toward Serenity Garden, our Baobab. Together they make this one of the best places in the park to compare several related trees.

There's a younger one too

We lost a Red Silk Cotton Tree of about thirty feet near the bridge at Big Pond to hurricane damage. A replacement now grows there, about six feet tall, and it will take years to regain the presence of the tree that was lost.

When

Flowering runs from about mid-February into March. The trees shed their leaves first, leaving the large red blooms exposed along otherwise bare branches. Later in spring the seed capsules open and release their white silk.

See a giant one in Sarasota

For a look at what this species can become, Marie Selby Botanical Gardens in downtown Sarasota has three, the largest on the east side of the Great Lawn, and there is a large one on the grounds of The Ringling. All flower at about the same time as ours.

What it does here

Compare the relatives

The two Red Silk Cotton Trees, the nearby Kapok and the Baobab give us a living family comparison. Look for the large trunks, the branching patterns and the seasonally bare crowns. The Red Silk Cotton and Kapok make an especially good pair: both can have spiny trunks and both produce seed pods filled with silky fiber, but their flowers immediately separate them.

Then comes the cleanup

When the Red Silk Cotton Trees flower heavily, the ground below fills with fallen blooms, each substantial enough that keeping the area clear becomes a real job. Later comes a very different kind of litter: white silk released from the seed pods drifts through the area like snow.

How to know it

Wait for the red

Large red flowers on mostly bare branches are the clearest seasonal clue. Each has five thick petals three to four inches long around a brush of stamens.

Watch for the spines

Younger trunks are armed with stout conical spines. The trunk becomes smoother as the tree ages.

Look for the hand-shaped leaf

When the leaves return, five to seven leaflets radiate from one point like fingers from a hand.

Don't confuse it with Silk Floss

Our Silk Floss Tree by the office shares the spiny trunk and hand-shaped leaves, which makes the confusion understandable. Its flowers, however, are pink and white and come in fall, usually around October, rather than the Red Silk Cotton's red late-winter display.

How it grows

Flowers first, leaves later

Red Silk Cotton is deciduous. It sheds its leaves before flowering, putting the large red blooms onto exposed branches. After flowering, foliage returns and the fertilized flowers develop into woody capsules containing seeds surrounded by silky fiber.

The "cotton" is part of the seed system

When a mature capsule opens, the silky material around the seeds expands out of it. On our trees, enough is released that white fiber can scatter across the surrounding ground.

Take care

Don't sample it here

Parts of Red Silk Cotton have traditional food uses in its native range, but preparation varies. Treat our trees as botanical specimens rather than food plants.
