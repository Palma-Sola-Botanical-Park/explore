At a glance

  • Each flower head looks like the head of a match: a ring of tiny white flowers around a purple knob of buds. The ring climbs as the buds open, so the head stretches from a ball into a little cylinder.
  • Three butterflies lay their eggs on it: the Phaon Crescent, the White Peacock and the Common Buckeye. In Florida the Phaon Crescent's caterpillars eat frogfruit and little else.
  • It is a Florida native that grows flat, so a mower passes over it. A whole patch can be one plant, rooting wherever a stem touches the ground.
  • Nobody knows where the name comes from. Older books spell it fogfruit, and "turkey tangle" has no story at all.

Where it comes from

A traveler that started here

Frogfruit is native to Florida and the rest of the southern United States, and it now grows on every warm continent. A study of its DNA from 160 places around the world put its home in central and southern North America, with the spread to Africa and Australia happening on its own, long before people carried it.

What it does here

A butterfly nursery at ankle height

The Phaon Crescent lays its eggs in clusters on the underside of a leaf, and the caterpillars feed together there. From egg to butterfly takes about a month, so a lawn with frogfruit in it can turn out a new generation every few weeks through the warm months. The Common Buckeye lays on the top of the leaf instead, and the White Peacock lays one egg at a time.

Food for the small ones

The flowers feed bees and small butterflies, including the Ceraunus Blue, that work low to the ground.

How to know it

A mat with matchheads

Look for a flat mat of small opposite leaves, each with a few teeth toward the tip, and flower heads on thin stalks held just above it. Each open flower is white with a yellow spot in the throat, fading to lilac as it ages.

How it grows

Walk on it, mow it, flood it

It takes light foot traffic and mowing, drought and flooding. Mow it no lower than four inches and not while it is in bloom. It handles salty wind but not salt water. It may go dormant in a cold winter and come back with the warmth.
