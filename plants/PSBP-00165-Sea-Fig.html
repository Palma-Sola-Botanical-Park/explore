Sea Fig

At a glance

  • Its thick, three-sided leaves are little water tanks, which lets it carpet hot, sandy, salty ground where little else holds on.
  • Long stems creep across the ground and root as they go, so one plant becomes a spreading mat.
  • The big flowers open in full sun and close again by evening. Ours are magenta-pink with a yellow center.
  • In South Africa one of its relatives is called the sour fig, and its fruit goes into jam.

Cultural significance

Sour figs and burns

Along South Africa's coasts the fruit of one sea fig, the sour fig, has been eaten and made into jam for generations, and the juice squeezed from its leaves is an old remedy for burns, stings and sore throats.

Planted to hold the coast

Through the 1900s sea figs were planted by the mile along California's highways and dunes to hold the sand. They held the surface, but they also smothered native dune plants, and they are now being pulled out of stretches of coast they were planted to protect.

Where to find it here

At the south end of the cactus garden, among the cycads, spreading over the ground near the Cardboard Cycad and the Madagascar Palm.

Where it comes from

Sea figs grow wild on coasts in southern Africa, with relatives in Australia and southern South America. They belong to the same succulent family as the living stones and ice plants of South Africa's dry country. Planted widely for groundcover, they have spread along coastlines far from home.

What it does

Groundcover for a hot spot

Here it fills the ground at the feet of the cycads and spills over the stone edges of the tiers: hot, sunny and quick to drain, which is exactly what it wants.

How to know it

Three-sided leaves

Thick, fleshy, gray-green leaves, three-sided like a short beam and sometimes tinged red. Look closely; there's no need to snap one.

The flower

A large flower with many narrow, shiny petals around a yellow center. Ours are magenta-pink, open in full sun and shut by evening.

A fig on the ground

After flowering, the base of the flower swells into a fleshy, fig-shaped fruit, which is where the name sea fig comes from.

How it grows

Rooting as it goes

Wherever a stem touches soil it can put down roots, so even a broken piece can start a new plant. That is how a single plant becomes a mat, and how it spreads so easily along a coast.

Dry feet

It wants full sun and sharp drainage. In soggy, watered ground its roots rot, so it does best on high, sandy spots that dry quickly.
