At a glance

  • Firewheel looks made for Florida: red-and-yellow flowers, beach-sand toughness, and a habit of reseeding itself with almost no help.
  • The surprise is that it probably is not a Florida native after all.
  • For generations it was treated as one. Newer work points to the Great Plains and northern Mexico as its original home.
  • Bees and butterflies do not care about the paperwork.

Where it comes from

The Great Plains

Its home ground is the central United States into northern Mexico. It reached Florida long enough ago to become thoroughly at home, and for years it was planted on roadsides and in coastal gardens as a native wildflower. A 2020 study of the southeastern flora concluded it had been introduced, and the Florida Native Plant Society and the state plant atlas now list it as non-native.

Where to find it here

Butterfly Garden

In the Butterfly Garden, in open sunny ground. Look for the red-and-yellow daisies about a foot off the ground.

What it does here

Native is messier than it sounds

Firewheel was sold and planted for years as a Florida native. It still does everything a pollinator garden asks of it. Its origin story changed once botanists looked closely, and this bed is a good place to think about what "native" means.

How to know it

Red centers, yellow tips

The rays look dipped in yellow paint. The center is a dark red-brown dome.

Low, loose growth

It branches freely instead of making one tall stem.

Round seed heads

Spent flowers dry into bristly balls packed with seed.

How it grows

Poor soil is fine

Sand, heat and drought bother it less than rich wet ground.

It reseeds freely

Individual plants may not live long, but a patch keeps coming back from seed.

Full sun for the best flowers

In shade it gets lanky and flowers less.
