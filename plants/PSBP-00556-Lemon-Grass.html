Lemon Grass

Cymbopogon citratus

At a glance

  • Beekeepers bait swarm traps with lemongrass oil, because its scent is almost the same as the pheromone scout bees use to say “this is a good home”. Chemically, this grass speaks bee.
  • Crush a leaf and there is the lemon. It comes from one compound, citral, which is most of the plant’s oil and the starting point for making vitamin A in a factory.
  • It almost never flowers, so every lemongrass you have ever seen was grown by pulling a clump apart.
  • Two plants in one for the cook: the pale lower stalk gets pounded into pastes and soups, the green leaves get bruised and steeped like a bay leaf and taken out before serving.

Cultural significance

Fever grass

Fever grass in Jamaica, where it has been a fever tea for centuries; the base of Thai and Vietnamese cooking; a perfume and soap oil traded by the ton. A stalk from an Asian grocery, stood in a glass of water, will root in a fortnight, which is how it has travelled from kitchen to garden across the whole warm world.

Where it comes from

India and Sri Lanka

Southern India and Sri Lanka, in wet grassland and open woodland, though it has been grown across Southeast Asia so long that native and planted blur into each other. It reached Florida through the herb trade and stays a near-permanent perennial in the warm half of the state.

What it does here

The one you are allowed to rub

An edible in the collection, and a plant you are welcome to touch. Rub a leaf between your fingers and smell them. Wildlife gets little from it, since it does not flower, but the dense clump is shelter for small things, and the odd Clouded Skipper caterpillar will eat it when nothing better is about.

How to know it

Smell first

Bruise a leaf. Nothing else in the park smells like this.

A fountain of grass

Narrow blue-green leaves two or three feet long arching out from a tight clump of stems, like a fountain.

Pale bulb-like stalk bases

Thick and pale at the bottom, wrapped in sheathing leaves. That is the kitchen part.

How it grows

Full sun, regular water, any decent soil, and it grows fast in summer and sulks in a cold snap. A clump builds to a hundred stems or more and is kept young by splitting it. Two to four feet tall here.

Take care

The leaf edges are finely saw-toothed and will cut a finger drawn along them the wrong way, so use a knife, not your hands. A herb for people; dogs and cats that graze it get an upset stomach.
