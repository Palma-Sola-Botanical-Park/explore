Lifou Palm

At a glance

  • The collar below the crown is coated in dense white felt. Most crownshaft palms have a smooth green one, so this frosted white column is the thing to look for - and you can pick it out from some distance off.
  • Nobody has a clean count of how many are left. One survey turned up a little over a hundred mature palms, but that was only the ground it covered and nobody pretends it is the whole story. What is not in question is the range: one patch of forest, roughly half the size of Anna Maria Island, and no second population anywhere.
  • It is the odd one out in its genus. Lifou is a raised coral island, and this is the only New Caledonian palm known naturally from that coral limestone - its relatives are all on the mineral rocks of the mainland.
  • The fronds stand up stiff and straight. Its nearest relative arches gracefully downward, which makes the two easy to tell apart once you have noticed it.

Cultural significance

Looked after by the people who live there

The conservation work on Lifou is done with the Jozip tribe, on whose land the palm grows; the island is Drehu to its Kanak community. A plant known from one forest survives or does not largely by the decisions of the people around that forest, and here those decisions have gone its way.

Where it comes from

One forest on Lifou Island

Lifou sits in the Loyalty Islands, about sixty miles off the New Caledonian mainland, and the palm grows in humid forest near a single village there. The island is uplifted coral rather than volcanic rock, which is why this palm sits on limestone while every other member of its genus sits on serpentine.

Rats, pigs, and some good news

Rats have been found working the fruiting stalks and feral pigs turn over the forest floor, which is a hard combination for a palm with nowhere else to go. Monitoring on Lifou has found young plants coming up anyway. For a species this narrowly confined, natural regeneration is the single most encouraging thing you could hope to hear.

Where to find it here

The palm row past the plumeria

Head out toward the big pond and keep going past the plumeria into the open field beyond it. A line of palms runs along the path there. This one is second in the row, with the enormous Talipot Palm on one side of it and the Hurricane Palm on the other.

What it does here

A palm with no room for error

Nothing about it shouts. It is a slim, tidy, well-behaved palm in a row of palms. But in the wild it is known from a single locality on one island, and that is the whole reason it is worth stopping for. Growing it here lets more people meet the plant. What happens on Lifou is still what counts.

Near the cool edge of what it likes

This is a warm-climate palm and Bradenton winters run cooler than its native forest. A genuinely hard freeze would mark the foliage, so how it does here over the long run is worth watching.

Compare the two crowns

Look two spots down the row at the Hurricane Palm and see how its fronds curve over. Then come back to this one, holding its leaves stiffly upright. The two are not related, but put side by side the contrast makes this palm's silhouette very easy to remember.

How to know it

The white collar

The tube of leaf bases below the crown carries a thick whitish wool. On a clean specimen it reads almost as a painted white band.

Fronds that refuse to droop

Feather-shaped and held rigidly upright, not fountaining out the way most palms of this size do. Dark green on top, silvery underneath.

Silver underneath

Look at the underside of a leaflet. It is noticeably paler, almost silvery, against the dark green of the upper surface.

Flower stalks from under the collar

When it flowers, whitish-green stalks up to three feet long push out from below the crownshaft rather than up among the leaves. The fruit that follows is about the size and shape of a small egg and ripens orange-red.

How it grows

Thirty to forty feet, in time

A single slim trunk to roughly thirty or forty feet, less in cultivation than in its own forest, ringed all the way up where old fronds have let go.

Every new plant starts from seed

There is one trunk and one growing point, so there are no offsets to divide. Seed is the only route - and it germinates willingly enough in cultivation.

Warmth and steady water

It came from humid forest and it still expects humid conditions: consistent moisture, shelter from cold, and no long dry spells.

Take care

The heart is edible. Please leave it alone

Crownshaft palms across the Pacific have been cut for palm heart for centuries and this is one of them. Taking the heart kills the palm outright, because that single growing point is all it has - a steeper price than usual for a species holding on in one patch of forest. The orange-red fruit is not known as a human food. Nothing about the plant is poisonous to people, dogs or cats.
