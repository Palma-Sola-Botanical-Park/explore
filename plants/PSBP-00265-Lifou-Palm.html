Lifou Palm

Cyphophoenix nucele

At a glance

  • The collar below the crown is coated in dense white felt. Most crownshaft palms have a smooth green one, so this frosted white column is the thing to look for - and you can pick it out from some distance off.
  • How many survive in the wild is hard to pin down honestly. Older sources put it at fewer than a hundred mature trees; more recent conservation work says the number is not known well enough to give. What everyone agrees on is how little ground there is — a single patch of forest about the size of Anna Maria Island, and no second population anywhere.
  • It is the only palm of its kind growing on raised coral. Its three closest relatives live on the mineral rocks of the New Caledonian mainland; this one crossed open ocean and settled on a different kind of ground entirely.
  • The fronds stand up stiff and straight. Its nearest relative arches gracefully downward, which makes the two easy to tell apart once you have noticed it.

Cultural significance

Looked after by the people who live there

The conservation work on Lifou is run with the Jozip tribe, on whose land the palm grows. Lifou is Drehu to the Kanak people who have always lived on it. A plant known from one forest survives or does not largely by the decisions of the community around that forest, and here those decisions have gone its way.

Where it comes from

One forest on Lifou Island

Lifou sits in the Loyalty Islands, about sixty miles off the New Caledonian mainland, and the palm grows in humid forest near a single village there. The island is uplifted coral rather than volcanic rock, which is why this palm sits on limestone while every other member of its genus sits on serpentine.

Rats and pigs, and some good news

Introduced rats destroy the seed and feral pigs turn over the seedlings, which is a hard combination for a palm with nowhere else to go. Monitoring on Lifou has found young plants coming along anyway. For a species this narrowly confined, natural regeneration is the single most encouraging thing you could hope to hear.

Where to find it here

The palm row past the plumeria

Head out toward the big pond and keep going past the plumeria into the open field beyond it. A line of palms runs along the path there. This one is second in the row, with the enormous Talipot Palm on one side of it and the Hurricane Palm on the other.

What it does here

Rarer than anything near it

Nothing about it shouts. It is a slim, tidy, well-behaved palm in a row of palms. But of everything growing along that path, this is the one with the smallest hold on existence in the wild, and there is a fair argument that palms like ours now outnumber the ones on Lifou. That is most of the reason a botanical park bothers to grow it.

A small test we are running

This is a warm-climate palm, comfortable in zones 10b and 11, and Bradenton sits right at the edge of that. A genuinely hard freeze would mark the foliage. Every winter it comes through clean tells us a little more about how far north this species can actually be grown.

Settle an argument for yourself

Walk two spots down the row to the Hurricane Palm and look at how its fronds curve over. Then come back to this one. Stiff and upright against arched and drooping - once you have seen the two side by side you will never mix up this group of palms again.

How to know it

The white collar

The tube of leaf bases below the crown carries a thick whitish wool. On a clean specimen it reads almost as a painted white band.

Fronds that refuse to droop

Feather-shaped and held rigidly upright, not fountaining out the way most palms of this size do. Dark green on top, silvery underneath.

A ringed trunk

Slender, with clean rings down it where old fronds have let go - one ring for each leaf the palm has finished with.

Flower stalks from under the collar

When it flowers, whitish-green stalks up to three feet long push out from below the crownshaft rather than up among the leaves. The fruit that follows is about the size and shape of a small egg and ripens orange-red.

How it grows

Quick, for what it is

Reportedly the fastest grower in its genus, which is a relative compliment among palms. Thirty to forty feet in time, less in cultivation than in its own forest.

Seed is the only way

There is one trunk and one growing point, so there are no offsets to divide. Every new plant comes from seed - and seed germinates willingly enough, which is the reason a palm this rare in the wild can be found in collections at all.

Warmth and steady water

It came from humid forest and it still expects humid conditions: consistent moisture, shelter from cold, and no long dry spells.

Take care

The heart is edible. Please leave it alone

Crownshaft palms across the Pacific have been cut for palm heart for centuries and this is one of them. Taking the heart kills the palm outright, because that single growing point is all it has. For a species holding on in one patch of forest, that is a steeper price than usual. The orange-red fruit is not a human food and should not be eaten; nothing about the plant is poisonous to people, dogs or cats.
