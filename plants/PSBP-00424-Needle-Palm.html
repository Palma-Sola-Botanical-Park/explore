Needle Palm

Rhapidophyllum hystrix
On this page

At a glance

  • The needles are not thorns on the leaves. They are the tough fibers left from old leaf bases, stiffened into spines up to about a foot long, and they pack tight around the short stem.
  • It is usually ranked the hardiest palm there is. In a winter test in Ohio, far colder than Florida, it kept most of its leaves even at the coldest sites, while windmill palms, a cold-climate favorite, lost about three-quarters of theirs.
  • Its flowers and fruit stay down among the needles, out of sight. The ripe fruit is woolly and smells foul, yet Florida black bears are reported to eat it.

Cultural significance

Dug from the woods

Nurserymen began digging wild Needle Palms for landscaping in the 1920s, and by the 1970s bulldozers were clearing other stands. Florida now regulates the digging of wild plants for sale: it takes a permit, and nursery-grown plants spare the woods.

A porcupine's name

Hystrix is the Greek word for porcupine. The genus name, Rhapidophyllum, seems to mean needle-leaf, for the spines of the leaf bases, though it has also been read as having the leaf of Rhapis, an Asian fan palm.

Where it comes from

Woods of the Southeast

It grows wild from South Carolina through Georgia and Florida to Alabama and Mississippi, in moist hardwood woods, ravines and river bottoms. In Florida it is common in the north and the Panhandle. A wild plant collected in eastern Manatee County in 1993, in river-bottom forest about 20 miles from the park, is among the southernmost records.

The only one of its kind

Rhapidophyllum has just one species. Botanists regard it as a relic, a lone survivor with close relatives among the windmill palms of China and the Himalayas.

What it does here

Young and low

The plants in the main photo are young: a handful of small fans in the mulch, with no trunk and no needles showing yet. The needles are the leftover bases of old leaves, which a young palm has not had time to pile up.

One of the natives

Four other Florida native palms are grown with it here: Dwarf Palmetto, Scrub Palmetto and two thatch palms from the Keys.

How to know it

A fan split nearly to the middle

Each leaf is a fan, perhaps two or three feet across, cut nearly to the middle into narrow, stiff segments. The upper side is glossy dark green and the underside is paler and silvery.

Look at the leaf stalk

The edges of the leaf stalk have no saw teeth, unlike Saw Palmetto's. A Dwarf Palmetto's stalk runs on into the blade as a fold, while a Needle Palm's ends where the blade begins.

A shrub with palm leaves

From a few steps back it can pass for a shrub: a low, rounded clump of fans with no trunk, the short stem hidden under old leaf bases.

How it grows

A fur coat on the stem

The woody stem is only a third or less of the plant's width. The rest is a mat of fibers, up to 8 inches thick in places. One botanist suggested that this coat insulates the stem and helps explain how the palm survives freezes.

Slow work

A leaf can stay on the palm for seven to ten years. Seed is slow too: in one test the first seed sprouted 14 months after sowing, and other batches had not sprouted after 18 months.

One palm, two sexes

Male and female flower stalks usually grow on separate palms. One grower's palm made only male flower stalks for five years, then female ones, then both on the same trunk.

Shade, moisture and no salt

It does best in shade and moist soil, as in the river-bottom woods it comes from, and it dislikes salt.

Take care

Needles at the base

The needles are stiff and sharp. Keep hands out of the base of the plant, and keep children and pets from pushing in under it.
