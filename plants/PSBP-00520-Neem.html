At a glance

  • Neem oil, the garden pest spray, is pressed from this tree's seeds.
  • Its main active compound works less like a poison than a disruptor: insects that eat a treated leaf stop feeding and have trouble molting and laying eggs. It doesn't kill on contact, so bees that only visit the flowers are unlikely to be harmed.
  • It belongs to the mahogany family. Look for long leaves of toothed, curved leaflets.
  • Ours flowered in late spring 2026 and was carrying small green fruit by September.

Cultural significance

Oil, soap and toothbrushes

The seeds are pressed for oil used in soaps and garden sprays. In South Asia people have long chewed neem twigs to clean their teeth, and the tree turns up in traditional medicine across Asia and Africa.

The free tree

The name Azadirachta comes from Persian words for the free or noble tree, a tree that asks little and is used for almost everything: shade, timber, oil, medicine. In India a frayed neem twig, a datun, is still sold as a toothbrush.

Where it comes from

It is native to the Indian subcontinent and Myanmar, in dry tropical forest, and has been planted across the tropics for shade and its many uses. From India it was carried to Africa, the Middle East, Australia and the Caribbean as a village shade tree.

Where to find it here

On the bank of the Big Pond, near the Queen Palm and the Traveller's Palm.

What it does here

The tree behind the bottle

Here you can see the source of something many gardeners keep in the shed. Ours was carrying small green fruit in September; the seeds inside are what get pressed.

Family in the park

Our Mahogany at the corner of Cypress Pond is in the same family. Put a neem leaf beside a mahogany leaf and the family shows: one long stalk with leaflets in pairs. Neem's leaflets are toothed and curved like little scythes; the mahogany's are smooth-edged.

How to know it

Feathery leaves

Long leaves made of many narrow, curved leaflets with toothed edges, and new growth flushed red at the branch tips.

Smell the flowers

In late spring, drooping sprays of small white flowers hang among the leaves. They are sweet-scented, and on a warm day you may smell them before you see them.

Rounded crown

A dense, rounded crown on a straight trunk. Ours is still a young tree, so look for the leaves rather than the size.

How it grows

It is a tree of warm climates, and a hard frost can kill back its branches. Once established it stands long drought, which is why it is planted for shade across dry parts of Africa and Asia.

Flowers, then fruit

Drooping sprays of small, starry, fragrant white flowers appear among the leaves, followed by olive-sized fruits that ripen from green to yellow, each holding one seed, sometimes two.

Take care

Not the oil

Neem oil and seed extracts are not food. Swallowed, concentrated products can make people, and especially children, seriously ill, and they have made cats ill too. Keep the bottle away from both.
