Footstool Palm

Saribus rotundifolius

At a glance

  • Round leaves. Rotundifolius means round-leaved, and each fan is close to a full circle, cut about halfway in at even intervals.
  • Hooks on the stalks. Black spines curve backward along the leaf stalks, most of them toward the base.
  • A palm that changed genus. It was long filed under Livistona, with the Chinese Fan Palm. DNA showed it sits closer to Licuala, so botanists gave it an older name, Saribus.
  • Not from Java. One of its common names is Java Palm, but Java is outside its native range. On Java it was brought in.

Cultural significance

Moved by DNA

Botanists long placed this palm in Livistona, the genus of the Chinese Fan Palm. In 2011 a DNA study showed that it and a few close relatives belonged nearer to Licuala than to the rest of Livistona. Rather than coin a new genus, botanists revived Saribus, a name from the 1830s. Older books and plant tags may still call it Livistona rotundifolia.

Where to find it here

Stand at the entrance to the Butterfly Garden and look to the back left. Just outside the edge of the butterfly plants, you'll see this palm. It is a handsome one, and plenty of people have walked past it for years without knowing what it is.

Where it comes from

It is native to the Philippines and parts of Indonesia. There it grows in wet forest, along rivers, and in swamp forest that dries out in some seasons.

What it does

Next door to the butterflies

It stands right beside the Butterfly Garden, but as far as we can tell, and as far as our reading goes, it has nothing to do with butterflies. It is here for the dappled shade cast by the trees around it.

How to know it

Start with the fan

Each leaf is a nearly round fan, cut about halfway to its center into many even segments. The tips can stand stiff or hang, so a droop alone tells you little.

Costapalmate (this one)
Stalk runs a little way into the blade on a rib before the segments spread.
Check the stalk

Black spines curve backward along the leaf stalks, most of them toward the base. Older palms sometimes lose them, but that is unusual.

Count the flower branches

When it flowers, look where the flower stalk comes out of its sheath. A Saribus usually starts with three main branches. A Livistona, such as the Chinese Fan Palm, starts with one.

Other fan palms here

Taraw Palm's fan is split by a few deep, uneven cuts; the cuts on this one are even. Ribbon Fan Palm's segments are cut much deeper. Thailand Fan Palm's leaf stalks have smooth, sharp edges with no hooks.

How it grows

In Florida gardens it usually ends up somewhere between ten and forty feet tall. In the forests of home it can pass 150 feet. Ours still has a short, shaggy trunk, and we look forward to seeing how tall it gets.

Take care

Mind the spines

Keep hands clear of the sharp, hooked spines on the leaf stalks.
