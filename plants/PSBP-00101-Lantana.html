At a glance

  • A lantana head keeps a record of its own age. New flowers open yellow in the middle and the older ones around them turn pink or red. In a lab study, one grain of pollen on a yellow flower was enough to start the change.
  • In 1877 a naturalist in Brazil reported a lantana whose flowers lasted three days: yellow, then orange, then purple. Butterflies ignored the purple ones.
  • Birds carry its seeds, and its pollen can reach native pineland lantana. University of Florida breeders have made lantanas that set almost no fruit, but a label like 'trailing' or 'hybrid' doesn't say whether a nursery plant is one of them.
  • It reached Hawaii as a garden plant in 1858 and was on every island by 1871.

Where it comes from

Not quite camara

True Lantana camara is a wild plant of tropical America, and it isn't known in Florida. What grows here is a garden creation, several wild lantanas crossed over centuries into hundreds of named forms. The botanist R. W. Sanders named it Lantana strigocamara in 2006, and the specimen that defines the name was collected in Miami in 1981. Most of what is written about lantana says camara, so keep that in mind when you read up on it.

A garden flower that got loose

Lantana was grown in European gardens long ago, and gardeners carried it around the world. It was planted in Calcutta's botanical garden in 1807, reached Cape Town and Hawaii in 1858, and by 1871 was on every Hawaiian island. By one account, Florida gardeners have grown it since 1804. The invasive-species specialists of the IUCN list lantana among 100 of the world's worst invasive species, although we have seen no sign of it spreading here. It basically stays put.

Named for a look-alike

Linnaeus borrowed lantana, the old Latin name of the wayfaring tree, a European shrub whose flower heads look vaguely like these. Camara is said to be a Brazilian name for the plant.

Where to find it here

Lantana grows in a few spots, mostly dotted around the office. We have no big mass plantings.

What it does here

Nectar, but no nursery

Butterflies visit lantana, and in a study in Guyana they were its most effective pollinators. But it feeds adult butterflies only. No Florida butterfly is known to raise caterpillars on it, and in a study in India, patches overrun by lantana held 43 butterfly species against 58 in native vegetation. A plant can be good at one job and no help at the other.

A native that eats it

The lantana lace bug is native to Florida and feeds on lantana until the leaves go almost white and drop. People have sent it to Australia, Fiji and other places as a control agent. It has been less of a cure than hoped: lantana is still one of the world's worst invaders.

How to know it

Read the head

Look at one flower head and read it from the middle out. On the plants in our photographs the middle is yellow or cream, the youngest flowers, and the ring around it is pink, the older ones. Some forms run from yellow to orange to red instead. The order depends on the garden form.

Rough, puckered leaves

Leaves come in opposite pairs, toothed at the edges and rough as sandpaper, with the veins pressed deep into the surface. They smell strongly when disturbed. Stay hands-off: the leaves can irritate sensitive skin.

Square, bristly stems

Stems are square and hairy, and often carry small prickles, though some garden forms have none.

Berries, if it fruits

The berries are small, a little under a quarter inch, and turn from green to purple or blue-black and shiny.

How it grows

One pollen grain

Pollination switches on a red-purple pigment that covers the yellow underneath. In a lab study, a single pollen grain on a yellow flower started it. It is why a head of flowers of several ages shows several colors at once.

A signal, not a gauge

The nectar tells a more careful story. In Guyana, nectar dropped as flowers went from yellow to red, and butterflies preferred the yellow. In Costa Rica a ring of old flowers around the young yellow ones kept nectar robbers off them, yet about a fifth of the old red flowers still held nectar. The color tells a visitor where to look. It does not promise the nectar is gone.

Two ways out

Birds eat the fruit and carry the seeds into natural areas. Pollen is the other way: a fertile lantana can fertilize native pineland lantana, which is rare and grows in far south Florida, one kind only in the pine rocklands of Miami-Dade County. Escape and crossing are separate problems. For both reasons Florida discourages planting it.

Bred to set no fruit

Breeders at the University of Florida's research center at Balm, in Hillsborough County, crossed lantanas to get plants with three sets of chromosomes, which make little viable pollen. Two selections released in 2011 set over ninety-nine percent less fruit than a fertile comparison plant. In a later trial the fertile one averaged about nine fruits per flower stalk, and the bred one 0.04. Our plants could be fertile or not; we haven't established which. A label like 'trailing', a color or the word 'hybrid' doesn't settle it, and trailing lantana is a different species that Florida also discourages.

Cut it back

It recovers fast from hard pruning, which is why gardeners cut it nearly to the ground at the end of the season.

Take care

Do not eat any part

Leaves and unripe berries are poisonous, and nobody should treat berries as safe because they look ripe. Children in Tampa were poisoned by green berries in the early 1960s, and one died. Lantana is toxic to dogs, cats and horses: vomiting, diarrhea, labored breathing and weakness, with liver failure more common in livestock. The leaves can also irritate sensitive skin.
