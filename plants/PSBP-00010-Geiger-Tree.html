Geiger Tree

Cordia sebestena

At a glance

  • Clusters of orange-red flowers can appear again and again through the warm season.
  • Rub a leaf and you have the other name for it — sandpaper leaf. Both surfaces are genuinely rough.
  • Pale, wrinkled fruits follow the flowers.
  • Audubon's White-crowned Pigeon is painted sitting in a flowering Geiger Tree.

Cultural significance

Audubon in Key West

On his 1832 Florida expedition, John James Audubon stayed with a Key West resident named John H. Geiger. His White-crowned Pigeon plate shows the bird perched in a flowering Geiger Tree — which is how the tree and one of his best-known Florida subjects ended up attached to each other. The naming is traditional rather than documented.

Leaves as sandpaper

The rough leaves have reportedly been used in the Caribbean to smooth wood and gourds. The plant's most obvious field mark and its most practical use are the same thing.

Where it comes from

Native to the tropical Americas and the Caribbean. In Florida it occurs in the Keys and the far southern peninsula.

Bradenton is well north of that range. Growing one here is a measure of how close Palma Sola sits to the line between subtropical and genuinely tropical planting.

Where to find it here

On Hibiscus Island and Welcome Island.

When

Look through the warm months for the orange-red clusters. The coarse leaves work as an identifier any time of year.

What it does here

Flowers for visitors with wings

Bees, butterflies and hummingbirds use the orange flowers. It is a documented nectar plant for the Schaus' Swallowtail in southern Florida — that butterfly's range stops well short of here, but the relationship is why the tree matters where it is native.

Birds and other animals take the pale fruit.

How to know it

Feel the leaf

The thick oval leaves are coarse enough to feel like sandpaper. This is the best identifier when the tree is not flowering.

Flowers

Dense orange-red clusters, each flower opening into a broad trumpet.

Fruit

Pale gray-white, fleshy and wrinkled, about the size of a small plum.

From a distance it is a small, often multi-trunked tree with a rounded, irregular crown — not a tall forest shape.

How it grows

Slow-growing, sun-loving, and suited to sandy ground that drains sharply.

Built for the coast

Salt spray, heat and drought are ordinary conditions where this tree grows naturally, and an established one takes all three.

When water is scarce

In a long dry spell it can drop leaves, cutting its losses until conditions improve.
