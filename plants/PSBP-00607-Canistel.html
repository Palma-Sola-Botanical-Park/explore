At a glance

  • This fruit was never meant for birds. The weight, the big seeds, the heavy scent and the orange colour are all aimed at mammals - monkeys and opossums in its home forest, who carried the seeds off. None of those live here, so in Florida the tree depends entirely on people.
  • It ripens after you pick it, which sounds forgiving and is not. Take one too early and it will never soften properly - it just stays astringent, however long you wait.
  • No two are the same shape. Round, oval, spindled, some with a little point on the end. Shape tells you nothing about ripeness; only colour and give do.
  • Every twig and every cut in the bark leaks sticky white latex. It is the family signature, and it will gum up your pruning shears.

Cultural significance

Three sapotes, one family

Sapote is a word that got attached to a great many unrelated tropical fruits. The park grows a Black Sapote and a White sapote, and neither is in the sapote family at all - one is a persimmon relative, the other is closer to citrus. Canistel, which nobody calls a sapote, is the real thing.

A fruit for the end of the meal

Across tropical America and the Caribbean it is mostly eaten as a dessert - fresh with a spoon, blended into drinks, or turned into custards, ice cream and baked things, where the dryness that makes it odd raw becomes the point.

Where it comes from

Lowland forest in Central America

Native from southern Mexico through Central America. It spread across the Caribbean and on to Southeast Asia, East Africa and Australia almost entirely because people carried it, which for a tree that lost its wild dispersers is a fair trade.

Of Campeche

The species name means of Campeche, on Mexico's Yucatan Peninsula, where Humboldt and Bonpland first described the tree for science in the early nineteenth century.

Where to find it here

Rare Fruit, on the way to the gazebo

Coming into the Rare Fruit area from the restroom end, follow the crushed shell path toward the gazebo. Canistel is on your right. The Lychee is along the same stretch, so look for the tree with narrow glossy leaves bunched at the ends of its twigs rather than the big rounded one.

What it does here

A fruit tree that needs us

In its own forest this tree had monkeys and opossums to move its seed. In Florida it has gardeners, and nothing else - the fruit is simply too big and too heavy for the animals here to bother with. A canistel in a collection is not an ornament; it is the whole dispersal system the species has left in this hemisphere.

Getting it right is the hard part

Most people who try canistel once and dislike it ate one that was not ready. The skin has to turn from green to yellow-orange on the tree, and then the fruit has to soften off it. Rushed, it is dry and puckering. Given its time, it is closer to pudding.

How to know it

Glossy leaves bunched at the tips

Evergreen, simple and narrow, crowded toward the ends of the twigs so the branches look tufted rather than evenly clothed.

Golden fruit

Three to five inches long, green hardening to a bright yellow-orange. Colour and a slight give are the only honest signs it is close to ready.

White latex

Break a twig and it bleeds sticky white sap. Worth knowing before you handle a cut branch: it is a mild skin irritant for some people, and gloves are the easy answer.

How it grows

Warm, sunny, well drained

A tropical tree at heart. It wants sun and soil that drains, and cold is what limits it in Florida. Twenty to thirty-five feet here, far taller in its own forest.

Grafted, or a long wait

Grafted trees are the standard because they fruit sooner and predictably. Grow one from seed and you wait three to six years to find out what you have got - and the seed must go in the ground within days of leaving the fruit or it dies.
