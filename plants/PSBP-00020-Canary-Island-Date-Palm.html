At a glance

  • The Canary Islands may be named for dogs: Pliny wrote that the island of Canaria swarmed with huge ones. The canary bird and this palm are both named for the islands.
  • On La Gomera, tappers draw about three gallons of sap a day from the crown and boil it down into a dark palm honey.
  • Ours is a female: in July 2026 its crown was hung with orange stalks of small green dates.
  • Its yellowing old leaves are a potassium store. Cutting them off for neatness speeds the palm's decline.

Cultural significance

Palm honey

On La Gomera, a tapper shaves a thin slice off the growing tip each evening, and sap runs overnight down a cane channel into a bucket. It is collected at dawn, before the sun can ferment it, and boiled for hours with nothing added into a dark syrup, miel de palma. A palm is rested four or five years before it is tapped again.

The islands' palm

Since 1991 this palm and the canary bird have been the official natural symbols of the Canary Islands. Its leaves are woven there into baskets and fishing creels, and the small flower stalks of male palms once made brooms for sweeping out bread ovens.

Islands of the dogs

Pliny, writing in the first century, passed on a report from King Juba II's expedition that Canaria held multitudes of very large dogs, and in the same passage noted that it grew date palms in great numbers. Some historians think the name came from a Berber people, the Canarii, instead.

Where it comes from

A ravine palm

It grows wild only in the Canary Islands, off northwest Africa, in ravine bottoms and on lower slopes where its roots reach groundwater, mostly below about 2,000 feet. The wild groves are protected under Spanish and European law.

Keeping it pure

The biggest threat at home is crossing with the date palm. In 2006 the islands banned bringing in palms from anywhere, mainland Spain included, and in 2016 they became the first place in the world to wipe out the red palm weevil.

The grocery date's cousin

Supermarket dates come from a different species, Phoenix dactylifera. This one's dates are about an inch long, with thin flesh that is edible but not worth eating. The species of Phoenix cross readily, so seed from a garden with several kinds can come up as hybrids.

What it does here

A female in fruit

Every Canary palm is either male or female, and only the females fruit. The dates on ours ripen from green to orange and then reddish purple. A male carries pollen instead.

A garden in the leaf bases

Old leaf bases cling to the trunk for years, and the pockets between them catch dead leaves and rain. Plants move in: a fern is growing among the leaf bases on ours. On Canary palms in New Zealand, botanists have counted 152 kinds of plants living this way, mostly ferns.

Cousins here

We also grow three other date palms: Reclining, Pygmy and Silver Date Palm. Of the four, this is the one that builds a single trunk up to three feet thick.

How to know it

Read the outline

From a distance: one stout trunk under a big, round crown of arching, bright green feather leaves, each with up to 200 stiff leaflets folded lengthwise into a V. A full-grown crown can spread 20 to 40 feet.

The trunk keeps a record

When the old leaf bases finally fall away, they leave diamond-shaped scars. On older palms the bottom foot or so of the trunk is covered with a brush of short roots, starting above the ground.

How it grows

Slow and long-lived

It grows slowly, in time reaching 40 to 50 feet with a trunk two to three feet thick. In the Canaries, palms live 200 to 300 years. It takes cold down to about 20 degrees Fahrenheit.

Count the leaves

A healthy crown carries 130 to 150 leaves and in South Florida grows about 50 new ones a year. Florida's sandy soils run short of potassium, and the palm moves what it has from its oldest leaves to its newest, so the lowest leaves turn yellow-orange first. Short of potassium, a crown can fall to half its leaves or fewer. Magnesium shortage, over-pruning and disease can thin a crown too.

Two Florida killers

Fusarium wilt, a fungus, reached Florida in 1994 and travels mainly on pruning saws, which is why careful crews disinfect blades between palms. Lethal bronzing, spread by a small planthopper, was first found in Florida in 2006 around Tampa Bay. Both are fatal.

Take care

Spines at the base of every leaf

The lowest leaflets on every leaf are rigid spines, two to eight inches long. They go deep, and the wounds easily get infected. Keep hands out of the crown and pick up a fallen leaf by its tip, not its base.
