Angel-wing Jasmine

Jasminum laurifolium
On this page

At a glance

  • Each flower is a white star of nine to eleven narrow petals, about an inch and a half across, opening from a slim pink-red bud. They are very fragrant.
  • Florida gardeners loved it for decades under the wrong names.
  • Its leaves shine, and each has three veins running up from the base.
  • In Florida it blooms in flushes several times a year, with a few flowers in between.

Cultural significance

Florida's favorite, misnamed

It came into Florida through the federal plant introduction garden in Miami in 1930, from a French nursery. Florida nurseries went on to sell it under at least four wrong names, and gardeners kept buying it whatever the label said. By 1949 it was reported to be in greater demand than any other jasmine planted in the state.

Where it comes from

Himalaya to southern China

It grows wild from the eastern Himalaya and Bangladesh through Myanmar and Thailand to southern China.

Named from the wrong islands

Kew first described it in 1898 as a new species, Jasminum nitidum, from plants a London nurseryman had brought in from the Admiralty Islands, north of New Guinea. It had only been planted there. Botanists later folded it into Jasminum laurifolium, the laurel-leaved jasmine, but nitidum, meaning shining, is still the name it is often sold under.

What it does here

Something usually open

Because it blooms in flushes with stragglers between them, there is usually a flower or two to smell. Ours was in flower in March and again in June 2026. Buds that get full sun color up bright pink; in shade they stay paler.

Who visits?

Nobody seems to have studied what pollinates it. The long, narrow flower tube and the strong scent suggest moths or butterflies. If you see what comes to the flowers here, tell us.

How to know it

A many-pointed star

The open flower has nine to eleven narrow white petals on a slender tube, and the backs of the petals are often flushed pink. Behind each flower, slender calyx teeth stand out at right angles like spokes.

Shining, three-veined leaves

The leaves are simple, glossy and set in pairs, two to three inches long, each with three veins running up from the base.

Not the other star jasmine

Confederate jasmine is also sold as star jasmine. Its flowers are smaller, with five petals twisted like a pinwheel, and its stems bleed milky sap.

How it grows

Long, scrambling stems

It is a bushy evergreen shrub that sends out long, flexible stems. Trimmed, it stays a mound or a low hedge; left alone, the stems lean into their neighbors and scramble through them, reaching ten feet or so.

Frost-tender

Frost is its limit. It grows best in the southern half of the Florida peninsula, and a freeze can kill it.

Fruit seldom seen

Its fruit is rarely seen in gardens; the botanists who first described it never saw any.

Predicted to spread

Florida's risk assessment for new plants predicts it could become invasive here (2021). It is not on the state's invasive plant list, and escapes are rare: one old record near Homestead, and plants found growing wild near Corpus Christi, Texas, in 2015.
