At a glance

  • This is the only species in its genus. For more than a century botanists knew it only from fruit and seed collected in the 1800s; living trees brought it back to science in 1987.
  • It is Critically Endangered. A 1997 survey found 32 palms growing naturally, on three islands at the southern end of Vanuatu; the rest of the known palms were planted in villages.
  • People in Vanuatu never lost track of it. The green fruit is eaten, the leaves become thatch and brooms, and the big leaf sheath makes a carrying basket.
  • Ours is on the walkway along the south side of Big Pond, the fourth palm from the bridge to the Rare Fruit Garden.

Cultural significance

Known in the villages, lost to botany

On Aneityum the palm is called Nohoej; across Vanuatu it is the Bangulu Palm. Villagers plant it near their homes: the soft inside of the green fruit is eaten, the leaves are used for thatch and brooms, and the large sheath that wraps the flower stalk serves as an improvised container. When science lost the palm for a hundred years, the people who lived with it had not.

Where it comes from

Three southern islands

Vanuatu is a chain of volcanic islands in the South Pacific, about a thousand miles east of Australia. Wild Vanuatu Palms grow in moist lowland rainforest on Aneityum, Futuna and Tanna, the three southernmost islands, and planted ones stand in villages on many others.

A rediscovery

The species was named in 1875 from fruit collected on Aneityum, with the locality recorded only as "New Hebrides", Vanuatu's colonial name. The specimens were later filed under the wrong genus in London, and for a century no botanist saw a living tree. In 1987 the Australian palm botanist John Dowe came across palms planted in villages on Espiritu Santo, and the species was finally studied from living material.

Where to find it here

Big Pond, south walkway

Take the walkway along the south side of Big Pond. Counting from the bridge into the Rare Fruit Garden at the southeast corner, the Vanuatu Palm is the fourth palm on the pond side: Satake Palm, Açaí Palm, Pemba Palm, then Vanuatu Palm, with Teddy Bear Palm next after it.

What it does here

Part of a collection we are still uncovering

This palm belongs to a group of unusual palms that a longtime volunteer bought and planted around the park over many years, spaced along this walkway to frame it. Most were never labelled for visitors, and it is easy to walk past a genus with a single species and 32 wild trees without knowing it.

How to know it

Start with the whole palm

A single trunk, a long glossy green crownshaft, and feather-shaped fronds that arch strongly over. On our young palm the crownshaft is most of what you see above the base.

The base

The trunk swells into a bottle shape at the ground, ringed with old leaf scars. Ours already shows it, and the swelling stays as the palm gains height.

Along a frond

The leaflets do not lie flat. They angle upward from the stalk on both sides, so a frond seen from the end makes a V.

If it has fruit

The fruit is big for a palm of this size, about two and a half inches long, and ripens from green to red. Near the tip is a small scar set slightly off centre; a centred scar means one of its relatives.

How it grows

A wet-forest palm

At home it is a rainforest palm, growing up through the understory into the light. It wants steady moisture and warmth, and cold nights will mark the leaves. Wild trees can pass eighty feet; in Florida gardens thirty to forty is more usual.

One trunk, both sexes

Male and female flowers grow on the same tree, so one mature palm can set fruit by itself. Ours has not flowered yet.

Take care

Leave the fruit on the palm

The soft interior of the green fruit is a traditional food in Vanuatu, where people know which stage to pick. Once the fruit ripens the inside turns to a hard shell. Please leave any fruit here on the palm.
