Four-o'clock

At a glance

  • It keeps late hours. The flowers open in late afternoon or early evening and close again the next morning, which is how it got its name. Cloudy weather can throw the schedule off.
  • One plant can wear several colors. A single Four-o'clock can carry flowers of different colors, even striped or splashed ones. Every photo we have of ours shows the same hot pink.
  • A root that waits. An older plant builds a thick, tuberous root, so the stems can die back and the plant comes up again from below.

Cultural significance

An Aztec garden plant

A plant identified as Four-o'clock appears in an Aztec herbal from the sixteenth century. It was already a garden plant in Mexico before it ever reached Europe.

A genetics lesson in pink

Early geneticists crossed dark-pink Four-o'clocks with white ones and got lighter-pink offspring: not one parent's color or the other's, but something in between. That in-between result is called incomplete dominance.

Genes outside the nucleus

It helped show something stranger, too. The green chloroplasts in its leaves carry DNA of their own, and in the classic crosses that DNA was passed down through the seed parent. Part of a plant's inheritance travels outside the nucleus altogether.

Where to find it here

On Welcome Island, the island the driveway circles just inside the entrance. No path gives you a good look at it. You are more likely to notice it from the driveway as you drive past. For much of the year it is a plain green shrub you would never look at twice. In the fall it puts on its show.

Where it comes from

Tropical America. People have grown it for so long, and carried it so far, that it now turns up in warm places all over the world.

What it does

Who comes at dusk

The flowers are fragrant, and the scent climbs to a peak in the evening. Researchers traced the main scent to the flared, colored part of the flower. Long-tongued hawkmoths come for the nectar, and hummingbirds visit too, so it is not strictly a night flower.

Here it is a quiet shrub that few people notice. We have not watched it at dusk to see who comes calling. If you have, tell us.

How to know it

The trumpet is not petals

The bright trumpet is the calyx: the sepals, which on most flowers just protect the bud, here fused into a colored tube that flares into five broad lobes. Each flower rises from a small green cup.

Leaves and habit

A bushy plant with smooth leaves in opposite pairs. Color alone will not name it; the trumpet shape and the late opening are the better clues. Come by in the morning and again late in the day, and see what has changed.

How it grows

An older plant builds a thick, tuberous root. When the stems die back for a season, that root survives and sends up new growth. Seeds give it a second way to carry on.

Take care

The seeds and roots are poisonous if eaten and can upset the stomach. Handling the roots can irritate the skin.
