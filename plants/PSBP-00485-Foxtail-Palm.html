Foxtail Palm

Wodyetia bifurcata
On this page

At a glance

  • Each frond's leaflets stick out all around the stalk, making a full, bushy frond like a fox's tail.
  • It grows wild in only one place: the granite boulder country of Cape Melville in far north Queensland.
  • Its genus name honors Wodyeti, an Aboriginal man who knew that country.

Cultural significance

Named for Wodyeti

The genus Wodyetia is named for Wodyeti, an Aboriginal man of the Cape Melville area who knew the land and its plants. Western botanists first took notice in the late 1970s and described the palm in 1983.

From the wild to every street

Its seeds were once collected illegally from the wild. Today cultivated trees supply the seed, and it has become one of the most widely planted palms in warm climates.

Where it comes from

Cape Melville, in far north Queensland, Australia: rugged granite boulder fields and coarse sand, where it grows among vine thickets and dry rainforest.

Where to find it here

At the nursery parking area, beside the Pitch Apple.

What it does here

A frond to compare

Most feather palms hold their leaflets in two flat rows along the stalk. The Foxtail's spray out all around it, which is what makes the frond look bushy. Stand to the side of a frond to see it best.

How to know it

Bushy fronds

Arching feather fronds with leaflets spraying out all around the stalk.

Smooth trunk

A single smooth, gray trunk with neat rings, topped by a smooth pale-green crownshaft.

Orange-red fruit

Mature palms bear heavy clusters of egg-sized orange-red fruit below the crownshaft.

How it grows

It cleans itself: old fronds drop off on their own, leaving a tidy ringed trunk.
