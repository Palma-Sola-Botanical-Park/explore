Foxtail Palm

Wodyetia bifurcata
On this page

At a glance

  • Each frond's leaflets stick out all around the stalk, making a full, bushy frond like a fox's tail.
  • It grows wild in only one place: a few square miles of granite boulder country on Cape Melville in far north Queensland.
  • Its genus honors Wodyeti, an Aboriginal man said to have been the last with traditional knowledge of the plants of that country.
  • Nurseries weren't allowed to grow it until 1992. Within a generation it became one of the most planted palms in the warm world.

Cultural significance

Named for Wodyeti

The genus Wodyetia is named for Wodyeti, an Aboriginal man of the Cape Melville area. He died in 1978, about the time the palm came to the attention of Western botanists, and it was formally described in 1983.

From the wild to every street

Once it was described, demand ran far ahead of supply. For years seed was collected and smuggled out of the national park, until nurseries were allowed to grow it legally in 1992. Cultivated trees now supply all the seed anyone needs.

Where it comes from

Its entire wild range fits inside Cape Melville National Park in far north Queensland, Australia: granite boulder fields and coarse sand, where it grows among vine thickets and dry rainforest.

Where to find it here

At the nursery parking area, beside the Pitch Apple.

What it does

A frond to compare

Most feather palms hold their leaflets in two flat rows along the stalk. The Foxtail's spray out all around it, which is what makes the frond look bushy. Stand to the side of a frond to see it best, then compare it with any of the flatter-leaved palms in the park.

How to know it

Bushy fronds

Arching feather fronds with leaflets spraying out all around the stalk, eight to ten fronds to a crown.

Smooth trunk

A single smooth, gray trunk with neat rings, topped by a smooth pale-green crownshaft.

Orange-red fruit

Mature palms bear heavy clusters of egg-sized orange-red fruit below the crownshaft.

The name in the seed

The species name bifurcata, twice divided, refers to the forked fibers inside the fruit, which show only when the pulp is cleaned off a seed.

How it grows

It cleans itself

Old fronds drop off on their own, leaving a tidy ringed trunk.

Flowers and fruit

Flower stalks appear just below the crownshaft and carry both male and female flowers. The fruits that follow ripen orange-red in big, heavy clusters.
