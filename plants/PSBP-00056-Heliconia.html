At a glance

  • Look between the red bracts: the slim orange tubes with dark tips are the actual flowers, and on this heliconia they are half the show.
  • Each flower opens just before dawn and has stopped making nectar by early afternoon.
  • In Brazil a skipper butterfly pollinates it before sunrise, alongside hummingbirds and an orchid bee.
  • High school volunteers dig its wandering rhizomes out of the cannas next door and send stray cannas back the other way.

Cultural significance

Once a banana

Linnaeus put the first heliconia he knew into the banana genus. In 1771 he gave them a genus of their own and named it for Mount Helicon, home of the Muses in Greek myth, and his son named this species in 1782. Psittacorum means of the parrots, and its common names, parrot's beak and parakeet flower, say the same.

A florist's flower

It is grown for the cut-flower trade across the tropics, and in the 1980s the University of Florida trialed it in Fort Lauderdale as a Florida crop, from a form collected in a Barbados garden. A cut stem lasts two weeks or more in a vase. Nurseries sell dozens of named forms, and several of the popular ones are sterile.

Where it comes from

Panama to Paraguay

It is native from Panama and Trinidad across northern and central South America, as far as Bolivia, Paraguay and Brazil. In the wild it stands about two and a half feet tall; in a Florida bed it grows to three to six feet. Its family sits beside the bananas and the birds-of-paradise.

Not by seed, here

Florida researchers in the 1980s reckoned that no hummingbird in the United States had a bill long enough to work the flowers, and they never saw it set seed here. Yet in Brazil its main pollinator is a short-billed emerald that carries the pollen on its forehead. Either way, ours spreads mainly underground, and it is on none of Florida's invasive lists.

Where to find it here

Outside the office front door: the mass planting ringed by white riprap, with the cannas beside it.

What it does here

Sorting rhizomes

The rhizomes push out in every direction, against the rock border, into the cannas next door and sometimes into paths and other beds. Staff and high school volunteers dig out the runaways and replant them in the mass. Canna rhizomes turn up in the heliconia bed too and go back the other way. The heliconia rhizomes are the thinner ones, which helps when both come out of the same hole.

Rocks don't stop it

In the Fort Lauderdale trials, two-year-old beds packed in more than sixty shoots to the square foot, and the advice was a solid barrier at least a foot deep. A ring of rocks marks the edge of our bed; underground, the rhizomes make their own decisions.

Worth the wait

Cut back, or overrun by wedelia, the bed can look rough for a while. When the stalks are up and both plants are flowering, orange heliconia beside red canna, it draws people in, and visitors sometimes need a moment to tell the two apart.

How to know it

Bracts and flowers

The pointed, boat-shaped bracts are modified leaves, held in one flat row up the stalk, red to orange and fairly shallow. Between them stand the slender true flowers, yellow to orange with a dark green-black band near the tip, about twenty to a stalk, opening a few at a time.

Canna or heliconia?

Stand where the two beds meet. Canna opens soft, loose petals in clusters; heliconia holds stiff bracts and narrow flowers in a flat, angular spike. The leaves are harder: both are long and banana-like, so start with the flowers.

One spike per shoot

What looks like a stem is a tight roll of leaf bases. Each shoot flowers once, from its top, then dies back while new shoots come up from the rhizome around it.

How it grows

A morning flower

In a Brazilian study the flowers opened at about half past four in the morning, nectar peaked at first light, and production stopped by about half past twelve. At peak bloom one glittering-throated emerald held a quarter of the patch as its territory, chasing off other hummingbirds, an orchid bee and even crickets; the morning nectar of about a hundred flowers came close to a hummingbird's daily energy needs. Late in the season the defending stopped, and a skipper made nine visits in ten, arriving before sunrise.

Warm nights

It grows and flowers without a break while nights stay above about fifty degrees. In south Florida flowering peaks from July to September and stops when the nights turn cold, and a hard chill burns the leaves back to the rhizomes. Wind tears the blades along their veins.
