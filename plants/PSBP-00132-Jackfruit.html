Jackfruit

At a glance

  • The fruit grows straight out of the trunk and the big limbs, not from the twigs. Only the heavy wood can carry it.
  • A single jackfruit is a whole flower cluster fused into one fruit. Ordinary ones weigh ten to sixty pounds; the giants pass a hundred.
  • Native to southwestern India, and grown across the tropics for so long that it is hard to say where cultivation ends.
  • Ours stands near the entrance to the Rare Fruit Garden, on the right, and it has fruited here.

Cultural significance

Eaten at two ages

Ripe, the pulp around each seed is sweet and strongly aromatic and is eaten fresh. Picked young and green, the whole fruit is cooked as a vegetable, and its fibrous texture has made it a meat substitute far from home. The seeds are boiled or roasted.

Oiled knives

The latex sticks to everything, so cooks oil their hands and their blades before cutting one open.

Where it comes from

Southwestern India

Kew gives the native range as the Western Ghats of southwestern India. From there it travelled with people across South and Southeast Asia and on around the tropics; it is the national fruit of Bangladesh and the state fruit of Kerala.

One of the figs

Jackfruit is in the fig and mulberry family. Its closest well-known relative is Breadfruit, the tree the Bounty was carrying when its crew mutinied.

Where to find it here

Rare Fruit Garden

From the pavilion and the raised restrooms, go to the big Manatee Rare Fruit Council sign at the north end of the Rare Fruit Garden and walk south. The jackfruit is the first tree past the sign, on your right: a dense, round-headed tree with big glossy leaves, a pale trunk and one heavy limb reaching low over the grass.

What it does here

Part of the Rare Fruit Garden

This corner of the park is planted and tended by the Manatee Rare Fruit Council, whose members grow tropical fruit most people have only seen in a market, if at all. Jackfruit is one of the showpieces: the tree with the largest fruit any tree carries, growing at the cool edge of where it will grow at all.

Watch the trunk

Ours has fruited. In late spring and summer, look at the trunk and the base of the big limbs rather than up into the canopy. That is where the fruit will be.

How to know it

Fruit on the trunk

When it is carrying fruit there is nothing else like it: green, knobbly, oblong fruits, some the size of a watermelon, hanging straight off the trunk and the thickest branches. Botanists call the habit cauliflory.

Glossy, leathery leaves

Oval, thick, dark green and shiny, four to eight inches long, with a blunt tip. Sprouts and young trees sometimes carry lobed leaves; on a mature tree they are plain.

White sap

Snap a leaf stalk and a sticky white latex beads out. It is the family trait of the figs and mulberries, and it is why jackfruit is cut with oiled hands and knives.

Two kinds of flower spike

Male and female flowers grow on the same tree, in separate club-shaped spikes. Only the female spikes, on the trunk and the thick wood, swell into fruit.

How it grows

Warm and well drained

A tropical tree at the cool edge of its range here. It wants the warmest spot going, with good drainage; a hard freeze damages it and standing water can kill it.

Fast, and big if you let it

It grows quickly and can reach thirty to sixty feet. In a garden it is usually pruned to keep the fruit within reach.

Fruit takes months

A fruit takes months to fill out after the flower spike sets, which is why a tree can carry fruit at several sizes at once.
