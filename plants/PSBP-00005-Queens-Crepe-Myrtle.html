At a glance

  • For a few weeks in early summer the whole crown turns pink, in upright clusters at the end of every branch. The two by the front pond are photographed more than almost anything else here.
  • The petals are thin and crumpled, like crepe paper. That is where the name comes from.
  • The leaves are brewed as a medicinal tea across the Philippines and India, where the tree is called banabá.
  • In parts of Asia the timber is rated second only to teak, and has built houses and boats for centuries.

Cultural significance

Banabá tea

Across the Philippines and India the leaves are dried and brewed, traditionally for blood sugar. The compound behind it, corosolic acid, has since been through clinical study. It is one of the better-documented cases of a folk remedy turning out to have something in it.

Second only to teak

The timber is held in high regard through much of tropical Asia, ranked just behind teak, and has gone into houses and boats for a very long time. The state of Maharashtra in India claims the flower as its own.

Where it comes from

Tropical Asia and beyond

Native across tropical South and Southeast Asia and out into Melanesia — India, the Philippines, Malaysia, and as far as northern Australia.

Where to find it here

Two at the front pond

As you come into the park and the driveway bends left at the pond, there are two of them right there. In bloom they are hard to miss and harder to walk past, and they end up in a great many visitors' photographs.

What it does here

The tree people stop for

These two do a particular job at the entrance: they are the first thing many visitors photograph, before they have properly arrived. For a few weeks a year the drive in is the best thing in the park.

Pollen in the hot months

It flowers through the summer heat, when very little else in a Florida landscape is offering much. Work at the University of Florida recorded honeybees, bumblebees and native carpenter bees all working these flowers — a wide range of bee sizes on one tree.

How to know it

Clusters held upright

The flowers come in big cone-shaped clusters standing up at the ends of the branches, rather than hanging. Each bloom is three or four inches across with six crinkled petals.

Look into the middle

The generous yellow brush of stamens in the centre is largely for show. The tree offers it to bees as food, while the pollen that actually does the fertilising sits on different, far less conspicuous stamens off to the side. The bee gets fed, the tree gets pollinated, and the showy part is the decoy.

Capsules that stay all winter

After flowering, hard brown woody capsules form and hang on the bare branches through the cool months. Seed-eating birds work them over long after the flowers have gone.

How it grows

Fast, and big for a crepe myrtle

This is not the shrubby crepe myrtle of suburban front yards. It grows quickly to thirty feet and can reach sixty, with a rounded to vase-shaped crown and a real trunk under it.

Take care

Not a food plant here

The leaves have a long history as a tea elsewhere, but this is not something to pick and brew from a park tree, and the fruit is not eaten. It is not toxic to people, dogs or cats.
