At a glance

  • Each leaf is a round fan up to a foot across, cut almost to the middle into nine to eleven narrow fingers. Multifida means many-cleft.
  • The plant that defines the species is a picture: an engraving of one growing in a garden near London in 1732, two decades before Linnaeus named it.
  • Each branch of the red flower head ends in one larger female flower, ringed by smaller male ones.
  • The fruits ripen from green to yellow and are in no hurry to open. Many spurges fling their seeds; this one barely splits.

Cultural significance

A garden plant for three centuries

Coral Plant was growing in an English garden before botanists had a name for it. The plant Linnaeus meant when he named the species in 1753 is the one engraved in 1732 from a garden at Eltham, outside London, so the official type of this tropical shrub is a picture of a cultivated plant, not a wild one. By 1837 it was growing in the Philippines, where a Spanish friar described it all over again under another name.

Physician's food

Jatropha is built from the Greek for physician and food. In West Africa the seeds were a purgative and the seed oil a rub for rheumatism and itch, and in the Philippines the sap went on wounds. Healers in Benin still use the sap to stop bleeding, and in laboratory tests it made blood clot almost at once. That is history and chemistry, not a remedy: the same seeds have put children in the hospital.

Where it comes from

Mexico and the islands

It is native to Mexico and the Caribbean islands, from Cuba and Hispaniola down to Trinidad, in tropical country with a long dry season. Gardens have since carried it across the warm world.

Rarely loose in Florida

Florida has grown it since at least the 1950s, from a Miami campus planting to Lido Key in Sarasota. In all that time Florida's plant atlas records just one self-sown plant, found at a pond edge in Broward County in 2019. It is on none of Florida's invasive lists.

Once filed with cassava

An eighteenth-century botanist moved it into the cassava genus, and it is easy to see why: the fingered leaves look a lot like cassava's. The two are close cousins in the spurge family, the same family as our Garden Croton, Copperleaf, Pencil Tree and Peregrina.

Where to find it here

In the cactus and succulent garden, just inside the block retaining wall, within a few steps of the Queensland Bottle Tree and the Desert Rose.

What it does here

A head that stays red for weeks

The flowers in a head open a few at a time, so one head stays red for weeks while the first fruits swell underneath it. Buds, open flowers and green fruit often share the same head.

Nectar for butterflies

Butterflies work the heads for nectar, and hummingbirds sometimes stop too. Who actually pollinates it, here or at home, nobody seems to have studied.

Same family, different trick

Its relatives here, Garden Croton and Copperleaf, catch your eye with painted leaves. Coral Plant keeps its leaves plain green and puts the color into the flower heads, held on long red stalks above the foliage.

How to know it

Follow the fingers back

Choose one leaf and follow its lobes inward with your eyes. They all run back to the top of a long stalk, and many carry smaller side lobes of their own. The fan is green above and paler underneath.

Dishes, not tubes

Each flower is a flat scarlet dish about the size of a fingernail, five rounded petals around a yellow knot of stamens. The heads are flat-topped and sit on stalks up to about nine inches long.

The fringe at the base

Look where each leaf stalk meets the stem. A small fringe of thread-like stipules sits there, finely divided like the leaf itself, each piece under an inch long.

How it grows

Built for a dry season

It grows into a shrub or small tree of eight to fifteen feet, as wide as it is tall if nobody prunes it, on smooth gray-brown stems. The stems are a little succulent and the roots tuberous, which is how it rides out a drought once it is settled. Salt it does not like.

Fruit in no hurry

A fertilized flower becomes a three-lobed capsule about an inch across, green at first and then yellow. It opens late, or barely at all, to let out its three mottled seeds.

Cold

Frost burns it back, though it usually recovers quickly. It is a little more tender than its cousin Peregrina, and full sun gives the most flowers.

Take care

Seeds and sap

Every part is poisonous, the seeds most of all; one seed can make someone sick. The seeds are said to taste pleasant, and children who ate the fruits have needed hospital care. The sap irritates skin and eyes. Keep children and pets away from the fruit and seeds.
