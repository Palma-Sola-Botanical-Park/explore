Bald Cypress

Taxodium distichum

At a glance

  • Unlike most conifers, Bald Cypress drops all of its feathery leaves each year. They turn rusty red before falling, leaving the tree bare in winter.
  • The woody “knees” rising around its roots are part of the root system. Scientists have proposed several functions for them, but exactly what the knees do remains unsettled.
  • Bald Cypress can live an extraordinarily long time. A tree documented in North Carolina was at least 2,624 years old when studied.
  • Our Bald Cypresses surround Cypress Pond, where their crowns help make it the shadiest of the park’s three pond edges.

Cultural significance

Built for wet places

Bald Cypress was once an important timber tree across the Gulf South. Its durable heartwood resists decay, which made it especially useful for boats, docks, bridges, water tanks and buildings in damp places. Extensive logging removed many of the great old cypress forests that once grew across the Southeast.

Where it comes from

A tree of southern wetlands

Bald Cypress is native to the southeastern United States, including Florida. It grows naturally along rivers and pond margins and in swamps and forests that flood seasonally.

Where to find it here

Cypress Pond

Our Bald Cypresses grow around Cypress Pond, the smallest of the park’s three ponds. More trees surround this pond than either of the others, and the cypresses are a large part of why it feels noticeably shadier.

Watch it change

Look for soft green foliage during the growing season. Before the leaves fall, they turn rusty red. In winter the bare branches, flared trunks and exposed knees become much more prominent.

What it does here

The knees change the pond edge

The woody knees around Cypress Pond are especially easy to see. Together with the cypresses’ extensive root systems, they also make this shoreline difficult to plant. There is little open ground between them where new vegetation can easily take hold.

A different kind of pond

We are adding vegetation around all three of the park’s ponds, but Cypress Pond presents a different challenge. Its established cypresses already create a more wooded, shaded edge, while their roots and knees limit where additional plants can go.

How to know it

Feathery leaves

The soft, narrow leaves grow in two rows along slender twigs, giving the foliage a flat, feathery look.

Knees

Look around the base of trees growing beside the water for woody projections rising straight up from the roots. Bald Cypress growing on drier ground may produce few or no knees.

Flared base

Older trees develop a broad, strongly buttressed base that narrows quickly into the trunk.

Watch the tree disappear

A green Bald Cypress can pass for an evergreen in summer. In fall its foliage turns rusty red and drops, revealing a bare, conifer-shaped tree for winter.

How it grows

Why the knees?

Bald Cypress roots growing in wet ground can produce woody projections called knees. They have often been described as helping submerged roots take in oxygen, but experiments and observations have not settled the question. Other proposed roles include steadying the tree and storing carbohydrates. Their exact purpose remains one of the unresolved parts of Bald Cypress biology.

A conifer that goes bald

Bald Cypress is a deciduous conifer. Instead of keeping its needles through winter, it sheds the small leafy twigs each year and grows a fresh set. That annual leaf drop gives the tree its common name.

Wet doesn’t mean required

Bald Cypress thrives in saturated and seasonally flooded ground, which is why it is so closely tied to swamps and pond edges. It can also grow surprisingly well on ordinary, better-drained soil. Trees growing away from wet ground generally develop fewer or smaller knees.
