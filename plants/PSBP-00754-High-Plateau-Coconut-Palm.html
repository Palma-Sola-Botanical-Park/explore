At a glance

  • Grown, this palm passes for a coconut palm from across a lawn. It comes from a river valley 3,400 feet up on Madagascar's central plateau, where nights are cool and frost is possible, and no coconut could live.
  • Botanists first noticed it in photographs from a survey of the area, sent an expedition in 2004, and named it in 2007. It is known from that one valley.
  • The IUCN listed it as Vulnerable in 2012.
  • Ours is a young palm, still in strap leaves, in the row south of Cypress Pond. The coconut look is years away.

Cultural significance

Named for the person who noticed

The species name alfredii honours Alfred Razafindratsira, who spotted unfamiliar palms in photographs taken during other fieldwork on the plateau and made sure someone went to look. A new species can begin that way: not with an expedition, but with one person noticing that a familiar-looking plant does not quite fit.

Where it comes from

One valley on the Mania River

High Plateau Coconut Palm is known from one place: a valley near Manalazina in the central highlands of Madagascar, at about 3,400 feet. It grows along the sandy banks of streams feeding the Mania River, in strips of forest that run through open grassland. The dry season is long and the nights are cool, which is not coconut country.

Found in a photograph

The palms turned up in photographs from a survey of the district. An expedition in 2004 confirmed they were not either of the two Beccariophoenix species already known, and the species was described in 2007. The IUCN assessed it as Vulnerable in 2012. The genus has three species, all from Madagascar, and all of them close relatives of the coconut.

Where to find it here

Along Cypress Pond

High Plateau Coconut Palm grows in the row of unusual palms just south of our Plumeria collection, on the south side of Cypress Pond. Starting from the Red Cabbage Palm end of the row it is the twelfth palm in, between Madagascar Queen Palm and Bailey Palm. Look low: it is a young plant a couple of feet tall in a ring of mulch.

Go ahead and get closer

There is no formal path through this palm collection yet. You are welcome to walk across the open grass to see the palms and read their signs.

What it does here

A palm to come back to

This one asks for patience. It is part of a collection of unusual palms that a longtime volunteer planted here over many years, and it is among the youngest of them. For now the page has to describe a palm the visitor cannot see yet: a grey trunk, a crown of long arching fronds, the coconut silhouette that fooled the first people to photograph it. The seedling in the mulch is the start of that.

How to know it

On a young palm

At this age it is a tuft of long, narrow, undivided leaves, each a single strap, rising from a stub of stem in the mulch. Many feather palms start this way; the sign is the surest field mark for a few years yet.

Windows in the leaf

Hold a young leaf up to the sky and look for small clear patches in the blade, the windows that young plants of this species carry and lose as they grow.

What it will become

A grown palm has a solitary grey trunk ringed with leaf scars, no crownshaft, and a full crown of long feather fronds. The flower stalks come out below the leaves, not among them, and the fruit are small, flattened and dark, about an inch across, and nothing like a coconut.

How it grows

A river-valley palm in the open

At home it stands beside highland streams in full light, with water at its roots and a long dry season overhead. Here it has the open sky of the palm row; the mulch ring is doing the stream's job of keeping the root zone even.

Cooler than it looks

Its high, cool home gives it more tolerance of cold than a true coconut palm, which is why collectors wanted it. How much more depends on who you ask, so this page does not put a number on it. Male and female flowers grow on the same palm, so a single mature tree can set fruit by itself.
