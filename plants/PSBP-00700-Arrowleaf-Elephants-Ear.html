At a glance

  • Under the leaves is a food crop: malanga in Cuba, yautía in Puerto Rico, tannia in Trinidad.
  • In West Africa it is new cocoyam, to tell it from taro, the old cocoyam that got there first.
  • Raw, every part is packed with microscopic needle crystals that make the mouth burn. Cooking is what turns it into food.

Cultural significance

The crop below

The crop is the corm, a fat underground stem, and the smaller cormels that cluster around it. They are peeled and boiled, baked, mashed or fried, never eaten raw, and in Ghana the young leaves go into stews. A farmer can go on digging cormels for well over a year while the mother corm stays in the ground making more.

Into the cocoa country

Accounts differ on when it reached West Africa. Some put it in the slave-trade era; the best-documented arrival is 1843, when West Indian missionaries brought it to Akropong, in what is now Ghana. There its broad leaves shaded young cocoa seedlings, and it spread through the cocoa country with them. West Africa now grows most of the world's cocoyams.

Where it comes from

An old American crop

It is native to tropical America, probably northern South America, and was grown there long before Columbus. It has been planted for so long that nobody is sure where its wild range ends.

Yellow body, arrow leaf

Xanthosoma means yellow body, probably for the yellow flesh or stigmas of some species. Sagittifolium means arrow-leaved.

What it does here

The stem is underground

The corm is not a root. It is the plant's stem, swollen with starch and buried, carrying the buds that become new leaves and new cormels; roots grow down from it and leaves rise straight up. That is why there is no trunk to see, and why a cormel left in the ground becomes a new plant.

A flower few people see

Now and then it flowers, low among the leaf stalks: a spike wrapped in a hood nine to twelve inches long, green and swollen at the base, cream above. In Florida it seldom sets seed. Nobody seems to have watched what pollinates this species. A wild relative in Colombia warms its flower spikes a few degrees at dusk, gives off a sweet scent, and holds scarab beetles inside for a day, sending them off the next night dusted with pollen.

How to know it

Follow the stalk

Follow a leaf stalk up to the blade. On this plant it joins right at the notch between the two back lobes. On taro, which looks much the same, the stalk joins the underside a few inches in from the edge, like an umbrella handle.

An arrowhead on a long stalk

Each blade is shaped like an arrowhead, two to three feet long on a mature plant, on a stalk usually longer than the blade. There is no trunk: every leaf comes straight up from the ground.

Watch the rain

After a shower, water beads up on the leaves and rolls off, taking dust with it. Taro sheds water even better.

How it grows

Spreading from the base

New cormels and short underground runners push up new plants around the parent, so a clump widens year by year without needing seed. A freeze can kill the leaves, but the corm survives below ground and sends up new ones.

Beyond the garden

In Florida it has escaped onto stream banks, wet ditch edges and disturbed wetland margins. It is on Florida's invasive plant list as Category II, spreading in the wild but not yet reshaping natural communities (2025 list). Wild taro, its look-alike, is Category I.

Take care

Raw, it burns

Every part is packed with microscopic needles of calcium oxalate. Chewed raw, they pierce the mouth and throat, and an irritant carried on them makes it burn and swell. Sap from cut stems can irritate skin. Don't taste it, and keep pets from chewing it.
