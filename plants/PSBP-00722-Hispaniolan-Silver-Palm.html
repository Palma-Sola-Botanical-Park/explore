Hispaniolan Silver Palm

Coccothrinax argentea
On this page

At a glance

  • Green on top, silver underneath. The silver comes from a dense coat of tiny hairs on the underside. The long, narrow segments are flexible, not leathery.
  • The trunk wears a woven sleeve. It is made from the fibers of old leaf bases, which stay wrapped around the trunk.
  • The fruit goes dark. Small fruits ripen to purple or nearly black.
  • It comes from Hispaniola, the island shared by Haiti and the Dominican Republic, sitting just a tad southeast of Cuba.

Cultural significance

In part of the Dominican Republic this palm is called Guano Manso, or "mild" or "tame" guano, a name tied to its pliable leaves. They are easier to work into handicrafts than the leaves of a related palm that grows nearby there, Coccothrinax boschiana. If you know more of the story, we'd love to hear it.

Where it comes from

This palm comes from Hispaniola, the island shared by Haiti and the Dominican Republic. It grows wild in dry forest, pine forest and secondary woodland, on limestone or serpentine soils, and it can hang on in disturbed ground as well as forest.

Where to find it here

Step down from the Galleria steps into the open event field and look left, along the bank of the Big Pond. A few trees stand along the bank, and this palm is one of them. It is only about six feet tall for now, tucked a little behind the umbrella papyrus, near the Kukui tree and the Jacaranda.

If you like this one, there are dozens of other rare palms further back in the park. Keep exploring.

How to know it

Look under the fan

Turn a leaf over and the silver is a coat of tiny hairs with fringed edges. The woven sleeve on the trunk is a different material: old leaf bases, not hairs.

A look-alike

Silver undersides and a woven trunk sleeve also belong to the Florida silver palm, Coccothrinax argentata, whose name is very similar, so a quick glance will not settle which is which. One clue sits on the underside of the leaf: this palm has fine cross-veins linking the main veins, and the Florida silver palm does not. You need a hand lens to see it.

How it grows

A single trunk, mostly

This palm usually grows one slender trunk. Clustered stems have been documented, so a few trunks from one base can happen.

Fruit in stages

The small fruits ripen in shades of purple, reddish purple or nearly black. Inside each one, the seed has deep lobes running from its base toward the tip. The fruit does not all ripen at once, so a single cluster can show several stages together.
