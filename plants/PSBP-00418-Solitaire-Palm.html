Solitaire Palm

Ptychosperma elegans
On this page

At a glance

  • The name is the field mark. Nearly every other palm in its genus grows as a clump of trunks; this one grows a single slender trunk. Ours is three of them planted together.
  • The leaflet tips look bitten off rather than pointed. Once you have seen it on one frond you will see it on the whole crown.
  • Bright red fruit hang below the green crownshaft, and birds carry them off. UF/IFAS rates the palm Caution for Central and South Florida (August 2026), and the Florida Invasive Species Council lists it as Category II.
  • One of the most planted palms in South Florida, and often sold under the wrong name, Alexander Palm.

Where it comes from

Queensland

Solitaire Palm is native to coastal Queensland, Australia, from the tip of Cape York south to about Mission Beach. At home it is a palm of the rainforest understory, usually near rivers and streams where the ground stays moist but drains well.

Named in 1810

The Scottish botanist Robert Brown described it in 1810, and Carl Ludwig Blume moved it into Ptychosperma in 1843. The genus name means folded seed: crack a fruit and the seed inside has five deep grooves down its length. It was in European glasshouses by the 1820s, among the first Australian palms grown abroad, and by the years after the Second World War it was a standard Florida landscape palm.

Where to find it here

Big Pond, south walkway

Take the walkway along the south side of Big Pond. Counting from the bridge into the Rare Fruit Garden at the southeast corner, the Solitaire Palm is the seventh palm along, just past the Mountain Fishtail Palm: three slender trunks growing as one clump.

What it does here

The one that stands alone

Four Ptychosperma palms grow in the park, and this is the only one that makes a single trunk. Macarthur Palm at the Pavilion Gazebo, and Scheffer's Palm and the Ptychosperma Palm in the palm row south of Cypress Pond, all grow as clumps. Look at those, then come back here: the same green crownshaft, the same bitten-off leaflets, the same red fruit, and one trunk. Ours is three palms set close together, the way nurseries often pot them; none of the three will ever send up a sucker.

How to know it

Bitten-off tips

Each leaflet ends bluntly and unevenly, as if a piece had been torn away. Botanists call the shape praemorse, bitten off, and every palm in this genus has it.

A thin ringed trunk

The trunk is pale grey and very thin for its height, about four inches across, and ringed with the scars of dropped fronds. The leaf bases wrap the top of it into a smooth olive-green sleeve, the crownshaft, and the fronds spring from the top of that.

Red fruit

The flower spray comes out just below the crownshaft, and the fruit that follow are egg-shaped, about an inch long, and bright red when ripe. Each holds one grooved seed.

Not the Alexander Palm

Nurseries often sell this palm as Alexander Palm. That name belongs to a different Australian palm, Archontophoenix alexandrae, whose leaflets are silver underneath. Solitaire's are green.

How it grows

Fast and narrow

It grows fast, stays narrow, and takes sun or part shade, which is why it is planted in so many small Florida yards. It wants steady water; at home it stands beside streams under taller trees.

Birds spread it

Male and female flowers grow on the same spray, so a lone palm sets fruit. Birds eat the fruit and drop the seeds, and the palm now comes up on its own in natural areas in South Florida. That is why UF/IFAS asks growers in Central and South Florida to use caution with it.
