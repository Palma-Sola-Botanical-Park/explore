White Bird of Paradise

Strelitzia nicolai
On this page

At a glance

  • In its African home it is pollinated by sunbirds' feet. A bird lands on the blue "tongue" of the flower, its weight splits the petals, and sticky pollen is pressed onto its feet. There are no sunbirds here, so bees take the nectar and almost no seed forms.
  • Back home its seeds wear an orange fuzz colored by bilirubin, the pigment that turns a bruise yellow. It was the first bilirubin ever found in a plant, in 2009, and the color never fades.
  • Two royal houses on one plant: Strelitzia honors Queen Charlotte, George III's wife, and nicolai honors a Russian grand duke.
  • The wind tears the big leaves along their veins into strips, so an older plant looks like it is carrying giant feathers.

Cultural significance

Rope from the leaf stalks

In KwaZulu-Natal, on the east coast of South Africa, people stripped the dried leaf stalks and twisted them into cord for building huts and the fish traps at Kosi Bay.

Where it comes from

The South African coast

It grows wild in the coastal forests and dune thickets of eastern South Africa, north into Mozambique. It is a cousin of the Traveller's Palm from Madagascar, in a family of just three kinds of plant, and of the smaller orange Bird of Paradise.

Where to find it here

Bird of Paradise Island

It grows on Bird of Paradise Island, behind the office.

What it does here

A bird that spits out the seed

Back home a small bird called the Yellow-rumped Tinkerbird eats the orange fuzz, which is up to two-thirds oil by dry weight, and spits out the hard black seed. That is how the plant travels.

A flower like a bird's head

The flower stands in a dark, boat-shaped sheath, with white petals for the crest and a blue arrow for the beak. A stalk can carry several of them and run twenty inches long. It blooms through the year, most in spring and summer.

How to know it

A real trunk

Unlike a banana plant, it has a true gray trunk, ringed with the scars of old leaves, and it can stand twenty to thirty feet tall here. The leaves are gray-green paddles up to six feet long, arranged around each stem rather than in one flat fan like the Traveller's Palm.

How it grows

Slow to flower, quick to tear

A seedling takes several years to flower. It likes sun or light shade and takes salty wind, though wind shreds the leaves. It is cold-sensitive, and new plants come easiest by dividing a clump.
