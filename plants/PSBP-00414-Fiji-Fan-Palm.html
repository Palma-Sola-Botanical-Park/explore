Fiji Fan Palm

Pritchardia pacifica
On this page

At a glance

  • There is no wild population of this palm left anywhere on earth. It is listed as Extinct in the Wild, which means every one of them — including ours — grew from a tree that somebody planted.
  • In Fiji the leaves were worked into fans and umbrellas for the chief alone. A village kept one or two of these palms and no more, because that was as many as the chief needed.
  • The leaves barely split. Most fan palms break into long ribbons; these stay joined through most of the blade, so from a distance the crown reads as solid green panels.

Cultural significance

The chief's palm

The fans and sunshades made from these leaves were not general property — they were for the chief, and the Fijian word ai viu covers both the fan and the umbrella. In time the tree itself came to stand for rank, which is why a village grew only enough of them to serve one man.

Named for a consul, not a country

The genus honours William Thomas Pritchard, a nineteenth-century British consul in Fiji. That is probably the whole answer to why a palm most authorities place in Tonga has spent the last century being called the Fiji Fan Palm.

Where it comes from

Tonga, as far as anyone can tell

Most authorities settle on Tonga — the islands of Vava'u and 'Eua — and treat the palms in Fiji, Samoa, the Marquesas and Tokelau as very old human introductions rather than natural spread. The difficulty is that with no wild population left, there is nothing to check the answer against.

A Pacific family

Pritchardia grows nowhere but the Pacific — about twenty-nine species from Tonga and Fiji across to Hawaii, where the genus ran to at least twenty-two of its own and eight are now on the US Endangered list. This one palm is a door into a much bigger story.

What it does here

Keeping it alive is the point

With nothing left in the wild, a palm like this survives only in gardens and collections. Growing one is not decoration so much as custody.

The disease we watch for

This species is highly susceptible to lethal yellowing, and lethal yellowing is present in Manatee County. That makes this palm's continued good health a genuinely open question rather than a given, and worth noticing each time you pass it.

How to know it

Fans that stay whole

Look at where the blade splits. It divides only a quarter or a third of the way in, so the outer edge is fringed but the middle stays one continuous pleated sheet. Almost nothing else here does that.

Smooth stalks

Run your eye down the leaf stems: no teeth, no spines. A good many fan palms will take skin off you; this one will not.

Storm damage is normal

Those broad unsplit blades catch wind like sails, and a tropical storm can leave them shredded along the folds. A tattered crown after a blow is not a sick palm.

How it grows

Slow, and it wants shelter

Thirty to fifty feet in time on a single trunk, unhurried about getting there. It does best tucked out of the wind, and a hard cold snap will spot and discolour the leaves before new growth replaces them.
