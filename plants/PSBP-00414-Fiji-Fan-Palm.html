Fiji Fan Palm

Pritchardia pacifica

At a glance

  • The name is wrong, or at least misleading. The strongest evidence puts its home in Tonga, not Fiji.
  • The leaves barely split. Most fan palms break into long ribbons; these stay joined through most of the blade, so from a distance the crown reads as solid green panels.
  • Those leaves were valuable enough that in Fiji they were reserved for the chief — worked into fans and sunshades nobody else was entitled to carry.
  • Nobody has found a confirmed wild stand of it. Every one of these palms grew from a tree somebody planted.

Cultural significance

The chief's palm

In Fiji the broad leaves were traditionally worked into large fans and rain shelters associated with chiefly rank. The palm became part of the visual language of authority rather than just another useful plant.

Where to find it here

Behind the Butterfly Garden

On the back side of the Butterfly Garden, nearest the fence. Go past the Bottle Palm and turn right — it is the one with the enormous near-solid fans, and once you have seen those you will not mistake it for anything else back there.

Where it comes from

Tonga, as best anyone can tell

The strongest evidence points to Vava'u in Tonga. People carried the palm to Fiji, Samoa, Tokelau and the Marquesas a very long time ago, which is how a plant called the Fiji Fan Palm ends up with such a tangled origin story — and why a confirmed wild population has never been found.

What it does

A very different kind of fan palm

Most fan palms in the park split their leaves into long narrow segments. This one barely does, which gives the crown a broad, almost architectural look. Standing it near the others is the point — once you have seen the difference you cannot miss it.

How to know it

Fans that stay whole

Look at where the blade splits. It divides only a quarter or a third of the way in, so the outer edge is fringed but the middle stays one continuous pleated sheet.

Flip a low leaf over

The underside is noticeably paler and waxier than the glossy green top. It is the quickest confirmation there is, and it takes a second.

Smooth stalks

Follow the leaf stem toward the trunk: no teeth and no spines. That separates it immediately from some of the much pricklier fan palms.

Wind leaves its mark

Those broad, barely divided blades catch more wind than a finely split fan. After a hard blow the edges can tear along their natural folds.

How it grows

Slow, and decidedly tropical

It becomes a tall single-trunked palm in the end, but it is in no hurry about it. Cold will spot and burn the leaves, and a sheltered spot keeps those broad fans intact.
