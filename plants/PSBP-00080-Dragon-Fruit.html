Dragon Fruit

At a glance

  • Each enormous white flower opens for just one night. Some stay open into the following morning, then wilt.
  • The fruit that follows is dragon fruit: the red-skinned, black-seeded fruit now grown commercially around the world.
  • This isn't a desert cactus standing on its own. It is a climber, producing aerial roots along its stems that help it scramble up trees and other supports.
  • Our Rare Fruit area plant can carry many flower buds at once, but seeing the open flowers rewards an early visit.

Cultural significance

From climbing cactus to fruit crop

Dragon Fruit has been carried far beyond its native range and is now grown commercially throughout the tropics and subtropics. The flesh and its many tiny black seeds are eaten fresh, and the fruit has become this cactus's most familiar feature.

Where it comes from

From Central America

Dragon Fruit is native from Mexico through Guatemala, El Salvador and Honduras. Unlike the upright cacti familiar from deserts, it naturally grows as a climbing or epiphytic cactus in seasonally dry tropical country.

Where to find it here

Two very different plants

The easiest Dragon Fruit to see is in the Rare Fruit area along the east edge of the park, tended by the Manatee Rare Fruit Council. It climbs a trunk and its long stems cascade down from the top, and it can carry many flower buds at once. Near the office, another grows overhead in our Shade Garden, climbing through a Sabal Palm and a ficus. Look up or you'll probably miss it.

Come early for the flowers

Flowers come in flushes from late spring through summer. They open at night; arrive early enough the next morning and you may catch some still open before they collapse.

What it does here

A show with no audience

The flowering is the defining event of this plant, and most of it happens while the park is closed. The flowers, nearly a foot across, white and intensely fragrant, open after dark. There are bats here: a Scout troop built a bat house just past the bridge in the southeast corner of the park, not far from this garden. But Florida's bats hunt insects rather than nectar. A bat over a Dragon Fruit at night is after the moths the flowers bring in; the moths are the pollinators. By the time the gate opens most flowers have collapsed; the ones still open in the morning are the stragglers, and bees work those.

Fruit for whoever gets there first

When a flower is pollinated and a fruit sets, birds and small mammals eat it as readily as people do.

Not a butterfly plant

The flowers open after dark, and no Florida butterfly caterpillar is known to feed on the stems. This is a plant for the fruit and the spectacle, not the butterfly garden.

How to know it

Three-sided stems

The long, fleshy green stems usually have three broad, wavy-edged sides. The stems do the photosynthesis; there are no ordinary leaves.

Look for the roots

Small aerial roots emerge along the stems and help the cactus cling as it climbs. Our Shade Garden plant shows this habit particularly well as it scrambles overhead through other plants.

A flower you could almost miss

The white flowers are enormous, fragrant and unmistakable, but each lasts only one night. A wilted flower hanging from a stem in the morning may be all that's left of a bloom that opened the evening before.

Then look for the dragon

After successful pollination, the flower can develop into an oval red fruit covered with large green-tipped scales: the familiar dragon fruit.

How it grows

A cactus that climbs

Dragon Fruit produces long, sprawling stems rather than holding itself up like a tree. Aerial roots along those stems attach to trunks and other surfaces, letting the plant climb toward light. Our Shade Garden plant is doing exactly that.

One night to get pollinated

The flowers open after dark and last only one night. Moths and bats can pollinate them, but that doesn't mean they necessarily do the job here. Some flowers stay open into the morning and are visited by bees. Growers also commonly pollinate Dragon Fruit by hand to improve the fruit crop.

Cactus doesn't always mean desert

Dragon Fruit tolerates dry spells, but it is a tropical climbing cactus, not a desert specialist. It grows well in Florida's warm climate, needs good drainage, and can take some shade.
