Devil's Backbone

Euphorbia tithymaloides
On this page

At a glance

  • The stems zigzag, kinking hard at every joint. That is not decoration — the thickened stems are where the plant keeps its water.
  • When it flowers, the red slippers are not petals. They are modified leaves, shaped to fit a hummingbird's head and load it with pollen. Its close cousin does the same trick in red at Christmas: this is a poinsettia relative.
  • One of its names is Redbird Cactus. It is not a cactus and never has been.
  • The white sap is caustic. It burns skin and can do real damage to an eye. Do not break a stem.

Cultural significance

In a garden before it had a name

It was growing in a garden in Amsterdam before 1688 — more than sixty years before Linnaeus got round to describing it formally. European glasshouses had it, traded it and grew it long before science filed it anywhere.

Named for whatever struck you first

Slipper Plant, Zigzag Plant, Jacob's Ladder, Redbird Cactus, Japanese Poinsettia. Almost nobody agrees what to call it, because everyone names it after the part they noticed — the stems, the bracts, the shape of the flower.

Where to find it here

One tier up, easy to miss

Go back into the cactus and succulent garden toward Cypress Pond and look one tier up. It does not announce itself — it is a modest, upright, tangly thing among showier neighbours, and you have to be looking.

Where it comes from

Tropical America, and once Florida

It ranges from Mexico and the Caribbean down into northern South America, and a Florida subspecies was described from a Miami plant collected in 1904. Botanists now think the genuinely wild Florida populations may be largely gone. What grows in Florida gardens today, ours included, is cultivated stock rather than anything collected from the wild here.

What it does

Kept in the clear

The sea fig spreads across this part of the garden and will grow straight over whatever is beside it. Volunteers cut it back through the year so the smaller plants on the tier keep their light and their room. When the sea fig has had a good run, this one can disappear under it entirely.

It changes colour with the season

Ours is a variegated selection rather than the plain green form. Through the warm months the leaves are green with cream margins; in the cool of winter the same leaves flush pink right through. It is worth seeing twice a year.

How to know it

Follow a stem

Nothing else here does this. Each stem runs straight for an inch or two, then kinks sharply the other way at the joint, over and over, so the whole plant is built from zigzags.

Leaves along the kinks

Small pointed leaves sit at the joints, green down the middle with pale cream edges on the form we grow.

If a stem is broken

A thick white latex runs out immediately. That is the giveaway for the whole spurge family, and on this plant it is the part to respect.

How it grows

Upright, slow, and thrifty

Two to six feet, multi-stemmed, in no hurry. In a dry spell it switches its breathing to the night shift, opening its pores after dark to take in carbon dioxide and losing far less water than it would in the middle of a Florida afternoon.

Take care

The sap is the danger

This is not a plant to handle. The milky sap is caustic: on skin it can blister and burn, and in an eye it can cause severe inflammation and lasting damage. It is toxic to dogs, cats and horses, and a mouthful burns immediately. Look at it, photograph it, and leave the stems alone.
