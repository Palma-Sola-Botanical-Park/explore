At a glance

  • Our tree stands outside the fence at 17th Avenue and 99th Street—part of the planting visitors are welcome to explore.
  • In the British Virgin Islands it is called White Cedar and is the Territorial Tree.
  • A single leaf may divide into several leaflets, spread like fingers from one point.

Cultural significance

White Cedar helped build the traditional Virgin Islands sloop. Its timber connects this flowering street tree to the boats that carried people and goods among the islands. “Cedar” is a local common name; this is a flowering tree, not a conifer.

Where it comes from

Pink Trumpet Tree is native to the Caribbean. It grows in dry woodland and disturbed ground as well as gardens, and established trees tolerate the heat and dry spells of exposed sites.

Where to find it here

Look at the corner of 17th Avenue and 99th Street, just outside the fence. We hope you will enjoy the plants along the outside of the grounds, too.

What it does here

A flowering branch is worth a closer look even when the whole tree is not covered in pink. The flared, ruffled mouth leads into a narrow tube. Some blossoms are very pale, with a warmer-colored throat.

How to know it

Count from one stalk

When flowers are absent, follow one leaf stalk to its end. Several leaflets can radiate from the same point, rather than attaching separately along a twig. Leaflet number varies—one reason the botanical name means “different-leaved.”

After flowering, look for long, narrow seed capsules. They turn brown and split open, releasing papery, winged seeds.

How it grows

Young trees have a narrower outline; older ones broaden into a spreading crown. A brief leaf drop can make a flush of flowers more visible, though flowers and leaves can also share the branches. Seed-grown trees do not all bloom on precisely the same schedule.
