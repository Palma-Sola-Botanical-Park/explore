Reed Palm

Chamaedorea seifrizii

At a glance

  • Each feathery leaf starts out as one unbroken blade. As the leaf grows, the blade comes apart along built-in lines into the narrow leaflets that line both sides of the stalk.
  • Slender green canes, each ringed like bamboo, rise together from one base, so one plant becomes a clump. The bamboo look is why this palm is also called Bamboo Palm.
  • In the 1980s NASA scientists sealed this palm in small airtight boxes with formaldehyde and other polluting gases to see how well it could clean the air. The palm did clear some of the gas, and that test is a big part of why it is sold as an air-cleaning houseplant. But a real room has air flowing in and out far faster than a sealed box, so one potted palm does little there.

Cultural significance

A name from Yucatán

Botanists named this palm in 1938 for William Seifriz, who collected it near Chichén Itzá, the Maya ruins in Yucatán. His name is inside the Latin one: Chamaedorea seifrizii.

Where it comes from

Under the forest roof

It grows in the forests of southern Mexico, Belize, Guatemala and Honduras, often on limestone soil, in the shade of taller trees. Moved into a garden, it still wants that shade: strong afternoon sun scorches the leaves and drying wind browns their edges.

Where to find it here

Beyond the Royal Palms

Reed Palm grows in the shady northeast corner of the park, near the Royal Palm area. Standing at the pavilion and looking toward the Butterfly Garden, go to your left, beyond the Royal Palms.

What it does

A young clump

The clump here is still young, with slim canes and leaves low enough to look at one by one. A grown clump reaches four to twelve feet.

An indoor plant outdoors

Most people meet Reed Palm in a pot indoors. Outside, it is a landscape plant for Florida's warmest zones. An unusually cold winter can burn or strip its leaves, and new growth usually returns from the base.

How to know it

Look at one cane

A cane is usually under an inch thick. Look for pale rings where old leaves were attached, and for papery scraps of old leaf bases still clinging to it.

Not the Parlor Palm

The Parlor Palm in most pots is a close relative that usually grows a single slender stem. Reed Palm sends up many canes from one base.

How it grows

Two kinds of plants

Male and female flowers grow on separate plants. A male plant can flower all its life without ever carrying fruit. On a female plant, the flower stalk turns orange, and the small beads of fruit ripen from green through orange or red to black.

Shade and shelter

It does best in part to full shade, in soil that drains. Drying wind browns the leaf edges, and it handles salty air poorly.

Take care

Leave the fruit alone

The ripe fruit can irritate skin. Avoid handling it, and don't eat it.
