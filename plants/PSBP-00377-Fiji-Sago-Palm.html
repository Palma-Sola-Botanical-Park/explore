At a glance

  • This palm flowers once in its life. It grows for decades, sends up one huge flower spike, sets seed, and the whole palm dies. It never makes suckers to carry on.
  • How long that takes is barely documented: the only confirmed figure is 24 years, for two palms.
  • It is Endangered. Its home is the swampy lowlands of Fiji, where drainage and cutting have reduced it to scattered stands.
  • Ours grows in the row of unusual palms south of Cypress Pond, right after Stumpy Palm. Mind the spines on the leaf stalks; they stay sharp on fallen fronds.

Cultural significance

A sago palm Fijians never used for sago

The name promises a starch crop, and this palm never was one. There is no record of Fijians eating sago, even in famine; the Fijian language has no word for it, while Rotuman, spoken on an island with a different sago palm, has two dozen. When the botanist Berthold Seemann sailed up the Navua River in 1860 he was struck by how many of these palms lined the banks, and by nobody using them. The uses came later: the long leaves are cut for thatch, much of it for resort roofs, and the growing tip is eaten as palm heart, which kills the palm. Both are now the main threats to it, and Fiji's recovery plan asks for sustainable harvest rather than a ban.

Where it comes from

Fiji's wet lowlands

Fiji Sago Palm is native to Fiji, with a record from the Wallis and Futuna Islands to the northeast. It grows in freshwater swamp forest and along rivers in the lowlands, mostly on the largest island, Viti Levu, and on Ovalau.

A shrunken range

Pollen preserved in swamp sediments shows the palm once grew across far more of Fiji's lowlands than it does now. Draining swamps for farms and resorts removed most of that ground, and cutting for thatch and palm heart pressed on what was left. The IUCN lists it as Endangered.

Where to find it here

Along Cypress Pond

Fiji Sago Palm grows in the row of unusual palms just south of our Plumeria collection, on the south side of Cypress Pond. Starting from the Red Cabbage Palm end of the row it is the fifth palm in: Red Cabbage Palm, Red Latan Palm, Lala Palm, Stumpy Palm, then Fiji Sago Palm.

Go ahead and get closer

There is no formal path through this palm collection yet. You are welcome to walk across the open grass to see the palms and read their signs. Give this one's leaf stalks a wide berth.

What it does here

One flowering, and we would like to see it

Most palms in this row will flower year after year. This one gets a single turn, and nobody can say when: it is part of a collection of unusual palms that a longtime volunteer planted here over many years, and its age is not recorded. If a flower spike ever rises above the crown, that is the last chapter of this palm's life, and we intend to photograph every step of it.

How to know it

Mind the spines

Look where the leaf stalks leave the base of the plant. Rows of fine, dark needle-like spines fan out in curved bands around each stalk, and smaller ones run along the midrib of the frond. Fallen fronds keep them.

No crownshaft

The fronds attach straight to the plant with no smooth green sleeve, the crownshaft, that many feather palms have. Old leaf bases hang on, so as it grows the upper trunk looks rough rather than ringed.

Step back

A solitary, coarse feather palm with very long fronds that rise steeply and then arch over. It is not a cycad: the nursery "sago palm" with stiff dark leaves in a rosette is a different plant altogether, and not a palm.

How it grows

Built for wet ground

This is a swamp palm. In Fiji it stands in ground that never dries, and it wants steady moisture here too; a long dry spell shows in the fronds. It is tender to frost, and because it has a single growing tip, damage there is damage to the whole palm.

Decades for one flowering

The palm builds one large body for one reproductive event. When it is ready, a branched flower spike rises above the leaves; scaly fruit follow over about a year, and then the palm dies. The only documented time from planting to flowering is 24 years, for two palms in Fiji, and some surely take longer.
