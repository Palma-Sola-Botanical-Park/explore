King's Mantle

Thunbergia erecta

At a glance

  • A Thunbergia that does not climb. Nearly all of its hundred-odd relatives are vines that will take a fence or a tree if you let them; this one stays a rounded shrub, and that is the whole reason it gets planted.
  • The flowers are a true deep purple with a yellow throat, and it produces them more or less all year when it is warm.
  • It grows beside the Queen’s Wreath by the office, a bigger, showier vine that tends to steal the scene. Look low.
  • The genus is named for Carl Peter Thunberg, a student of Linnaeus who botanised Japan, South Africa and Sri Lanka in the 1700s.

Where it comes from

Tropical Africa

Native across tropical Africa from the west coast to Tanzania, in forest edges and thickets. Its climbing cousins, the Black-eyed Susan Vine and the Sky Vine, are the ones most gardeners know.

Where to find it here

By the office, at the edge of the path, next to the Queen’s Wreath. The Wreath is taller and lusher and pulls your eye up; the King’s Mantle is the mound of small glossy leaves at your knee with purple flowers dotted through it.

What it does here

The King, outshone by the Queen

It is easy to walk straight past. The Queen’s Wreath beside it is bigger, floppier and covered in lavender when it blooms, and it wins the glance. Give the shrub a second look. Its flowers are a darker, richer purple than anything on the vine, and they are there most days of the year, which the Wreath cannot say. Butterflies and bees work them steadily.

How to know it

Purple trumpet, yellow throat

Each flower is a flared trumpet about two inches across, a saturated violet that holds its colour in full sun, with a small yellow throat at the centre. They come singly from the leaf joints.

Small glossy leaves, a mound of them

Opposite pairs of small, dark, slightly wavy leaves on a rounded shrub four to six feet tall. It arches; it does not twine.

How it grows

Fast, and it wants sun to flower well; in shade you get leaves. A cold snap browns it, but it comes back. Cut it lightly and it thickens into an informal hedge; shear it hard and you lose the flowers for a while.
