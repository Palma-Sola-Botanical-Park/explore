Purple Heart

Tradescantia pallida
On this page

At a glance

  • The purple is a pigment the plant makes to shield its leaves from too much light. The more sun a leaf gets, the more it makes; in shade the leaves go greener.
  • Each flower lasts a single day. It opens in the morning, pale pink, between two boat-shaped leaves.
  • In the wild it grows on the Gulf coast of Mexico, on limestone outcrops and at the edge of dry forest.
  • A broken piece roots where it lands. Stems send out roots at their joints, which is how one plant becomes a mat.

Where it comes from

Mexico's Gulf coast

It is native to the Gulf coast of Mexico, from Tamaulipas down to the Yucatan, and it is a spiderwort. Its species name, pallida, means pale. The first plant described, collected in Tamaulipas in 1907, had grayish leaves; the deep purple is what gardeners favor.

Where to find it here

Grows as a groundcover in a few spots around the driveway, tucked between showier plants that take the spotlight.

What it does

Fills a bed

Stems that lie on the ground put down roots at each joint, so a few pieces knit quickly into a solid mat.

How to know it

Purple blades, small pink flowers

The leaves are long, narrow and fleshy, deep purple, and clasp the stem at the joints. The flowers are small, with three petals, pale pink to lavender, tucked in a pair of boat-shaped leaves at the stem tips. They come most from midsummer into fall. A plant stays about eight inches tall and spreads a foot and a half.

How it grows

Sun for color, frost for a haircut

Full sun gives the deepest purple and shade washes it out toward green. Frost can kill the tops, but the roots survive and it comes back quickly in spring.

Take care

Sap can sting

The juice can sting some people's skin. Some gardeners get an angry red rash within moments, so wear gloves when cutting it back.
