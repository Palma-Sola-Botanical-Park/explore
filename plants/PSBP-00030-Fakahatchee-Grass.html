Fakahatchee Grass

Tripsacum dactyloides
On this page
At a glanceWhere it comes fromWhere to find it hereWhat it does hereHow to know itHow it grows

At a glance

  • Fakahatchee Grass is a Florida-native perennial grass also called eastern gamagrass.
  • It is a close botanical relative of corn and teosinte. Its segmented flowering and seed spikes make that relationship easier to see than most grass-family connections.
  • The plant forms large fountain-like clumps with leaves several feet long.
  • UF/IFAS recommends it for stormwater-pond banks because its dense root system helps stabilize soil.

Where it comes from

Its native range spans the central and eastern United States through the Caribbean and Central America into northern South America.

Where to find it here

Cypress Pond

Fakahatchee Grass has grown at Cypress Pond as long as anyone at the park can remember — not something planted on purpose, just always there.

What it does here

A plant the park doesn't enjoy managing

Fakahatchee Grass gets thick and clumped fast, and when it overgrows a spot — as it did around Cypress Pond — machinery is needed to remove it. The park record notes that an established clump once resisted the John Deere Gator entirely and had to be pulled with the full tractor. Nobody here is a fan of how large and unruly the clumps become.

Native, but capable of spreading here

The park tracks this plant in its "watch" category because it has spread beyond intended planting areas on the grounds. That is a local management issue, not a claim that this Florida native is an invasive exotic.

How to know it

A very large grass clump

The leaves are broader and heavier than those of many ornamental grasses and arch outward from a dense base.

Find the segmented spikes

Flowering stems carry thick, jointed spikes rather than airy plumes.

Male above, female below

Different portions of the spike bear the pollen-producing and seed-producing flowers.

The corn resemblance is structural

The stacked segments can suggest a miniature primitive ear, reflecting the plant's close relationship to Zea rather than making it a kind of corn.

How it grows

Wet ground suits it, but it is not fragile

UF/IFAS describes Fakahatchee Grass as preferring moist sites while still tolerating some drought and flooding.

Short rhizomes build a stubborn clump

The plant expands gradually from the base rather than racing across the ground on long runners.

Useful at pond edges

UF/IFAS stormwater guidance specifically recommends it for bank erosion control.

Butterfly connection

UF/IFAS butterfly guidance lists eastern gamagrass as a larval host for the Byssus Skipper.

Also known as
