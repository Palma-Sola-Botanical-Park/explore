At a glance

  • American Elm once formed the arching canopies over streets all across North America.
  • Dutch elm disease, arriving in the twentieth century, killed most of those trees.
  • The species survived, and breeding programs keep selecting elms that tolerate the disease.
  • It is a Florida native, at the southern end of a range that reaches Canada.

Cultural significance

The lost elm street

Towns planted elms because their tall trunks and arching limbs could meet over a road. Dutch elm disease erased many of those avenues within a few decades, and "Elm Street" outlived its elms.

Where it comes from

Half a continent

Native across eastern and central North America, from Canada south into Florida and northern Mexico. Florida's elms were once given their own variety name, floridana; most botanists now fold them back into the species.

Where to find it here

The south end of the park

Walk south with the Big Pond on your left, past the Traveller's Palm, and you reach the southernmost part of the park. Three elms stand there with a bench beneath them, a good place to rest in the shade after the walk. From the bench, left takes you into the Rare Fruit Garden; right takes you out to the open field of unusual palms.

What to look for

Spreading trees whose trunks split low into several rising limbs.

What it does here

Florida natives with a national story

The park's elms are Florida trees first, but they belong to one of the best-known cases of an introduced disease reshaping forests and cities. The species is listed as Endangered on the IUCN Red List because of the disease, even though it is still common in parts of its range.

How to know it

Uneven leaf bases

The two sides of an elm leaf meet the stalk at different heights. Hold one up and look at the base.

Double-toothed edges

Coarse teeth along the margin, each carrying smaller teeth.

Flat winged seeds

In spring the seeds are papery discs, not nuts or berries.

How it grows

A tree of wet ground

Naturally at home on floodplains and bottomlands, and happy in ordinary garden soil.

The disease is part of its life

Dutch elm disease still kills susceptible trees in North America. Individual variation and resistant selections are what restoration work builds on.
