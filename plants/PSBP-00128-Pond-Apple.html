At a glance

  • This is the only plant the caterpillar of the giant sphinx moth will eat, and that moth is the largest of its kind in North America.
  • In the wild this tree stands in water, along pond edges, sloughs and the upstream fringe of the mangroves. Ours stands on mown grass.
  • The fruit is edible in the strict sense and disappointing in every other way. Mealy and bland, and far better appreciated by a raccoon.
  • The seeds, bark and leaves are a different matter. Those are genuinely toxic and should never be eaten.

Cultural significance

The Custard Apple country

Before the drainage projects of the early 1900s, a dense belt of pond apple forest ran along the southern and southeastern shore of Lake Okeechobee — roughly thirty-two thousand acres of it. People called it the Custard Apple country. It was cleared for what became the Everglades Agricultural Area, because the black muck underneath was some of the richest soil in the state, and almost none of it survives. The largest stand left is down in Everglades National Park.

The root under better fruit

Growers graft soursop and sugar apple onto pond apple roots. Those cousins make the fruit people actually want to eat but will not tolerate wet ground; this one will. In a collection like ours it is worth knowing that the plain tree is often the one holding up the interesting one.

Where it comes from

A Florida swamp tree

It is native here, and also through the Caribbean, tropical America and West Africa. In Florida it is one of the characteristic trees of freshwater swamp, pond margin and the brackish upstream edge of mangrove country — ground that is wet most of the year.

Where to find it here

In the Rare Fruit area

Look for ours behind the Black Sapote, near the restrooms at the start of the path. As of September 2026 it has no sign, and it sits back toward the fence. Feel free to walk back there, even though there is no clear path to it. Ours is quite mature, and you can walk under the canopy with ease.

What it does here

A swamp tree on dry ground

Everything about this tree is built for standing in water, and ours is not standing in any. It does not seem to mind. What it loses is the trick it is named for: in a swamp the fruit drops, floats off and starts a new tree downstream. Here it drops into the grass and stays put.

Feeding something large

If the giant sphinx moth is breeding anywhere on the property, this is the tree it needs — the caterpillars eat the leaves of this species and very little else. If you are ever here at dusk and something very large goes past, we would like to hear about it.

How to know it

Plain glossy leaves

Simple oval leaves, untoothed and glossy, held fairly flat. Nothing fancy, which is itself a useful clue among the fussier fruit trees nearby.

Green fruit, heavy and smooth

The fruit is about the size of a small mango, green ripening to yellow, with a smooth skin rather than the knobbly one you see on its cousins.

A flared foot

The base of the trunk widens out where it meets the ground. In a swamp that buttress is what keeps the tree upright in soft mud.

How it grows

Low and wide

Thirty to forty-five feet at most, and often a good deal less. It branches low and runs to several trunks rather than one, though the canopy lifts enough to walk in under it.

Built for wet feet

It will tolerate standing water and even a degree of salt that would finish most fruit trees, which is why it holds the ground where the swamp meets the mangroves.

Take care

Pulp only, and not much of that

The ripe pulp will not hurt you but is not worth the trouble. The seeds, bark and leaves contain toxins and must not be eaten. Keep a dog from chewing fallen fruit.
