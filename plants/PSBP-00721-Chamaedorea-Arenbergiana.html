At a glance

  • The fruit clusters look strikingly like small ears of corn — green at first, ripening to black — and they form right at the trunk, below the leaves.
  • It has no common name. Older books call it Chamaedorea latifolia, which means broad-leaved, and that is about as close as anyone has come to naming it.
  • It is heading for roughly twelve feet on a trunk not much thicker than your thumb.
  • Male and female flowers are on separate plants, so one lonely palm will never make those black fruit clusters by itself.

Where it comes from

It comes from wet tropical forests from southern Mexico through Central America. It grows beneath taller rainforest trees, where the light is filtered and the ground stays moist.

Where to find it here

The shade end of the palm collection

Past the Royal Palms, in the stretch where the park puts collection palms that cannot take full sun. It stands between the Calapparia Palm and the Gap Palm.

Not much to look at yet

Right now it is a knee-high spray of broad leaves coming straight out of the ground, with no trunk at all. Everything the books describe is still ahead of it — the thin ringed cane, the corn-cob fruit. Worth checking on in a few years.

What it does here

The one that breaks the pattern

The Cat Palm stands five places along this same row — a Chamaedorea of the usual kind, all narrow strap-like leaflets. Seeing the two within a minute of each other is the point: in a collection, being the exception is the job.

Planted where the sun cannot reach it

It grew up under rainforest canopy and it is put here for the same reason, in the shaded end of the collection. Its placement and its biology explain each other.

How to know it

Wide leaflets

Most Chamaedorea palms look fine-textured. This one doesn't. The leaflets are broad, almost paddle-shaped, and taper into long pointed tips — noticeably different from the narrow, strap-like leaflets of many of its relatives.

A bamboo-like trunk

In time the trunk stays remarkably thin and dark green, with pale rings marking where old leaves once attached — almost a bamboo cane from a few steps back. Ours has not started one yet.

The giveaway, when it fruits

The black fruits are packed so tightly around a short, upright spike — emerging below the leaves — that the whole cluster really does look like a little ear of corn. The fruits are squeezed close enough that they press into slightly angular shapes.

How it grows

This is an understory palm. Filtered light and steady moisture suit it much better than harsh, exposed conditions. Too much direct sun can bleach or scorch the leaves.

It grows from one stem rather than spreading into a clump. The neat rings on that skinny trunk become more obvious as the palm gains height.
