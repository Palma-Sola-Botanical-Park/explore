At a glance

  • A mahogany can carry mahogany mistletoe, which is itself endangered in Florida. The tree matters to a second rare species as well as to itself.
  • This is the original mahogany — the tree that gave the whole category its name. The Honduras mahogany that replaced it in the timber trade is a different species.
  • It was cut so hard for so long that Florida now lists it as Threatened. Its natural range here is only the Keys and the far southern counties, so ours is a long way north of home.
  • Look at the bark: dark grey and scaly, flaking off in patches to show red underneath.

Cultural significance

The wood of Georgian England

Thomas Chippendale worked almost entirely in this one species, and Hepplewhite and Sheraton followed him. The antique mahogany furniture in grand houses across Britain and America is very largely this tree. It was cut in the Caribbean by enslaved people.

The name outlived the supply

The trade took so much of it that commercial mahogany moved on to a different tree, the Honduras mahogany, which now carries the name in most of the world. This is the one the word was coined for. It is the national tree of the Dominican Republic.

Where it comes from

The Keys, and the islands beyond

Native to the West Indies and the southern tip of Florida — hammocks in Miami-Dade and Monroe Counties and down through the Keys, and nowhere else in the United States. Florida protects it under the Preservation of Native Flora Act.

Where to find it here

The northwest corner of Cypress Pond

Walk the crushed shell path with the cactus and succulent garden on your right. The mahogany stands in front of the shady cypress pond, just before you reach the cycad area — a big green tree on the northwest corner of the water, easy to pick out from across the pond.

What it does here

Further north than it grows wild

This tree belongs three hundred miles south of here. Growing one this far up the peninsula is not a hardship for it, but it is not something the species does on its own — every mahogany in Florida outside the far south is there because somebody put it there, and with a Threatened species that is worth something.

Winter is when it gives

The capsules ripen and split open while still on the tree through the winter, and the seeds come down spinning on a single papery wing. Squirrels work the ground under it afterwards. In spring the small greenish flowers bring in bees and small butterflies.

How to know it

Leaves in pairs along a stalk

Each leaf is divided into several glossy leaflets set in pairs along a central stem, so what looks like a small branch of leaves is in fact one leaf.

Red under the grey

The bark darkens and roughens with age and then flakes away in scales, leaving reddish patches where it has come off. On an older trunk that mottling is the give-away.

Capsules that stand up

The seed pods are large, woody and held upright above the foliage rather than hanging. They split into sections on the tree, which is not something most trees around here do.

Worth reporting

If you ever see mistletoe up in the crown, tell us. Mahogany mistletoe is endangered in Florida, it grows on this tree and very little else, and nobody has recorded it here.

How it grows

Fast, then long-lived

It puts on height quickly and then settles into a dense dome, forty to eighty feet in time and as much across. A well-sited one lives past a hundred years.

Takes the coast well

It handles a wide range of soils and shrugs off salt spray, which is part of why it suits a Gulf-coast garden so much better than its rarity here would suggest.
