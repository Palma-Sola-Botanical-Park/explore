At a glance

  • Mahogany can host another rare Florida plant: the endangered mahogany mistletoe.
  • This is West Indian mahogany, the Caribbean tree behind the great eighteenth-century furniture boom.
  • It was heavily logged for its timber, and Florida now lists it as Threatened. Its wild range in the United States is the far south of the state and nowhere else, so ours is a long way north of home.
  • Look at the bark: dark grey and scaly, flaking off in patches to show red underneath.

Cultural significance

Why cabinetmakers wanted it

The wood is strong but workable, holds its shape instead of warping, carves cleanly and polishes to a deep reddish sheen. That combination is rarer than it sounds, and it is the reason mahogany became the signature wood of Chippendale-era furniture in Britain and America.

What it cost

Much of the early Caribbean mahogany used in that furniture came from this species, cut and shipped through a colonial trade built on enslaved labour.

Then the supply ran down

West Indian mahogany dominated the early trade until the big trees were gone. Big-leaf mahogany from mainland tropical America took over as the commercial source, and carries the name in most of the world today. It is the national tree of the Dominican Republic.

Where it comes from

South Florida and the Caribbean

In the United States this is a South Florida tree — Miami-Dade, Monroe County and the Keys — growing in hammocks on limestone close to sea level, and found wild nowhere else in the country. Its range carries on through the West Indies.

Protected here

Florida lists it as Threatened under the Preservation of Native Flora Act.

Where to find it here

The northwest corner of Cypress Pond

Walk the crushed shell path with the cactus and succulent garden on your right. The mahogany stands in front of the shady cypress pond, just before you reach the cycad area — a big green tree on the northwest corner of the water, easy to pick out from across the pond.

What it does here

Planted, not wild

Around Bradenton a mahogany is a planted tree rather than part of the species' natural Florida range, which stops a few hundred miles south of us. Ours is one of a large cultivated population growing well outside a very small wild one.

Winter is when it gives

The capsules ripen and split open while still on the tree through the winter, and the seeds come down spinning on a single papery wing. Squirrels and small rodents take them from the ground. In spring the small greenish flowers bring in bees and small butterflies.

How to know it

Leaves in pairs along a stalk

Each leaf is divided into several glossy leaflets set in pairs along a central stem, so what looks like a small branch of leaves is in fact one leaf.

Red under the grey

The bark darkens and roughens with age and then flakes away in scales, leaving reddish patches where it has come off. On an older trunk that mottling is the give-away.

Big woody capsules

In winter, look for brown egg-shaped pods up to five inches long, hanging from slender stalks. They split open while still on the tree, peeling back into five sections to let the winged seeds go.

Worth reporting

If you ever see mistletoe in the crown, tell us. Endangered mahogany mistletoe can grow on West Indian mahogany, and we have not recorded it here.

How it grows

A real canopy tree

Given room it makes forty to eighty feet with a broad, dense, rounded crown and lives a long time. This is a tree to plant where nothing will need the space back.

Coastal, within limits

It handles drought and poor soil once established, and stands up to wind better than most — it has a reputation for coming through hurricanes. It will take salty air, but direct salt spray is another matter.
