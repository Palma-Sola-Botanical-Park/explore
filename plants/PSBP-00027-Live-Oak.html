At a glance

  • A big Live Oak can feed more than five hundred kinds of caterpillar, more than almost any other tree in North America. That matters more than it sounds: caterpillars are what most songbirds feed their chicks.
  • A grown one spreads wider than it stands tall, often far wider, with heavy limbs running nearly level.
  • It is never bare, but it does not keep its leaves. The old ones let go in spring just as the new ones push out.
  • We have them right across the park — one big one shading the parking lot as you arrive, and smaller ones along the pond behind the Galleria.

Cultural significance

Why the navy wanted it

Live Oak timber is dense and heavy, and the grain runs interlocked rather than straight, which makes it very hard to split. Shipwrights also wanted the shape: a Live Oak throws great curving limbs, and those curves could be cut into ship frames that a straight-growing tree could never supply. That is the wood the cannonballs came off.

How large they get

The Angel Oak in South Carolina, the same species, carries branches that run one hundred and eighty-seven feet from tip to tip and is reckoned to be four or five centuries old. Ours are a long way from that, but it is the right thing to picture.

Where it comes from

The southeastern coastal plain

Native from Virginia south through the whole of Florida and west into Texas and Oklahoma. It belongs to sandy coastal ground but will take almost anywhere inland that drains well.

Where to find it here

One meets you in the car park

A large Live Oak stands between the Galleria and the office, set among ferns, reaching a long way out over the parking lot. On a hot day it is the first shade you walk into after getting out of the car.

And more along the pond

A line of smaller ones follows the pond in the field behind the Galleria, the same water the Kukui stands beside.

What it does

The tree that takes the heat off

The parking lot oak is doing a job nothing else on that side of the park can do. It reaches out far enough that the walk in from the car happens in shade, which in July is the difference between arriving comfortable and arriving wilted.

More an ecosystem than a tree

A mature Live Oak is the most productive thing in the collection by a wide margin. Caterpillars by the hundred feed the birds raising young; acorns feed squirrels, jays and ducks; the limbs carry Spanish moss, bromeliads and lichens that amount to a second garden growing on the first.

How to know it

Small leaves, not lobed

Nothing like the deep-cut oak leaf people expect. These are small, oval, thick and leathery, dark and glossy on top and pale beneath, with the edges mostly smooth.

Limbs that go sideways

The trunk divides low into a few very heavy limbs that head outward rather than up. On an old open-grown tree they can dip and touch the ground before rising again.

Worth catching in spring

Late winter into spring is when the swap happens: the old leaves come down and the new ones come out almost together. For a week or two there is leaf litter under a tree that never actually went bare.

How it grows

Fast, then patient

Quick while young and steadily slower with age, working toward forty to eighty feet tall and anything from sixty to a hundred and twenty feet across. The width is the point; this is a tree that needs room more than it needs height.

Take care

Acorns need work

Acorns are genuinely food, and were an important one, but only after the tannins are leached or roasted out. Raw and in quantity they will upset a stomach, human or dog. A few off the ground will not hurt anyone; a pocketful is a bad idea.
