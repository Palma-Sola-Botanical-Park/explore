Gray Nickernut

Guilandina bonduc

At a glance

  • The gray seeds float. In one Florida test two were still bobbing in seawater after 32 years.
  • It is the favorite caterpillar food of the endangered Miami blue, a butterfly that once flew from Tampa Bay south.
  • Nicker is an old word for a clay marble, borrowed from the Dutch knikker. The seeds have been marbles in Hawaii and Tonga.
  • In the Hebrides, a washed-up nickernut set in silver was a childbirth charm, pressed into the mother's hand.

Cultural significance

Rooster's spur

In Tonga the plant is talatala 'amoa, the rooster's spur, for its prickles. The seeds are used as marbles and strung into leis, and the thorny stems were once tied to long poles to snag fruit bats.

Kidney of Mary

Sea beans wash up in the Hebrides, off Scotland, after crossing the Atlantic, and islanders prized the pale gray nickernut above the rest, partly because it is so hard to spot on sand. One from Barra, mounted in silver and given to a collector in 1869, was a childbirth charm: the midwife put it in the mother's hand while she prayed. Another, from Colonsay, went to a museum in 1898 because its power was thought to be used up.

Fever nut

In India the seeds are a traditional fever medicine, roasted and powdered, and the bitter compound in them has been called poor man's quinine. In the Caribbean the roasted seeds were brewed as a medicinal tea. Old sources also call the raw seeds poisonous, so this is history, not a recipe.

Where it comes from

Every tropical shore

It is native here, from the Keys up both coasts to Volusia and Levy counties, Manatee included, and it grows on nearly every tropical shore in the world. The pods open partway as they dry, and floods and storm surges carry the seeds out to sea.

Seeds that wait

A seed that had drifted for at least a year to a sub-Antarctic island was nicked open and grew. Sea travel also locks them shut: in one trial, 23 of 25 fresh seeds sprouted within 16 months, against 1 of 25 weathered ones. They turn up on beaches in Britain, Ireland and the Netherlands, and one was found in a seabird's nest on St Kilda in 1883.

A name with pirates

Guilandina honors Melchior Wieland, a sixteenth-century Prussian naturalist who was captured by pirates on his travels and ransomed by an Italian anatomist. Older books call the plant Caesalpinia bonduc; DNA work in 2016 confirmed Guilandina as a genus of its own.

What it does here

Food for the Miami blue

The Miami blue lays its eggs on the flower buds and tender new shoots, and ants tend the caterpillars. At Bahia Honda in the Keys, gray nickernut was the butterfly's only caterpillar food. Hurricane Georges churned the dunes there in 1998, nickernut sprawled across them, and the butterfly, thought lost, was rediscovered on it the next year. It was gone from Bahia Honda by 2010, and the last wild adults were seen in 2022, in the Marquesas Keys. It once ranged north to Tampa Bay but hasn't been seen on the mainland since the 1980s.

First back after a storm

Nickernut is one of the first plants to leaf out after a hurricane or a fire, and on restoration sites it is aggressive enough to need cutting back. The stems root where they touch the ground and can add a yard or more a year.

How to know it

Go by the tangle

Look for a sprawling mound, waist- to head-high where nothing holds it up, with long stems running out twenty feet or more and climbing into anything nearby.

Prickles everywhere

Hooked prickles cover the stems and twigs and even run along the leaf stalks, with a pair where each side branch of the leaf begins. They catch on a sleeve before you see them.

Leaves divided twice

Each leaf is split into side branches, and each branch into pairs of oval leaflets. At the base of the leaf sits a small pair of leafy stipules. Florida's rarer yellow nicker, with yellow to brown seeds, lacks them.

How it grows

Flowers by spike

The small yellow flowers are fragrant and come on upright spikes for much of the year, most in spring and summer. Each spike is all male or all female, and the female flowers even carry stamens that make no pollen.

Pods

The pods are two to four inches long and covered in stiff bristles. They ripen from green to reddish-brown and split partway open, each holding one to three hard gray seeds, round to egg-shaped and about the size of a marble.

Sun, sand and salt

It wants full sun and sandy, well-drained ground, takes salt spray in stride and grows fast. Given room, it becomes a thicket.

Take care

Prickles and seeds

The prickles on the stems, leaf stalks and pods catch clothing and scratch skin, so look without reaching in. Old sources call the raw seeds poisonous in quantity; keep them from children and pets.
