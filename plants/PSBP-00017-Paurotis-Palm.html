Paurotis Palm

Acoelorrhaphe wrightii

At a glance

  • This fan palm is a Florida native that grows wild only at the southern tip, and the state protects it. Around the Caribbean and Central America it is far more widespread. The clump here was planted.
  • A Paurotis is a clump of slender stems. As the older ones lean outward they sag under their own weight, then turn upward again, and each sag leaves a twist in the stem.
  • A big clump crowds itself with new shoots and dead fronds, so keeping it tidy is real work. Here, a volunteer thins the shoots and old growth near the ground, then cuts dead fronds high overhead with a pole pruner. A mature clump can take a day or two.

Cultural significance

Named from Cuba

The species name honors Charles Wright, a Connecticut plant collector who made three long trips to Cuba. He gathered the plant that the palm was first described from in March 1862, at a place called Hanábana in Matanzas Province, and in 1866 botanists named it for him as a Copernicia palm. In 1879 it was moved into a genus of its own.

Where it comes from

Swamps and wet hammocks

It grows wild in swamps and wet hammocks. The state lists Florida plants only in Collier, Miami-Dade and Monroe counties. Elsewhere its home runs from the Bahamas and Cuba through Mexico and Central America to Colombia.

What it does here

Clumps, compared

The park's European Fan Palm also grows in clumps, so a cluster of stems alone does not name a Paurotis. Look at the fans and the leaf stalks instead.

Dead fronds have residents

In 2010 a fungus new to science was found on dead Paurotis leaves in a Broward County park. Its spores are long and slender, each with a thin beak, and show only under a microscope. That record is from Broward, not from here, but the spent fronds cut from the clump here are the same kind of leaf.

How to know it

A fan on a long stalk

Each leaf is a light-green fan about two feet across, cut into narrow segments, silvery underneath, on a stalk up to three feet long.

Slender, shaggy stems

The stems are only two or three inches thick, often wrapped in coarse brown fibers and old leaf bases, so the base of a clump looks shaggy.

How it grows

Slow and wide

New shoots keep rising from the base, so a clump widens slowly. An old one can stand about 30 feet tall and 20 feet across.

Flowers and fruit

Creamy-white flower sprays rise past the leaves in spring. The fruit is about a quarter inch across and ripens from green through orange to black.

Short of something, and it shows

A shortage of manganese yellows and streaks the newest leaves and, when bad, withers and curls their tips. A shortage of potassium spots the oldest leaves instead. Both are common in Florida soils, and in limy fill under parking lots and road edges a Paurotis can die of manganese shortage.

Take care

Teeth on the stalks

The edges of the leaf stalks are lined with sharp teeth. Keep hands and faces away from them, especially down in the shaggy base of a clump.
