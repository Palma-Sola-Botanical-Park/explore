Plumbago

Plumbago auriculata

At a glance

  • The big pale-blue bush on the island at the entrance is this. It is a large one for the species, and it flowers nearly all year.
  • The Cassius blue butterfly lays its eggs on it and the caterpillars eat the leaves and flowers — a small blue butterfly raised on a sky-blue shrub.
  • Touch a spent flower and it may stick to your finger. Tiny glandular hairs let the seed capsule hitch a ride on fur and clothing.
  • Florida's native plumbago flowers white. The blue one is the South African.

Cultural significance

Named for lead, for reasons nobody is sure of

The name comes from plumbum, Latin for lead. One old explanation is that the plant was used as a supposed treatment for lead poisoning, which is also where leadwort comes from. Another is simply the lead-blue colour of the flowers. The honest answer is that nobody has settled it.

Where it comes from

South Africa and Mozambique

It grows wild in lowland scrub and thicket down the south-east of Africa, scrambling through whatever it can lean on. It is now planted in warm countries everywhere, for the simple reason that very few shrubs give this much blue for this little effort.

Where to find it here

Welcome Island, by the fountain

On the island the driveway circles as you come in, beside the Tree Crinum. You will not need directions once you are close — in flower it is the blue thing, and there is very little other plumbago in the park to confuse it with.

What it does here

It grew around a palm that is gone

This plumbago used to skirt the base of the park's big Bismarck Palm, a specimen that greeted people as they arrived. The hurricanes took the Bismarck. The plumbago is still there, and still flowering, on the same ground.

Kept in bounds on purpose

Left alone it sprawls and scrambles over whatever is next to it, so it gets cut back regularly. That is not tidiness for its own sake — it is a shrub at the front entrance, and the alternative is a thicket across the drive.

Worth two minutes

Stand still beside it on a warm day and watch for something very small and very blue. The Cassius blue is about the size of a thumbnail, breeds on this shrub, and most people walk straight past it.

How to know it

Blue, in phlox-like clusters

Pale sky-blue flowers, five flat petals each, carried in rounded heads at the ends of the shoots. Almost nothing else here flowers this colour.

The flowers stick

Touch a spent bloom. The base of each flower is covered in tiny glandular hairs and it will cling to your fingers — the same trick the seed capsule uses on fur and clothing to get carried somewhere else.

How it grows

Fast, and it does not mind the shears

Three to six feet in most gardens, more where it can lean on something, and it spreads by rooting where stems touch the ground. It takes drought once settled and resprouts happily after hard pruning, which is the only reason it can be grown at a park entrance at all.

Take care

The sap is rough on skin

Plumbago contains plumbagin, which can cause strong skin irritation and blistering in sensitive people and animals — there is a published veterinary case of a dog blistering after contact with it. Wear gloves if you are pruning, wash sap off promptly, and do not let a dog chew it or nose around in the clippings.
