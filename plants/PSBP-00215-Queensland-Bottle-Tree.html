Queensland Bottle Tree

Brachychiton rupestris

At a glance

  • A grown bottle tree is a water tank. The swollen trunk holds enough moisture in its soft, fibrous wood to carry the tree through months of drought, and people in dry country have bored into it for exactly that reason.
  • Its name, rupestris, means growing among rocks. It comes from the stony ridges of central Queensland and grows wild nowhere else on earth.
  • Ours has not got there yet. Right now it is a slender pole about fifteen feet tall with a trunk a few inches across.
  • The seeds are real food, roughly a fifth protein and a quarter fat. Getting at them is the hard part.

Cultural significance

The tree left standing

In Queensland, when a paddock is cleared, the bottle trees are often left where they are. They carry long-standing cultural meaning, they throw shade in open country, and in a hard season the leaves and soft inner wood will feed stock. A cleared field with a few fat trunks still standing in it is an ordinary sight there.

Water, food, and a bad cup of coffee

First Nations Australians drew drinking water from the trunk in dry seasons and ate the seeds raw or roasted, once the hairs were cleaned off. European settlers later roasted and ground the same seeds as a coffee substitute, which tells you more about how badly they wanted coffee than about the seeds.

Where it comes from

Central Queensland, and nowhere else

It is native to a band of central Queensland and northern New South Wales, on rocky ridges and stony plains in dry open woodland. It gives its name to bottletree scrub, a habitat that has been cleared heavily enough to be listed as endangered in Australia.

Where to find it here

On the wall in the cactus and succulent garden

Walk the crushed shell path with the cactus and succulent garden on your right, before you reach the mahogany and the cypress pond. The bottle tree stands just inside the block retaining wall at the garden edge, and once you pick out that thin bare trunk you will not lose it again.

Close neighbours

A Desert Rose and a Coral Plant grow within a few steps of it, on and beside the same wall.

What it does here

Planted for later

This is a tree put in for what it will become. The trunk that gives the species its name is years away, and the tree standing here now gives no hint of it. Most visitors walk past without a second look, which is fair enough — there is not much to see yet.

Among the water savers

It sits in the right company. Everything around it in that garden is built to hold water through a dry spell, and the bottle tree does the same thing on a larger scale and a slower clock.

How to know it

A pole, for now

Look for a straight, smooth trunk, grey-green and netted with fine pale markings, only a few inches through, with the faintest widening where it meets the ground. That slight flare at the base is the beginning of the bottle.

Leaves like a willow

The crown is loose and thin, carried high on the trunk, and the leaves are long and narrow. From a distance the whole top reads as a wispy tuft rather than a canopy.

Four trunks, one idea

The park grows four plants that handle drought the same way, by keeping the water in the trunk: this one, the Baobab over at the Serenity Garden, the Madagascar Palm, and the Desert Rose a few steps from here. The bottle tree and the Baobab are relatives and come by the shape honestly. The other two are no relation to either and worked it out on their own.

How it grows

Slow to show

The swelling usually does not begin until a tree is five to ten years old, and the full exaggerated shape takes decades. Nothing about this species rewards impatience.

Drops its leaves when it feels like it

In Australia it sheds in the dry season. Here the timing is looser, and it may thin out during a dry spell or a cool stretch and refill later. A bare bottle tree in winter is not a dead one.

Take care

The pods are the problem

The seeds sit in woody pods packed with fine, stiff, star-shaped hairs that itch badly on skin and are genuinely nasty in the eyes. If you find an open pod on the ground, leave it there. The leaves, bark and flowers are harmless to people, dogs and cats.
