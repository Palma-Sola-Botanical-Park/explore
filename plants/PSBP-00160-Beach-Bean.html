Beach Bean

Canavalia rosea
On this page

At a glance

  • Its seeds float. A seed can ride the sea for months with its hard coat sealed, which is how the plant reached beaches all around the warm world.
  • Each leaf is three leaflets, and they fold up in the heat of the day.
  • Pink-purple pea flowers and thick woody pods can be on it in any month, because it blooms and fruits all year.
  • A single vine can sprawl forty feet wide. It is a good plant where it has room, and hard to keep in bounds in a tidy bed.

Cultural significance

Food elsewhere, poison here

In some places the young pods and seeds are eaten cooked, but they turn poisonous as they mature on the plant.

Where it comes from

Beaches, everywhere warm

It is native to Florida's coasts, wild from Volusia and Dixie counties south into the Keys, and it grows on tropical beaches around the world. Older books call it Canavalia maritima. The Swedish botanist Swartz first described it from Jamaica in 1788, as a different kind of bean, and it moved to its present genus in 1825.

Where to find it here

Around all three ponds in the park, installed as part of pond preservation grants from the Tampa Bay and Sarasota Bay Estuary Programs.

What it does

Holding sand

On a beach it runs across open sand in long sprawling runners, which helps hold a dune in place. Like other beans it carries nitrogen-fixing bacteria in root nodules, a help in sand with almost no nutrients.

How to know it

Round leaflets, in threes

Each leaf is three rounded, leathery leaflets.

Pink pea flowers

The flowers are pink to rose-purple, shaped like pea flowers, in clusters. The pods that follow are thick and woody.

How it grows

Salt, sun, and sand

It takes full sun, pure sand, salt spray and drought. A vine can spread forty feet across, and the Florida Native Plant Society warns that it can be aggressive in a garden, so it wants room.
