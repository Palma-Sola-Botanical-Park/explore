Ribbon Fan Palm

Livistona decora

At a glance

  • The ribbons are parts of a single fan-shaped leaf, split deeply into narrow, hanging strips.
  • This Australian palm grows outside our fence beside California Fan Palm; visitors are welcome to enjoy the roadside planting.
  • Its leaf stalks carry hooked teeth—a sharp contrast to the loose, flowing crown.

Cultural significance

You may meet this palm under the older name Livistona decipiens. Decipiens means “deceiving,” a name thought to recall a mix-up when it was cultivated in France as another species. The two nursery names refer to the same ribbon palm.

Where it comes from

Its home is coastal Queensland in eastern Australia, where it grows along forest margins and in coastal swamps. It has since become a landscape palm in warm regions well beyond Australia.

Where to find it here

Look outside the fence, on the strip beside the road, next to California Fan Palm. You are welcome to walk out and enjoy the whole line of plants along the fence.

What it does here

The two neighboring palms make a useful comparison. Stand back far enough to see their crowns: on Ribbon Fan Palm, the long, loose leaf tips are as much a part of its outline as the upright fans. A little breeze makes the difference easier to see.

How to know it

Follow the fan

Follow one leaf from its stalk to its tips. The broad fan folds around a central rib, then breaks into strips that hang below it. Those strips belong to the leaf itself; they are not the fine, loose threads seen on some other fan palms.

Our palm still wears a rough covering of old leaf bases. As a trunk lengthens and those bases fall, the exposed surface shows the marks left by earlier leaves.

How it grows

It grows upward on one trunk rather than spreading into a clump. In a Florida landscape it can reach roughly thirty feet, lifting that curtain of foliage well above head height. Established plants tolerate dry spells, though regular moisture supports fuller growth.

Take care

Keep hands clear of the edges of the leaf stalks. The dark, hooked teeth are easy to overlook among the hanging leaves.
