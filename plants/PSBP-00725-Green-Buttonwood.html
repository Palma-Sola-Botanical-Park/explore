At a glance

  • The Welcome Island tree replaced a Bismarck Palm lost in recent storms.
  • Green and silver buttonwoods are the same species; fine leaf hairs account for the silver appearance.
  • The little brown “buttons” are clusters of fruits packed together.
  • Its dense wood is prized for smoking meat and fish.

Cultural significance

Buttonwood’s dense wood has long been used for fuel and charcoal. Old trees also lend themselves to bonsai: small leaves and weathered, twisting trunks keep the character of a coastal tree at a much smaller scale.

Where to find it here

Look on Welcome Island, just inside the gates as you drive in.

There is a second buttonwood in the Natives area.

Where it comes from

A Florida native, buttonwood also grows around the tropical American coasts. Along mangrove shorelines it usually occupies the higher, landward edge, where salt reaches the soil but roots are not continually underwater.

Not a true mangrove

Florida's three true mangroves are the red, black and white. Buttonwood grows alongside them and is sometimes called the fourth, but it is not a true mangrove: red mangrove seedlings sprout while still hanging on the parent tree, and buttonwood seeds fall first and sprout later. It does belong to the same family as the white mangrove.

What it does

We planted this buttonwood where a Bismarck Palm came down during the storms of recent years. It brings a different shape to the island: a branching tree with a spreading canopy in place of the palm’s single trunk and enormous fans.

How to know it

Green or silver

Look at the texture as well as the color. Smooth green leaves and leaves covered in fine silver hairs can belong to the same species, with intermediate forms between them.

Small flowers gather in rounded heads. After flowering, the heads develop into firm, brownish fruit clusters about half an inch across. Those persistent clusters explain the name buttonwood.

Leaves have smooth edges and alternate along the twigs. Near the base of a leaf blade, a close look can reveal a pair of small glands.

Ants at the glands

Those glands ooze nectar that ants drink. In a field study, buttonwoods kept free of ants lost more of their leaves than buttonwoods with ants.

How it grows

Full sun, salt spray and dry spells are conditions established buttonwoods handle well. Tolerance of coastal exposure does not mean a tree needs to stand in salt water. Older bark becomes rough and furrowed as the branching framework thickens.
