At a glance

  • Its leaflets leave each frond at different angles, which turns the leaves into soft plumes.
  • It grows one trunk, ringed with leaf scars, topped by a crownshaft.
  • It comes from the wet forests of northeastern Madagascar.
  • It can flower while still young. Ours was already in bloom in July.
  • Ours came to us labeled Dypsis plumosa, the name it went by until 2022.

Cultural significance

A name catches up with a palm

For years growers sold this palm as Dypsis ambositrae, because it looked like that palm. Botanists who compared it with plants in Madagascar had their doubts, and in 2009 they described it as a new species, Dypsis plumosa, using flowers and fruit from cultivated palms. The reference specimen came from a garden in Hawaii, and the paper gave it the name we use, Madagascar Queen Palm. In 2022 it moved to the genus Chrysalidocarpus, so today it is Chrysalidocarpus plumosus.

Where to find it here

It stands at the end of the row of palms just south of the Plumeria patch, on the side closest to the Big Pond. The silver form of Saw Palmetto is not far away.

Where it comes from

It is native to the wet tropics of northeastern Madagascar. Not much is known about it in the wild: its conservation status is listed as data deficient, which means nobody has gathered enough information to judge.

How to know it

Plumes, not flat fronds

Look along a frond. The narrow leaflets come off the central stalk at different angles, and their tips droop even where the leaflet itself points outward. That jumble is what makes each frond look like a plume.

Not its look-alike

The palm it was once sold as, Dypsis ambositrae, holds its leaflets flat in one plane and carries small flaps at the tips of its leaf sheaths. Tidy, flat fronds point away from this palm.

Trunk and crownshaft

There is a single trunk, ringed with leaf scars, green when young and turning brown with age. Fine whitish to reddish-brown hairs can coat the crownshaft above it.

How it grows

Flowers early

It can flower and fruit while still fairly small, and ours did: in July its branched flower spikes hung below the leaves.

Hawaii or California?

Planted palms grew faster in tropical Hawaii than in cooler southern California. Bradenton sits somewhere in between, so we will find out how it does here.
