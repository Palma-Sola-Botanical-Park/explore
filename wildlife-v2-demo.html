Brown Anole

How to know it

Easily confused with

Green Anole — the green anole turns bright green, flashes a pink throat fan, and climbs high. The brown anole never turns green, flashes reddish-orange, and stays low.

Color

Brown, tan, or gray, usually patterned with triangles, diamonds, or a pale stripe down the back. The one thing it will never do is turn bright green.

Throat fan

Males flash a reddish-orange dewlap edged in yellow. The native green anole's is pink — that single detail settles most identifications.

Body

Stocky, short-snouted, with ridged scales that give the back a slightly rough look. Five to eight inches long, tail included; males run larger.

Where it sits

Low. Fence rails, walls, sidewalk edges, the base of a trunk — often head-down, bobbing. A green anole in the same garden will usually be well above it.

Where to find it here

Almost anywhere the sun hits. Fence rails, tree trunks, the warm edge of a sidewalk, the border of a planting bed — scan low surfaces on a warm day and you will find one within a minute or two.

They are here every month of the year, though a cold snap sends them out of sight for a few days. Warm mornings are best; that is when they are out basking and displaying.

In this park

The brown anole is the most-recorded animal in the park. Visitors have been logging it here since 2018, and it turns up more often than any other creature we track. If you are looking for a lizard, this is the one you will find.

How it lives

Active by day and almost always close to the ground. Males hold territory and advertise it constantly: a bobbing push-up display and a flash of the throat fan, repeated at anything that comes near. It is body language for this spot is taken, and it works on rivals and visitors alike.

They hunt whatever they can catch and swallow — beetles, ants, flies, spiders. Grabbed by a predator, an anole can drop its tail and leave it twitching as a decoy, then regrow a shorter one over the following weeks.

What it does here

Brown anoles reached the Florida Keys around 1887, most likely as stowaways in cargo and on nursery plants. They have since spread through nearly the whole state and are now among Florida's most abundant land vertebrates.

That arrival reshaped life for the green anole, Florida's only native anole. The two want the same things — the same insects, the same sunny perches — and where browns move in, greens move up, shifting into the shrubs and higher trunks. Brown anoles also eat green anole eggs and hatchlings, which adds to the pressure.

Evolution you can watch

Here is the part worth knowing. Green anoles pushed onto higher, smoother perches have evolved larger, stickier toepads — measurably, in only a few generations. It is one of the clearest real-world examples of rapid evolution anywhere, and it is happening in ordinary Florida gardens.

None of this is the brown anole doing anything wrong; it is simply very good at being a lizard in Florida. It is also food for a great many things — birds, snakes, larger lizards, cats — so its abundance feeds the park as much as it crowds it.
