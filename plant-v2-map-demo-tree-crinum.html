Tree Crinum

Crinum asiaticum

At a glance

  • A giant lily from tropical Asia that grows from a bulb the size of a football and makes a fountain of leaves four or five feet across.
  • The white spider flowers open in the evening and smell strongest at dusk, the mark of a flower built for hawkmoths, though butterflies work them by day as well.
  • The bulb keeps making new bulbs beside itself, and the big seeds sprout where they fall, so one plant becomes a colony.
  • Every part is poisonous if eaten, the bulb most of all. One of its old names is poison bulb.

Cultural significance

Cemetery lily

Across its home range the fleshy leaves have long been warmed and laid on sprains and swellings, a folk remedy that never involves eating the plant. In the American South, crinums are the lilies of old cemeteries: planted at a grave and left alone, they are often the last thing still growing there, which is how this one came by the name cemetery lily.

Where it comes from

Shores of tropical Asia

Tree Crinum grows wild on shores from India and Sri Lanka through Southeast Asia to northern Australia and the islands of the Pacific, often just above the beach. It has been planted through the tropics for centuries and now grows on its own in a few coastal spots in Florida. Crinum is from the Greek for lily.

Where to find it here

The gazebo by the pavilion, and the Welcome Island

Two places: a colony at the gazebo by the pavilion, and a clump on the Welcome Island, the island the driveway circles in front of the Galleria, near the fountain.

Five plantings: one on the Front Island near Fountain, three at the Pavilion Gazebo and one at the Pavilion. Tap a dot.
Dots are placed to about twenty feet. North is up.
When

The leaves are there all year. Flower stalks come up in most of the warm months, not on any schedule, and the lumpy green fruit follow.

What it does here

Volunteers

This is a plant that looks after itself. Around the gazebo by the pavilion the fallen seed sprouts in the mulch, and the colony is spreading on its own toward the Pavilion. Those newcomers are volunteers, not plantings, and the park could use volunteers of the other kind to keep them in check.

A flower for moths

The long white tube, the pale colour and the evening scent are the signs of a flower made for hawkmoths, which hover and probe with tongues as long as the tube. Studies of this species have found butterflies carrying its pollen too, so the flower is less particular than it looks. Come at dusk to smell it.

How to know it

A fountain of leaves

Broad, fleshy, strap-shaped leaves rise straight from the base in a big open rosette, arching outward. No trunk; the leaf bases wrap each other into a thick green neck at the ground.

Flowers above the leaves

A stout leafless stalk carries a cluster of white flowers, each a long tube opening into six narrow petals that curl back, with long stamens tipped in purple-red. They are strongly fragrant.

Lumpy green fruit

After the flowers come knobbly green fruit, each holding a few seeds as big as walnuts, soft and green rather than hard and dry. They fall and sprout where they land.

How it grows

A bulb that makes bulbs

The bulb underground stores food and buds off new bulbs beside itself, so the clump widens year by year. Gardeners pull the offsets to make new plants, and the seeds do the rest.

Hard to kill

It takes Florida heat, sun or part shade, sand or heavier soil, and a spell of flooding, and it comes back from a freeze. A crinum planted at a grave a century ago is often still there, which is why the South calls them cemetery lilies.

Take care

Not for eating

No part of this plant is food. The leaves, flowers, seeds and above all the bulb contain lycorine and related alkaloids, which cause vomiting, cramps and diarrhea. Anyone who has eaten some should get medical care.

Dogs

The same alkaloids poison dogs, which sometimes chew the leaves or dig at the bulb. A dog that has eaten any of it needs a vet.
