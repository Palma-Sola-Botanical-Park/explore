Royal Poinciana

At a glance

  • When a Royal Poinciana blooms, it earns its other name — Flamboyant Tree. The canopy can be entirely covered in vermillion flowers, visible from blocks away.
  • Native to Madagascar, where it's now considered vulnerable in the wild despite being planted across every tropical city on Earth.
  • Look closely at the flowers—the top fifth petal is splashed with yellow and white to serve as a literal visual landing strip for pollinators.
  • In Caribbean communities, the blooming of the Poinciana is a cultural calendar event marking the start of summer.

How to know it

Briefly deciduous

drops leaves in late winter or dry season before dramatic bloom, then leafs out fully after peak flowering.

Flowers

Brilliant scarlet-orange, 3 to 4 inches wide, in large terminal clusters. Five petals — four spreading and one upright 'standard' petal streaked with white and yellow. Peak bloom May through July. Bloom is often triggered by a distinct dry or cool season.

Seed pods

Flattened, dark brown, 1 to 2 feet long, persisting through winter and rattling audibly in the wind when dry. Seeds inside are hard and bean-like.

Leaves

Feathery, bipinnate — up to 20 pairs of primary leaflets, each carrying 25 to 35 pairs of tiny secondary leaflets. The effect is a lacy, fern-like canopy that creates dappled shade.

In bloom (May through July), the canopy can be entirely hidden under flowers — visible and recognizable from blocks away. After bloom, watch for the long rattling seed pods. Run a finger along the underside of a compound leaf to feel the fine-textured leaflets that give the canopy its feathery quality.

Where to find it here

In the Front Pond, Nursery, Nursery Parking and Serenity Garden.

What it does here

The royal poinciana's blazing canopy is more than a show — its claw-shaped flowers are shaped to dust visiting birds and insects with pollen, drawing bees, butterflies, and nectar-seeking birds all summer, while the wide ferny crown throws cooling shade and gives birds perches and shelter.

Its real conservation story is survival: royal poincianas are rare and threatened in their native Madagascar, yet so widely planted elsewhere that cultivated trees like this one have become a genuine safety net for the species. In a sense, every healthy specimen helps keep a Madagascan treasure from vanishing. It's a reminder that an ornamental tree can also be an act of conservation.

Where it comes from

Native to Madagascar but widely planted in tropical and semi-tropical areas worldwide. The origin was unknown to science until the 1930s when a natural colony was discovered on the west coast of Madagascar. Common name honors M. de Poinci, 17th century governor of the French West Indies.

In full bloom the canopy can be so densely covered with flowers that the leaves become invisible — the entire tree appears to be on fire, explaining the alternate name Flamboyant.

It is the national flower of St. Kitts and Nevis and the national tree of Puerto Rico. For centuries it had been cultivated throughout the tropics but nobody knew where it originally came from — a tree celebrated worldwide whose homeland was a mystery for 400 years.

Patience is required — trees usually need 5 to 10 years to reach blooming age — but the wait is legendary.

Cultivated worldwide for centuries, its true wild homeland was completely unknown to science until it was finally traced back to Madagascar in the 1820s.

Though fiercely protected in parks globally, this tree is endangered in its native Madagascar. Human cultivation is the only reason it survived extinction.

After the fiery blooms fade, the tree drops massive, two-foot-long woody seed pods that rattle when shaken and are used as traditional Caribbean musical instruments.

How it grows

trees usually require 5 to 10 years from planting to first bloom; large seed pods are persistent litter — plan placement accordingly

Light

Full sun

Soil

Well-drained; tolerates poor, dry, and rocky soils

Drought

High once established

Cold

Size

Habit
