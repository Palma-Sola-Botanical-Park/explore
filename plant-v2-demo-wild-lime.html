Wild Lime

Zanthoxylum fagara
On this page

At a glance

  • The essential host plant for the Giant Swallowtail — Florida's largest butterfly, with a wingspan reaching six inches.
  • Crush a leaf and it smells of citrus. This is a native Florida relative of the orange and the lime.
  • The branches carry sharp hooked spines. Small, thorny, and easy to walk straight past.
  • The caterpillar that lives on it looks like a fresh bird dropping.

How to know it

Crush a leaf

It smells of citrus. That one test separates Wild Lime from almost anything growing beside it.

Spines

Short, sharp and hooked backward along the branches — the reason it is sometimes called catclaw.

Leaves

Compound, with small rounded leaflets set along a flattened, winged stalk. Those narrow flanges between the leaflets are a Zanthoxylum signature.

Look for a dense, dark, thorny evergreen shrub at the edge of a planting, five to twenty feet tall.

How it grows

It flowers nearly year-round, peaking in winter and spring. The flowers are tiny and greenish-yellow — individually easy to miss, produced in quantity, and worked by small bees and beetles rather than anything showy.

The fruit

A small reddish-brown capsule ripening to black, holding one shiny black seed. It is aromatic, and it has been used as a spice in its native range — but it numbs the tongue.

Where it comes from

Wild Lime is native here. It grows in hammocks through central and south Florida, and on south into the Caribbean, Mexico and South America.

A native citrus

It belongs to Rutaceae, the citrus family — a wild Florida cousin of the orange and the lime. The genus name Zanthoxylum comes from the Greek for yellow wood.

What it does here

A nursery for Giant Swallowtails

Giant Swallowtails lay their eggs on wild lime and other citrus-family plants. The caterpillar that hatches here spends its entire larval life eating these leaves — so the big swallowtail crossing the path and this thorny shrub are the same story. See the butterfly

Look for the caterpillar

It resembles a glossy bird dropping, which is exactly the point. Disturb one and it rears up and everts a forked orange organ from behind its head, releasing a smell aimed at small predators. It is harmless to you.

The endangered Schaus' swallowtail uses the same plant further south in the Keys. And the thorns do a second job: dense, spiny, evergreen growth makes good nesting cover for small birds, which also eat the fruit.

Take care

The spines

The branches carry sharp, backward-hooked spines that catch on the way out. Do not reach through the foliage.
