Royal Poinciana

Delonix regia

At a glance

  • In bloom the canopy can vanish under vermillion flowers — recognisable from blocks away.
  • Four petals spread wide; the fifth stands upright, streaked in yellow and white.
  • Native to Madagascar, where it is threatened in the wild, while being planted across every tropical city on earth.
  • After the flowers come woody pods up to two feet long that rattle when they dry.

How to know it

In bloom

Scarlet-orange flowers three to four inches across, in clusters dense enough to hide the leaves. May through July.

The fifth petal

Four petals spread; the fifth stands upright and carries yellow and white streaks — a landing mark aimed at whatever comes to the flower.

Leaves

Feathery and twice-divided, each leaf carrying hundreds of tiny leaflets. The canopy reads as lace rather than foliage.

Out of bloom, go by the silhouette: far wider than it is tall, a flat-topped umbrella of a tree.

How it grows

It sheds to flower

Briefly deciduous — it drops its leaves in late winter or the dry season, flowers on bare branches, then leafs out fully once the peak is past. A distinct dry or cool spell often sets it off.

The pods

Flattened, dark brown, one to two feet long, hanging on through winter. As they dry the seeds work loose and rattle in the wind. In the Caribbean they have been used as instruments.

It grows fast — thirty feet in ten years is ordinary — but it is slow to flower. Five to ten years from planting before the first bloom.

Where it comes from

A tree with no known home

For centuries the Royal Poinciana was planted across the tropics while nobody could say where it came from. Its wild population was eventually traced to western Madagascar.

Threatened where it belongs

It is vulnerable in the wild there and abundant everywhere else. Cultivation is much of the reason the species is secure at all.

The name honours M. de Poinci, a seventeenth-century governor of the French West Indies. It is the national flower of St Kitts and Nevis and the national tree of Puerto Rico, and in Caribbean communities its bloom marks the start of summer.

What it does here

A cultivated safety net

A tree this common is easy to read as ordinary. It is the opposite: rare at home, secure only because so many were planted elsewhere. A healthy specimen in a botanical park is part of why the species still exists.

The claw-shaped flowers dust visiting bees, butterflies and nectar-feeding birds with pollen right through the summer, and the wide ferny crown throws deep, dappled shade with room for birds to sit in it.
